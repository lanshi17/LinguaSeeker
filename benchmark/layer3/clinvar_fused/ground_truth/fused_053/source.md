# Expanding research and care for Leigh syndrome: efforts of a patient-led advocacy organization

## Abstract

Background Leigh syndrome is a rare, progressive neurometabolic disorder caused by pathogenic variants in over 110 mitochondrial or nuclear genes. Its clinical and genetic heterogeneity creates challenges for diagnosis, care, and research. Cure Mito Foundation, a parent-led nonprofit established in 2018 to develop a gene therapy for SURF1-related Leigh syndrome, has since evolved into a global organization supporting individuals and families worldwide affected by all forms of Leigh syndrome. Methods This article describes the multifaceted efforts of Cure Mito Foundation to accelerate research and support for Leigh syndrome through family-led engagement and collaborative scientific partnerships. Strategies include funding the development of diverse disease models, gene therapies, drug repurposing pipelines, and a global patient registry. Emphasis is placed on co-production with affected families, sharing of biospecimens and data, and alignment with regulatory and research standards. Results The Leigh Syndrome Global Patient Registry comprises over 400 participants from 48 countries, with data made available to qualified researchers, and results shared regularly with the patient community to promote transparency and trust. Notable research accomplishments of Cure Mito include facilitating the development of multiple gene therapy candidates, patient-derived organoids and animal models, and repurposed drugs now entering early-phase trials. Cure Mito also played a key role in the launch of the Mitochondrial and Inherited Metabolic Disease Taskforce, led by the Critical Path Institute (C-Path), to integrate registry and clinical data into the Rare Disease Cures Accelerator platform. Additional efforts include community-developed educational tools, international awareness campaigns, and support programs tailored to the unique needs of Leigh syndrome families. Conclusions Through relentless effort and dedication, the Cure Mito Foundation has shown that a small group of determined individuals can drive extraordinary change. By building a global patient registry, advancing data sharing and research, developing patient-centric education and support, and facilitating collaboration among scientists, clinicians, and families, the Foundation has created momentum toward effective treatments. With support from the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative’s Rare As One grant, Cure Mito is poised to expand its impact even further. ## Leigh syndrome and the evolution of cure Mito foundation

Leigh syndrome is a severe neurometabolic disorder that arises from pathogenic variants in more than 110 genes encoded from mitochondrial or nuclear DNA [ 1 , 2 ]. Disease onset is most commonly associated with developmental delay or regression, with symptoms usually appearing between the ages of 3 months and 2 years, leading to early death [ 3 ]. Features of Leigh syndrome include a loss of achieved developmental milestones, loss of motor abilities, weakness, changes in cognition and behavior, as well as gastrointestinal, respiratory, and cardiac issues, among other signs [ 4 ]. Although a rare disorder, Leigh syndrome is the most commonly diagnosed primary mitochondrial disease, estimated to affect 1 in 40,000 individuals [ 5 ], although in some populations it may be as frequent as 1 in approximately 2,000 people [ 6 ].

The pathology of Leigh syndrome is complex, affecting multiple organ systems and presenting differently in each affected individual [ 7 ]. Leigh syndrome thus poses unique challenges for research, care, and education, given its rarity and variable etiology. The clinical heterogeneity of Leigh syndrome also poses a challenge to therapeutic development, as clinical trials tend to be small and may yield variable outcomes depending on the underlying genetic etiology.

Cure Mito Foundation began with one family’s efforts to fight for the health of their son, who was diagnosed with a deficiency of the surfeit locus protein 1 ( SURF1 ) gene, the most common nuclear gene causing Leigh syndrome [ 8 – 10 ]. Faced with no treatment for this rare diagnosis, the family sought answers in science, reaching out to global researchers specializing in Leigh syndrome. In 2018, the family started a nonprofit called the Cure SURF1 Foundation, with an initial mission focused on funding the development of a gene replacement therapy for their son [ 11 ]. While the original gene therapy program, which is still in development, faced scientific and commercial setbacks, it laid the groundwork for Cure Mito Foundation’s broader vision [ 12 – 14 ].

Recognizing that families facing other genetic causes of Leigh syndrome shared the same urgent need for treatments, the organization broadened its scope. In 2021, Cure SURF1 Foundation became Cure Mito Foundation, dedicated to supporting the entire mitochondrial disease community, with a focus on Leigh syndrome. Cure Mito Foundation’s mission is to unite the global community to accelerate patient-centered research, treatments, and cures [ 15 ].

In 2024, Cure Mito Foundation was selected to join the Rare As One Network as recipients of the highly competitive Chan Zuckerberg Initiative (CZI) Grant [ 16 ]. This 5-year, $800,000 capacity-building grant is designed to provide support through training, mentoring, research advancement, and network development. Cure Mito Foundation supports a diverse array of approaches to Leigh syndrome research, with an emphasis on developing or repurposing therapeutics that have the potential to improve the lives of children currently living with the disease.

The organization prioritizes funding researchers who are interested in partnerships and who actively collaborate with the organization toward shared goals. This approach reflects a broader shift in advocacy in recent years - from a more passive role to one of active leadership - where families and individuals affected by both rare and common diseases are increasingly stepping up as partners in driving progress forward [ 17 – 20 ].

Given the organization’s history, research on the SURF1 gene has been a dominant focus; however, the scope has recently expanded to other genes, such as mitochondrially encoded ATP synthase membrane subunit 6 ( MT-ATP6 ) and short-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase 1 ( ECHS1 ). Families who fundraise through Cure Mito Foundation have the opportunity to engage in research aimed at understanding and developing novel therapies for the genetic causes of Leigh syndrome affecting their loved ones [ 21 ]. The hope is that efforts and advancement of research on SURF1 -Leigh syndrome can serve as a roadmap for additional genes that cause Leigh syndrome. Additionally, the extensive network established by Cure Mito Foundation can serve to unite families and scientists toward the efficient development of genetic therapies for rare diseases.

Cure Mito Foundation believes that individuals affected by mitochondrial disease and their families are people first - not just patients - and is dedicated to creating a space where everyone feels seen, supported, and valued. What began as a single family’s search for answers has expanded into a worldwide network encompassing research, patient support, and advocacy (Fig. 1 ). Cure Mito Foundation approaches its mission with a deep and personal sense of urgency and a conviction that information and research knowledge should be accessible to all.

## Cure Mito foundation’s research initiatives

The study of Leigh syndrome poses a unique set of challenges. Pathogenic variants in more than one hundred causative genes have been identified, and these can occur in both nuclear and mitochondrial genes. Leigh syndrome is multisystemic in nature, and patients exhibit a high level of heterogeneity [ 22 ]. Animal models can be useful for studying specific mutations or phenotypes, but they have limitations. For example, the SURF1 knockout mouse displays a biochemical but not a behavioral phenotype [ 23 , 24 ], while the NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunit S4 ( NDUFS4 )-deficient mouse recapitulates some of the clinical and neuroimaging findings found in people with Leigh syndrome [ 25 ]. However, these models represent only two of over 100 genetic causes, among which variations in disease mechanisms may exist. Furthermore, Leigh syndrome may arise from defects in mitochondrial DNA, where heteroplasmy and localization within the mitochondria impact disease pathobiology and putative therapeutic strategies [ 26 , 27 ]. These points underscore the importance of diversifying Leigh syndrome animal models and studying human data and biospecimens.

### Gene therapy

Gene therapy refers to the delivery of nucleic acids to treat diseases resulting from genetic variations, such as SURF1 Leigh syndrome [ 44 ]. Progress in developing gene therapy for SURF1 deficiency has been driven in large part by Cure Mito Foundation’s fundraising and research initiatives. These efforts were propelled by grassroots community support [ 11 ] and strategic collaboration with research scientists at the University of Texas (UT) Southwestern Medical Center, experts in genetic therapies for rare neurological disorders [ 45 ]. This partnership, coupled with rapid fundraising exceeding $1 million, facilitated the development of an adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9)-based gene therapy, an approach supported by preclinical data in a Surf1 knockout mouse model [ 24 , 28 ] and subsequently licensed by Taysha Gene Therapies, earning Orphan Drug Designation (ODD) from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2020 [ 46 , 47 ].

Furthermore, in late 2020, the Cure Mito Foundation’s leadership mobilized the global SURF1 community to collect nearly 40 skin biopsies from patients across nine countries within a matter of weeks, securing essential patient-derived fibroblasts required for the FDA Investigational New Drug (IND) application [ 48 , 49 ].

Despite these advances, the gene therapy program was subsequently paused due to financial constraints, and licensing rights to the therapeutic approach granted to UT Southwestern Medical Center [ 12 , 14 ]. There is an outstanding opportunity to rekindle this gene therapy program as a platform technology not only for pathogenic SURF1 variations but potentially other genetic causes of Leigh syndrome. However, the high cost of gene therapy development, along with technical complexity and delivery challenges, continue to pose barriers to clinical translation.

In response, Cure Mito Foundation remained committed to its mission and strategically redirected its efforts. Building on the scientific, regulatory, and legal insights gained during the initial program, the Foundation launched a renewed therapeutic initiative. This effort led to the identification of a new therapeutic candidate, now advancing toward clinical trials [ 50 ]. Additionally, a Surf1 -deficient rat model created using a newly developed construct is currently undergoing characterization to support ongoing preclinical studies and inform the design of future therapeutics.

### Mitochondrial genome editing

A collaborative initiative between two European genome editing centers (Heinrich Heine University, Germany, and the University of Cambridge, United Kingdom) was facilitated by Cure Mito Foundation in partnership with additional funding organizations. This alliance has led to foundational advancement in the field of mitochondrial gene therapy, exemplifying the impact of sponsoring cross-institutional collaborative efforts.

To model maternally inherited Leigh syndrome (MILS), the research teams developed both cellular and animal systems that replicate the pathological effects of variations in the mitochondrial gene MT-ATP6 . Utilizing DddA-derived cytosine base editors (DdCBEs), the team successfully introduced the pathogenic m.8069G >A point mutation into the mitochondrial genome, resulting in a truncated, non-functional ATP6 protein. This genome editing strategy enabled the generation of isogenic cell lines with varying levels of heteroplasmy, facilitating investigation into dose-dependent effects. To translate these findings in vivo, DdCBEs were microinjected into one-cell mouse embryos, producing the first generation of mice harboring the mutant mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Subsequent generations were bred by selecting mothers with the highest heteroplasmy levels, establishing a robust mouse model for MILS [ 34 ].

The cellular models also revealed that heteroplasmy levels exceeding 25% led to impaired structural integrity of the ATP synthase complex, accompanied by secondary defects in electron transport chain complexes I and III. These defects are believed to result from disrupted mitochondrial cristae morphology, a hypothesis supported by transmission electron microscopy, which revealed pronounced structural abnormalities in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Complementary analyses using confocal microscopy confirmed fragmentation of the mitochondrial network. Similar defects have been identified in MT-ATP6 mutant mice, particularly in skeletal muscle and cardiac tissue, where ATP synthase assembly is disrupted at heteroplasmy levels exceeding 45%. Ongoing studies are quantifying ATP production and employing proteomics to examine steady-state levels of mitochondrial proteins affected by the MT-ATP6 mutation.

Alongside model development and disease characterization, the team is advancing a therapeutic strategy utilizing mitochondrially targeted transcription activator-like effector nucleases (mitoTALENs). These engineered nucleases are designed to selectively cleave mutant mtDNA, thereby favoring a shift in heteroplasmy toward wild-type mtDNA. Currently being tested in cellular models, these tools are expected to restore the molecular phenotype. Further efforts are concentrated on evaluating this approach in vivo in MT-ATP6 mutant mice, employing a range of viral and non-viral delivery methods. These studies aim to provide a compelling proof of concept for heteroplasmy correction, offering a promising therapeutic avenue for MILS caused by pathogenic variants of MT-ATP6 and other maternally inherited types of Leigh syndrome.

Utilizing DddA-derived cytosine base editors (DdCBEs), the team successfully introduced the pathogenic m.8069G >A point mutation into the mitochondrial genome, resulting in a truncated, non-functional ATP6 protein. This genome editing strategy enabled the generation of isogenic cell lines with varying levels of heteroplasmy, facilitating investigation into dose-dependent effects. To translate these findings in vivo, DdCBEs were microinjected into one-cell mouse embryos, producing the first generation of mice harboring the mutant mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Subsequent generations were bred by selecting mothers with the highest heteroplasmy levels, establishing a robust mouse model for MILS [ 34 ].

The cellular models also revealed that heteroplasmy levels exceeding 25% led to impaired structural integrity of the ATP synthase complex, accompanied by secondary defects in electron transport chain complexes I and III. These defects are believed to result from disrupted mitochondrial cristae morphology, a hypothesis supported by transmission electron microscopy, which revealed pronounced structural abnormalities in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Complementary analyses using confocal microscopy confirmed fragmentation of the mitochondrial network. Similar defects have been identified in MT-ATP6 mutant mice, particularly in skeletal muscle and cardiac tissue, where ATP synthase assembly is disrupted at heteroplasmy levels exceeding 45%. Ongoing studies are quantifying ATP production and employing proteomics to examine steady-state levels of mitochondrial proteins affected by the MT-ATP6 mutation.

Alongside model development and disease characterization, the team is advancing a therapeutic strategy utilizing mitochondrially targeted transcription activator-like effector nucleases (mitoTALENs). These engineered nucleases are designed to selectively cleave mutant mtDNA, thereby favoring a shift in heteroplasmy toward wild-type mtDNA. Currently being tested in cellular models, these tools are expected to restore the molecular phenotype. Further efforts are concentrated on evaluating this approach in vivo in MT-ATP6 mutant mice, employing a range of viral and non-viral delivery methods. These studies aim to provide a compelling proof of concept for heteroplasmy correction, offering a promising therapeutic avenue for MILS caused by pathogenic variants of MT-ATP6 and other maternally inherited types of Leigh syndrome.


### Drug repurposing

Drug repurposing refers to the process of identifying new therapeutic uses for existing drugs. Repurposed therapies can offer more immediate treatment options for patients while research into new and potentially curative therapies continues [ 51 ]. Cure Mito Foundation approaches this process from multiple angles (Table 2 ), including seeking ODD for existing products that could be applied to Leigh syndrome. National agencies grant ODD to drugs or biological products intended for the treatment, diagnosis, or prevention of rare diseases or conditions [ 52 , 53 ]. This designation is intended to encourage the development of therapies for rare disorders, which typically affect a small patient population and may offer limited financial incentives for pharmaceutical companies.

A collaborative venture facilitated high-throughput drug screens and dose-response studies in SURF1 -deficient yeast to identify candidate drugs for repurposing [ 37 ]. This process identified the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) inhibitor losmapimod, originally developed for the treatment of Facioscapulohumeral Muscular Dystrophy (FSHD), a genetic disorder that weakens muscles [ 54 ]. Losmapimod was granted rare pediatric drug development (RPDD) status for the treatment of Leigh syndrome with the goal of launching two IND studies based on the same protocol, in two patients with pathogenic variants in ECHS1 and SURF1 respectively. Cure Mito Foundation will sponsor these studies, and Perlara will provide clinical trial consulting services towards their execution [ 55 ].

Another collaborative venture of Cure Mito Foundation resulted in the screening of thousands of small-molecule drugs to measure transcriptomic changes that could potentially ameliorate mitochondrial deficits in Leigh syndrome patient induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons [ 38 , 39 , 56 ]. This preclinical work culminated in N-of-1 clinical data with the potential to support an expanded access trial of an FDA-approved drug for SURF1 deficiency and other subtypes of Leigh syndrome. These studies also produced a patient-derived iPSC line and a control line from an unaffected relative, which researchers can request from Coriell.

Cure Mito Foundation also funded the formulation of drug repurposing pipelines based on iPSC-derived neuronal models of Leigh syndrome at Heinrich Heine University in Dusseldorf, Germany. In collaboration with the University of Luxembourg, a machine learning-based in silico drug screening approach was applied to single-cell RNA sequencing (RNAseq) data of brain organoids with SURF1 mutations. In parallel, Perlara performed rescue screens of FDA-approved drugs in SURF1 -deficient yeast models. Both screening pipelines identified azole compounds that were validated in SURF1 -mutant human iPSC-derived neurons as midbrain organoids. The azole compounds talarozole and sertaconazole rescued growth defects, lactate levels, and neuronal branching structure (33). These findings align with and further support prior evidence implicating retinoic acid-PPARγ pathways in neurodevelopmental regulation [ 57 ], thereby offering potential therapeutic avenues for targeted SURF1 -related mitochondrial diseases.

Another candidate identified by the Prigione lab using high-content screening in MT-ATP6 iPSC-derived neural cells involves modulation by phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors (PDE5i) [ 58 ]. In particular, sildenafil rescued disease-associated phenotypes in patient-derived neural cells and brain organoids, normalizing calcium homeostasis and promoting neuronal growth in MT-ATP6 and SURF1 models [ 32 ]. Sildenafil also extended survival in NDUFS4 -deficient mice and SURF1 -deficient piglets [ 32 ]. In a pilot study involving six patients with MT-ATP6 variants, sildenafil improved motor function and enhanced metabolic stability [ 32 ]. Based on these data, the team received ODD from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the use of sildenafil for patients with Leigh syndrome (EU/3/23/2831). A multicenter clinical trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of sildenafil in pediatric patients with pathogenic variants of MT-ATP6 is currently in the planning phase across several European sites. While there is an urgent need to advance therapeutic candidates into human studies for Leigh syndrome, independent validation of these compounds in additional preclinical settings remains essential. Interestingly, independent work recently suggested that the PDE5 inhibitors sildenafil and tadalafil may have benefit for patients affected by different mitochondrial diseases [ 59 ].

Leveraging strategic partnership, Cure Mito Foundation is empowering global patient participation in drug discovery through home RNA collection kits (Unravel Biosciences, rareSHIFT™ project) [ 60 ]. Nasal swabs collected by families are processed through RNAseq and the AI-enabled BioNAV™ platform. The aim is to generate primary patient and matched family control datasets, focusing on common Leigh syndrome genes to identify potential treatments, with the ultimate goal of accelerating clinical trials [ 35 , 36 ]. Researchers can request access to RNAseq data through Cure Mito Foundation.

### Mitochondrial transplantation

Some recent studies have indicated that mitochondria transplantation may provide therapeutic benefits for primary mitochondrial diseases with both nuclear genome and mtDNA etiologies [ 29 – 32 , 61 ]. Cure Mito Foundation has funded studies that are necessary to facilitate the translation of this therapy from mouse models to people using Leigh syndrome patient-derived fibroblasts.

### Gut microbiota

Recent research has also suggested a link between the gut microbiome and symptoms in mitochondrial disease [ 62 – 65 ]. Building on this, Cure Mito Foundation is funding an investigation of the gut microbiome in patients with primary mitochondrial disease and Leigh syndrome, across a range of genetic etiologies associated with varying degrees of ROS production. This work may also unearth associations between mitochondrial haplogroup, microbiome composition, and other co-morbidities. The long-term implications of this research may reveal new clinical associations between gut dysbiosis and mitochondrial function, potentially highlighting novel therapeutic targets to modulate the gut microbiome and mitochondrial disease.

## Leigh syndrome patient registry

In September 2021, Cure Mito Foundation launched the Leigh Syndrome Global Patient Registry to build a strong and connected international community, better understand how Leigh syndrome affects individuals, and facilitate recruitment for clinical trials. The registry was intentionally designed not only to offer hope for the future, but to deliver meaningful insights in the present. From the outset, this registry prioritized sharing data and providing value to patients now.

### Registry findings and progress

Initial results of the Leigh Syndrome Global Patient Registry were reported in September 2023 and included data from 116 respondents [ 69 ]. This highly accessed publication detailed registry data, described the patient registry platform, survey design, data analysis process, and handling of missing data. It also outlined patient recruitment strategies along with planned efforts around recruitment, community engagement, and data standardization.

Recognizing that registry recruitment, data interoperability, and collaborative community efforts are important drivers of scientific progress [ 18 , 70 – 72 ], Cure Mito has hastened to achieve its aims laid out in the 2023 publication. Consequently, registry enrollment has more than doubled, and the number of countries and genes represented has significantly expanded. Data interoperability with Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium (CDISC) and Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) standards have been achieved, a multi-stakeholder taskforce with the Critical Path Institute (C-Path) has been launched [ 73 ], and the Clinical Research IDentifier format (CRID) has been adopted to enable secure cross-dataset linkage [ 74 ]. ## Community resources

As parents of affected children, Cure Mito Foundation’s leadership is deeply attuned to the daily needs of families impacted by Leigh syndrome. This perspective drives a unique, collaborative approach to resource development - one that directly recognizes and addresses longstanding gaps in support. Families reported that information was scattered across multiple platforms, existing content was not presented in a patient or family-friendly format, and information on specific topics, such as family planning, was difficult to locate.

Additionally, the severity of Leigh syndrome makes this disorder unique compared to many other mitochondrial diseases. Children with Leigh syndrome often require intensive, around-the-clock care, and the impact on families is profound and far-reaching. Alongside hope, there is also loss and grief. These challenges mean that general resources designed to support those with mitochondrial disease may be insufficient or not fully applicable to the unique challenges of Leigh syndrome.

In response to these unmet needs, Cure Mito Foundation created multiple new, essential tools that did not exist previously (Table 5 ).

These challenges mean that general resources designed to support those with mitochondrial disease may be insufficient or not fully applicable to the unique challenges of Leigh syndrome.

In response to these unmet needs, Cure Mito Foundation created multiple new, essential tools that did not exist previously (Table 5 ).

Cure Mito’s programs are intentionally global in scope. The leadership team frequently contributes at national and international conferences, sharing expertise in grassroots engagement, fundraising, clinical development, and data collection- drawing on both professional experience and lived connection to the rare disease community.


## Looking into the future

Cure Mito Foundation has grown significantly since its start as Cure SURF1 in 2018. The CZI Rare As One grant, received in 2024, not only provides critical funding but also equips organizations with intensive training and coaching in key areas such as fundraising, strategic planning, leadership development, and more. This opportunity is helping Cure Mito Foundation to evolve from a grassroots effort into a sustainable and impactful organization with a long-term vision.

Some near-term initiatives include in-person conferences and research roundtables to expand the mitochondrial disease research network, strengthen collaboration across the scientific community, better understand patient needs, and prioritize critical areas of research. These initiatives will inform Cure Mito Foundation’s funding priorities and help the Foundation to more strategically support research that matters most to families.

Cure Mito Foundation will continue to foster a supportive and inclusive environment for the community by growing research programs, building upon patient registry and data sharing capabilities, and expanding family support and education initiatives, in hopes of accelerating progress and improving outcomes for individuals affected by Leigh syndrome and mitochondrial disease. ## Conclusion

Through relentless effort and dedication, the Cure Mito Foundation has shown that a small group of determined individuals can drive extraordinary change. By building a global patient registry, strengthening data sharing and research, developing patient-centric education and support resources, and establishing meaningful opportunities for collaboration among scientists, clinicians, and families worldwide, Cure Mito has laid a strong foundation for continued advancement. Now, with capacity-building support from CZI’s Rare As One grant, Cure Mito is positioned to accelerate progress even further - strategically expanding programs, strengthening research networks, and moving closer to effective treatments that can transform the future for everyone living with Leigh syndrome.
