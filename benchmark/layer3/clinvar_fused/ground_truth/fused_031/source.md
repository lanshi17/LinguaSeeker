# Molecular and structural characterization of a novel β-hemoglobinopathy caused by in Cis β-globin mutations in a Thai individual

## Abstract

Hemoglobin (Hb) E is the most common Hb variant in Southeast Asia. We described a novel form of Hb E caused by an in cis combination of Hb E and another β-hemoglobinopathy affecting Hb stability. Study was done on a Thai woman at a routine prenatal screening of thalassemia. Hb analysis was carried out by HPLC and capillary electrophoresis. Mutation and β-globin gene haplotype analyses were done using PCR-based assays. Structure of the abnormal Hb molecule was predicted using molecular dynamics simulations. The subject had mild normocytic anemia with Hb 10.3 g/dL. Hb-HPLC analysis revealed Hb A 2 A with 6.9% Hb A 2 , whereas capillary electrophoresis showed Hb A 2 EA with 3.6% Hb A 2 and 3.4% Hb E. No α- and β-thalassemia mutations were detected. Further β-globin gene analysis identified a novel variant caused by two in cis mutations, i.e., Hb E (codon 26 GAG > AAG) and Hb Palmerston North (codon 23 GTT > TTT), namely Hb E-Hb Palmerston North. Splicing site prediction of the Hb E-Palmerston North gene showed similar splicing scores at both normal and cryptic splice sites with those of the Hb E gene. System stability prediction of the Hb E-Palmerston North expression indicated a weakening αβ contact, resulting in instability and a dramatic reduction of Hb variant (3.4%). The novel Hb E variant, designated Hb E-Palmerston North, was characterized by unusually low levels, which was attributed to its high instability. The diagnosis of this novel Hb variant is best by multiplex PCR assay developed.


## Introduction

Hemoglobin (Hb) E is the most common β-thalassemic hemoglobinopathy in Southeast Asia, especially Thailand, Cambodia, Myanmar, Lao PDR, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Sri Lanka. Heterozygous Hb E has minimally abnormal RBC morphology 1 – 4 . Homozygous Hb E reveals hypochromic microcytic RBC with marked target cells. Interaction of Hb E and β-thalassemia leads to the Hb E-β-thalassemia disease with various clinical symptoms ranging from mild to severe anemia, requiring regular blood transfusion 1 – 4 . It is caused by a mutation at codon 26 of the β-globin gene (HBB: c.79G > A) and an amino acid substitution of GAG (Glu) to AAG (Lys) (rs33950507, ClinVar ID: 15161, and Uniprot ID: P68871 E27K). This mutation at codon 26 can also activate a cryptic splice site within exon 1 of the β-globin gene, leading to abnormal messenger RNA processing. Most β E -globin mRNA has the corrected splicing site at codon 30, generating Hb assembling as Hb E (α 2 β E 2 ) with the level at most of around 30–35% Hb E in the heterozygote. Some β E -globin mRNA activates an alternative splicing site at codon 25, generating a truncated variant protein. Hb E is therefore considered as a β + -thalassemia due to a reduction in mRNA splicing at the normal splice site. Hb E is also unstable due to the weak α 1 β 1 contact of the Hb E molecule 1 – 3 , which is the basis for the development of an effective screening method using dichlorophenolindophenol (DCIP), the Hb E screening test widely applied in a prevention and control program of thalassemia in the region 5 , 6 . Here, we described a novel form of Hb E, which is alternatively caused by two in cis mutations within a β-globin gene with less stable and is associated unusually with a reduction in Hb E expression in heterozygotic form.

### DNA analysis

Common α-thalassemia mutations (-- SEA , -- THAI , -α 3.7 , -α 4.2 , Hb Constant Spring and Hb Paksé) and β-thalassemia and Hb E mutations are routinely carried out in our laboratory by PCR-based methods as described 6 – 8 . DNA sequencing of the whole α- and β-globin genes was done using an ABI PRISM 3730 XL analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA). To define β-globin gene haplotypes of the patient, seven DNA polymorphisms within β-globin gene cluster including ε- Hinc II, G γ- Hind III, A γ- Hind III, ψβ- Hinc II, 3′ψβ- Hinc II, β- Ava II, and 3′β- BamH I, were examined by PCR-restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) as described 2 , 9 .

### Molecular confirmation of the Hb E-Palmerston North mutations

Locations of the two β-globin gene defects causing Hb E-Palmerston North were examined using allelic amplification 10 . The wild-type (codon 26; G AG) and Hb E (codon 26; A AG) alleles were amplified separately in two allele-specific PCR using a reverse primer G23R (5ʹ-CCAACCTGCCCAGGGCCTC-3ʹ) for wild-type specific allele and reverse primer G212 (5ʹ-CCAACCTGCCCAGGGCCTT-3ʹ) for Hb E specific allele with the common forward primer G78 (5ʹ-GCTTACCAAGCTGTGATTCC-3ʹ) to produce specific fragments of 442 bp, as shown in Fig. 2 . Amplification of the homozygous Hb E (β E /β E ) and normal subjects (β A /β A ) were used as a negative control for the wild-type allele and Hb E-specific allele, respectively. Each amplified fragment was then sequenced to define whether the two mutations were located in cis or in trans configurations.

### Development of a multiplex diagnostic PCR assay for Hb E-Palmerston North

Multiplex allele-specific PCR assay was developed to characterize Hb E and Hb E-Palmerston North, as shown in Fig. 3 . Common primers S1 (5ʹ-TGTCATCACTTAGACCTCAC-3ʹ) and S3 (5ʹ- TCCCATAGACTCACCCTGAA-3ʹ) were used to produce a fragment of 631 bp internal control. Hb E-specific primer G212 and primer S1, and Hb Palmerston North-specific primer G214 (5ʹ-CAAGGTGAACGTGGATGAAT − 3ʹ) and primer S3 were used to produce fragments of 270 bp and 409 bp specific for Hb E and Hb Palmerston North, respectively. The PCR reaction mixture (50 µl) contains 50–100 ng genomic DNA, 30 pmoles of all primers, 200 µM dNTPs, and 1 unit Taq DNA polymerase (New England Biolab, Inc., USA) in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer pH 8.3, 50 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl 2 and 0.01% gelatin. PCR was amplified on a T-Personal Thermocycler (Biometra; GmbH, Gottingen, Germany) with the PCR steps: 1 cycle of 94 °C for 3 min followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 65 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min. The PCR product was separated on 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, stained by ethidium bromide, and visualized under UV light.

### Splice site prediction of Hb E-Palmerston North gene

Human splice sites among wild-type, Hb E, Hb Palmerston North, Hb E-Palmerston North, and other Hb E variants with double mutations located in cis with other β-hemoglobinopathies were predicted using the online splice site prediction of Neural Network in NNSPLICE 0.9 version ( https://www.fruitfly.org/seq_tools/splice.html ) 11 . A score of 0 to 1 from the program output represents a potential splice site. The potential genes and exons in the human genomic DNA of the variants were predicted by the online FGENESH 2.6 program ( http://www.softberry.com/berry.phtml ) 12 . A log likelihood*10 score represents the weight of prediction of potential genes and exons in human genomic DNA.

### System preparation and molecular dynamics simulation

The X-ray crystal structure of human deoxyhemoglobin (PDB ID: 3HHB) 18 was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank ( http://www.rcsb.org/ ). Three mutant variants, Hb E (E26K), Hb Palmerston North (V23F), and the double mutant Hb Palmerston North (V23F/E26K), were generated by homology modeling using Swiss-Model webserver 19 with the wild-type structure as a template. Subsequently, the PDB2PQR web server 20 ( https://server.poissonboltzmann.org/pdb2pqr ) was utilized to assign protonation states to ionizable residues (D, E, R, K, and H) at pH 7.4. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the wild-type and mutant deoxyhemoglobin structures were performed using the AMBER 22 software package 21 , following a previously established MD protocol 22 , 23 . Briefly, the ff19SB force field 24 was employed for the protein, and parameters for the heme group were derived from the AMBER parameter database ( http://amber.manchester.ac.uk/ ). Each system was solvated in a TIP3P water box 25 with a 10 Å buffer and neutralized by the addition of sodium (Na⁺) or chloride (Cl⁻) ions, as appropriate. Following standard energy minimization, thermalization, and density equilibration procedures, production MD simulations were conducted under an isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble at 310 K and 1 atm for 500 ns per system. Trajectory analysis was performed using the CPPTRAJ module 26 within AmberTools 22, and three-dimensional (3D) protein structures were visualized using Chimera 1.17 27 .

## Results

An adult Thai woman was first diagnosed as having β-thalassemia trait based on the result of Hb-HPLC analysis of Hb A 2 A with elevated Hb A 2 (6.9%) and Hb F (1.0%) levels (Fig. 1 A). Complete blood count revealed a mild anemia with Rbc 3.9 × 10 12 /L, Hb 10.3 g/dL, Hct 33%, MCV 84.6 fL, MCH 26.4 pg, MCHC 31.2 g/dL, and RDW 17.3%. Her blood specimen was transferred to our center for prenatal diagnosis of thalassemia and hemoglobinopathies. No α- and β-thalassemia mutations were identified by DNA analysis. Repeated Hb analysis using capillary electrophoresis, which could report A 2 in the presence of Hb E, revealed Hb A of 92.2%, Hb A 2 of 3.6%, Hb E of 3.4%, and Hb F of 0.8% (Fig. 1 B). This Hb analysis pattern is similar to that of the patient with Hb E-blood transfusion. However, she had no history of blood transfusion. This unusually low level of Hb E in heterozygotes prompted us to examine the Hb E mutation of the woman by allele-specific PCR assay 6 . This analysis confirmed the existence of heterozygosity of the β E mutation at codon 26; G AG > A AG (data not shown). To identify further the possible existence of the β-thalassemia mutation, DNA sequencing of the whole β-globin gene was carried out. As shown in Fig. 1 C, instead of a β-thalassemia mutation, we unexpectedly identified the second mutation at codon 23; G TT (Val) > T TT (Phe) in addition to the β E mutation at codon 26; G AG (Glu) > A AG (Lys), previously undescribed in Thai population. This mutation at codon 23 corresponds to the Hb Palmerston North (rs33929459, ClinVar ID: 15306, and Uniprot ID: P68871 V24F) identified previously in a New Zealand patient with polycythemia 28 . However, we did realize that the compound heterozygotic state of Hb E / Hb Palmerston North was unlikely since Hb analysis revealed a major peak of Hb A with no abnormal Hb peak corresponding to Hb Palmerston North and an unusually low level of Hb E.

To prove the molecular characteristics of these two mutations if they are located in cis or in trans configuration, both the wild-type and Hb E alleles were separately amplified by allele-specific PCR and sequenced. As shown in Fig. 2 A, we observed normal DNA sequences at both codon 23 (GTT) and codon 26 (GAG) on the wild-type β-globin gene. However, both Hb Palmerston North (GTT > TTT) and Hb E (GAG > AAG) mutations were identified on the Hb E allele (Fig. 2 B). This result indicated that the β Palmerston North mutation is located in cis to the β E mutation on the same β-globin gene. Accordingly, this novel Hb variant was named the Hb E-Palmerston North (HBB: c.70G > T;79G > A). This novel mutational event has been submitted to the IthaGenes (IthaID: 4106) and ClinVar (ClinVar ID: 3777010) databases. Unfortunately, blood specimens of the family members could not be obtained. As shown in Fig. 3 , differential diagnosis of a plain Hb E and Hb E-Palmerston North could be done using a multiplex allele-specific PCR assay developed.

To explain the dramatically low expression of Hb E-Palmerston North as compared to Hb E and Hb Palmerston North in heterozygote states, splice sites and potential genes and exons in human genomic DNA were comparatively predicted among wild-type, Hb E, Hb Palmerston North, and Hb E-Palmerston North globin genes, as shown in Table 1 . In the online splice site prediction of Neural Network in the NNSPLICE 0.9 version, an equal potential splicing site score (0.64) of wild-type and all other variants was found at the normal donor splice site at codon 30 of the β-globin gene. In contrast, splicing site scores of the cryptic splice site at codon 25 within exon 1 were 0.54 for wild type, 0.59 for Hb Palmerston North, 0.98 for Hb E, and 0.99 for Hb E-Palmerston North. The higher scores of cryptic splicing as compared to the normal splicing for Hb E (0.98 vs. 0.64) and Hb E-Palmerston North (0.99 vs. 0.64) indicated the preferable mRNA splicing to take place at the cryptic splice site at codon 25. Similarly, in the online FGENESH 2.6 program, the decreased scores were observed in the Hb Palmerston North (11.82), Hb E (11.73), and Hb E-Palmerston North (11.46) as compared to the wild-type β-globin gene (12.09) for the normal donor splice site at codon 30. Function prediction of the Hb E and the Hb Palmerston North using the CADD, REVEL, SIFT, PolyPhen-2, and PROVEAN softwares indicates that they are both deleterious, damaging, or disease-causing in most functional predictions, as shown in Table 2 .

The stability of Hb (Fig. 4 A), including wild-type (Hb A), Hb E (E26K), Hb Palmerston North (V23F), and the double mutant Hb E-Palmerston North (V23F/E26K), was investigated using MD simulations. These methods, which considered the number of residue interactions and movements, aimed to determine appropriate simulation ranges for subsequent structural and dynamics analyses. To assess the impact of tetramer binding on each hemoglobin variant, root-mean-square deviations (RMSD), the number of hydrogen bonds (# H-bonds), and the number of atom contacts (# Contact atoms) were calculated and graphically represented (Fig. 4 B). The all-atom RMSD, along with the # H-bonds and # Contact atoms, indicated that all systems remained stable over the course of MD simulation time. However, only the final 50 ns of each simulation were used for further structural analysis. To investigate the stabilizing impact of each hemoglobin variant, we analyzed # Contact atoms formed at the α1β1 and α2β2 interfaces. Figure 5 A illustrates the interfaces, highlighting key residues within 8-Å sphere of the center of mass (C m ) of V23-E26. The average # Contact atoms at each interface was quantified. Our analysis revealed clearly a reduction in # Contact atoms in the double mutant Hb E-Palmerston North (V23F/E26K) (37.48 ± 0.40) as compared to wild-type (51.00 ± 0.45), Hb E (62.61 ± 0.44), and Hb Palmerston North (55.89 ± 0.39) at both interfaces. This decrease suggests destabilization of both interfaces due to the double mutation. We also generated contact distribution heatmaps to further understand the specific interactions affected by these modifications, as shown in Fig. 5 B. These heatmaps depict the frequency of contact formation between residues across the α1β1 and α2β2 interfaces for each system. The heatmap analysis visually confirms the reduced contact frequency observed in Hb E-Palmerston North. Furthermore, we examined the specific contacts formed at these interfaces (Fig. 5 C), focusing on residues known to be crucial for interface stability with ≥ 60% cut-off. In the wild-type, we observed consistent contact formation between residues such as P114(α1)–H116(β1), F117(α1)–R30(β1), and P119(α1)– V33(β1). A similar analysis was performed for the α2β2 interface. The single mutants, Hb E and Hb Palmerston North, exhibited a similar range of contact atom percentages (17–19 pairs). However, in Hb E-Palmerston North, a significant reduction in the occupancy of these key contacts was observed (4 pairs), corroborating the overall decrease in # Contact atoms and suggesting specific disruptions in interface interactions due to double mutations.

An adult Thai woman was first diagnosed as having β-thalassemia trait based on the result of Hb-HPLC analysis of Hb A 2 A with elevated Hb A 2 (6.9%) and Hb F (1.0%) levels (Fig. 1 A). Complete blood count revealed a mild anemia with Rbc 3.9 × 10 12 /L, Hb 10.3 g/dL, Hct 33%, MCV 84.6 fL, MCH 26.4 pg, MCHC 31.2 g/dL, and RDW 17.3%. Her blood specimen was transferred to our center for prenatal diagnosis of thalassemia and hemoglobinopathies. No α- and β-thalassemia mutations were identified by DNA analysis. Repeated Hb analysis using capillary electrophoresis, which could report A 2 in the presence of Hb E, revealed Hb A of 92.2%, Hb A 2 of 3.6%, Hb E of 3.4%, and Hb F of 0.8% (Fig. 1 B). This Hb analysis pattern is similar to that of the patient with Hb E-blood transfusion. However, she had no history of blood transfusion. This unusually low level of Hb E in heterozygotes prompted us to examine the Hb E mutation of the woman by allele-specific PCR assay 6 . This analysis confirmed the existence of heterozygosity of the β E mutation at codon 26; G AG > A AG (data not shown). To identify further the possible existence of the β-thalassemia mutation, DNA sequencing of the whole β-globin gene was carried out. As shown in Fig. 1 C, instead of a β-thalassemia mutation, we unexpectedly identified the second mutation at codon 23; G TT (Val) > T TT (Phe) in addition to the β E mutation at codon 26; G AG (Glu) > A AG (Lys), previously undescribed in Thai population. This mutation at codon 23 corresponds to the Hb Palmerston North (rs33929459, ClinVar ID: 15306, and Uniprot ID: P68871 V24F) identified previously in a New Zealand patient with polycythemia 28 . However, we did realize that the compound heterozygotic state of Hb E / Hb Palmerston North was unlikely since Hb analysis revealed a major peak of Hb A with no abnormal Hb peak corresponding to Hb Palmerston North and an unusually low level of Hb E.

To prove the molecular characteristics of these two mutations if they are located in cis or in trans configuration, both the wild-type and Hb E alleles were separately amplified by allele-specific PCR and sequenced. As shown in Fig. 2 A, we observed normal DNA sequences at both codon 23 (GTT) and codon 26 (GAG) on the wild-type β-globin gene. However, both Hb Palmerston North (GTT > TTT) and Hb E (GAG > AAG) mutations were identified on the Hb E allele (Fig. 2 B). This result indicated that the β Palmerston North mutation is located in cis to the β E mutation on the same β-globin gene. Accordingly, this novel Hb variant was named the Hb E-Palmerston North (HBB: c.70G > T;79G > A). This novel mutational event has been submitted to the IthaGenes (IthaID: 4106) and ClinVar (ClinVar ID: 3777010) databases. Unfortunately, blood specimens of the family members could not be obtained. As shown in Fig. 3 , differential diagnosis of a plain Hb E and Hb E-Palmerston North could be done using a multiplex allele-specific PCR assay developed.

To explain the dramatically low expression of Hb E-Palmerston North as compared to Hb E and Hb Palmerston North in heterozygote states, splice sites and potential genes and exons in human genomic DNA were comparatively predicted among wild-type, Hb E, Hb Palmerston North, and Hb E-Palmerston North globin genes, as shown in Table 1 . In the online splice site prediction of Neural Network in the NNSPLICE 0.9 version, an equal potential splicing site score (0.64) of wild-type and all other variants was found at the normal donor splice site at codon 30 of the β-globin gene. In contrast, splicing site scores of the cryptic splice site at codon 25 within exon 1 were 0.54 for wild type, 0.59 for Hb Palmerston North, 0.98 for Hb E, and 0.99 for Hb E-Palmerston North. The higher scores of cryptic splicing as compared to the normal splicing for Hb E (0.98 vs. 0.64) and Hb E-Palmerston North (0.99 vs. 0.64) indicated the preferable mRNA splicing to take place at the cryptic splice site at codon 25. Similarly, in the online FGENESH 2.6 program, the decreased scores were observed in the Hb Palmerston North (11.82), Hb E (11.73), and Hb E-Palmerston North (11.46) as compared to the wild-type β-globin gene (12.09) for the normal donor splice site at codon 30. The stability of Hb (Fig. 4 A), including wild-type (Hb A), Hb E (E26K), Hb Palmerston North (V23F), and the double mutant Hb E-Palmerston North (V23F/E26K), was investigated using MD simulations. These methods, which considered the number of residue interactions and movements, aimed to determine appropriate simulation ranges for subsequent structural and dynamics analyses. To assess the impact of tetramer binding on each hemoglobin variant, root-mean-square deviations (RMSD), the number of hydrogen bonds (# H-bonds), and the number of atom contacts (# Contact atoms) were calculated and graphically represented (Fig. 4 B). The all-atom RMSD, along with the # H-bonds and # Contact atoms, indicated that all systems remained stable over the course of MD simulation time. However, only the final 50 ns of each simulation were used for further structural analysis. To investigate the stabilizing impact of each hemoglobin variant, we analyzed # Contact atoms formed at the α1β1 and α2β2 interfaces. Figure 5 A illustrates the interfaces, highlighting key residues within 8-Å sphere of the center of mass (C m ) of V23-E26. The average # Contact atoms at each interface was quantified. Our analysis revealed clearly a reduction in # Contact atoms in the double mutant Hb E-Palmerston North (V23F/E26K) (37.48 ± 0.40) as compared to wild-type (51.00 ± 0.45), Hb E (62.61 ± 0.44), and Hb Palmerston North (55.89 ± 0.39) at both interfaces. This decrease suggests destabilization of both interfaces due to the double mutation. We also generated contact distribution heatmaps to further understand the specific interactions affected by these modifications, as shown in Fig. 5 B. These heatmaps depict the frequency of contact formation between residues across the α1β1 and α2β2 interfaces for each system. The heatmap analysis visually confirms the reduced contact frequency observed in Hb E-Palmerston North. Furthermore, we examined the specific contacts formed at these interfaces (Fig. 5 C), focusing on residues known to be crucial for interface stability with ≥ 60% cut-off. In the wild-type, we observed consistent contact formation between residues such as P114(α1)–H116(β1), F117(α1)–R30(β1), and P119(α1)– V33(β1). A similar analysis was performed for the α2β2 interface. The single mutants, Hb E and Hb Palmerston North, exhibited a similar range of contact atom percentages (17–19 pairs). However, in Hb E-Palmerston North, a significant reduction in the occupancy of these key contacts was observed (4 pairs), corroborating the overall decrease in # Contact atoms and suggesting specific disruptions in interface interactions due to double mutations.

## Discussion

Hb E is the most common β chain variant (α 2 β 2 26Glu> Lys ) in Southeast Asia. The β E chain is synthesized at a reduced rate compared to its corresponding β A chain due to the creation of an abnormal splicing within the exon 1 of the β-globin gene 29 . The heterozygous state of Hb E is asymptomatic, but Hb E homozygote may be associated with mild hypochromic microcytosis 30 . In an area where both Hb E and thalassemia are prevalent, such as Southeast Asia, genetic interactions between them are common 3 . Interaction of Hb E with β-thalassemia leads to the Hb E-β-thalassemia disease with variable clinical phenotype ranging from thalassemia intermedia to transfusion-dependent thalassemia 31 , 32 . Double heterozygosity of Hb E and α 0 -thalassemia can result in a reduction of Hb E levels due to the limited availability of α-globin chains on the αβ-assembly 33 . The association of Hb E with Hb H disease commonly seen in the region leads to a complex thalassemia syndrome known as AEBart’s disease with a further reduction in Hb E level 34 .

At present, six double mutations of Hb E located in cis with other β-hemoglobinopathies have been described, including Hb Corbeil (HBB: c.[79G > A;314G > C]) 35 , Hb T-Cambodia (HBB: c.[79G > A;364G > C]) 36 , Hb E-Udon Thani (HBB: c.[79G > A;92 + 7 A > G]) 10 , HBB: c.[79G > A;92 + 7 A > T] 37 , Hb E-Gurdaspur (HBB: c.[79G > A;313 A > G]) 38 , and Hb E-Myanmar (HBB: c.[79G > A;198G > C]) 39 . We reported for the first time an unusual form of Hb E, namely the Hb E-Palmerston North (HBB: c.70G > T;79G > A), caused by double mutations within exon 1 of the β-globin gene with great reduction in Hb E phenotypic expression.

While Hb E (HBB: c.79G > A) is the most common Hb variant among the Southeast Asian population, Hb Palmerston North (HBB: c.70G > T) is a rare variant that has been reported originally in a New Zealand subject with polycythemia and later in American people 28 , 40 . It has never been found in Thailand 41 . This rare Hb variant has been considered unstable and associated with increased oxygen affinity and secondary erythrocytosis. It is classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic by ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines 2015 28 , 40 , 42 . The replacement of valine to phenylalanine at codon 23 of the Hb Palmerston North does not alter the total charge of the Hb molecule. This should lead to a co-migration of Hb Palmerston North and Hb A on capillary electrophoresis. However, it has been shown that Hb Palmerston North could be separated from Hb A on isoelectric focusing with the amount of 40% of total Hb in the heterozygotic form 28 , 40 .

The finding of Hb E-Palmerston North in a Thai pregnant woman is quite unusual. In this Thai subject, apart from the very low level of Hb E expression [6.9% (Hb E + Hb A 2 ) by HPLC and 3.4% Hb E by capillary electrophoresis] (Fig. 1 ), this Thai subject was generally healthy and had only mild anemia as usually seen among pregnant women with Hb E trait. Hemolytic anemia was suspected in the subject. Unfortunately, due to the lack of fresh blood specimens from the proband and her family members, further investigation of hemolytic markers (such as Heinz bodies, LDH, haptoglobin, and reticulocyte) could not be performed. We have also noted that this Thai subject was positive in the routine dichlorophenolindophenol (DCIP) screening test for Hb E 5 , though with the very low level of Hb E-Palmerston North (3.4%), the data indicating an instability of the Hb E-Palmerston North molecule. It is most likely that Hb E-Palmerston North is migrated at the same position as Hb E on capillary electrophoresis. In contrast, the level of Hb E in heterozygotic form without co-inheritance of α-thalassemia is around 30% 34 , 43 . The lower level of Hb E-Palmerston North, as compared to Hb E, could be associated with a more abnormal mRNA splicing. In the online splice site, potential genes and exons in the human genomic DNA, Hb E-Palmerston North increased the abnormal donor splicing site score at codon 25 by Neural Network prediction (0.54 for wild type vs. 0.99 for Hb E-Palmerston North). In addition, it decreased the normal donor splicing site score at codon 30 by the FGENESH 2.6 program (12.09 for wild type vs. 11.46 for Hb E-Palmerston North) (Table 1 ). In fact, this is not unexpected. It has been shown that mutations at the sequence -GT GGT GAG G- between codons 24 to 27 in exon 1 of β-globin gene e.g. codon 24 GGT > GGA (HBB: c.75T > A), Hb E (codon 26 GAG > AAG), and Hb Knossos (codon 27 GCC > TCC, HBB: c.82G > T) could activate the cryptic donor splice site in exon 1 and affect mRNA processing 1 , 3 , 29 . Pure heterozygous Hb E expresses a Hb E level of around 30% on Hb analysis due to this abnormal mRNA processing. As shown in Table 1 , Hb E mutation alone can increase the abnormal donor splicing site at codon 25 by the Neural Network prediction (0.54 vs. 0.98) and decrease the normal donor splicing site at codon 30 by the FGENESH 2.6 program (12.09 vs. 11.73), quite similar to those of the Hb E-Palmerston North described above. However, the level of 40% of Hb Palmerston North (codon 23 GTT > TTT) found in the New Zealand heterozygote subject without Hb E mutation 40 likely indicated that the Hb Palmerston North mutation alone does not have much effect on the activation of the cryptic donor splice site in exon 1 of β-globin gene. We found that both Hb E and Hb E-Palmerston North had similar scores of cryptic donor splicing site at codon 25 in the Neural Network prediction (0.98 vs. 0.99) and normal donor splicing site at codon 30 (11.73 vs. 11.46) in the FGENESH 2.6 program. This data confirms that the introduction of the Hb Palmerston North mutation at codon 23 onto the Hb E gene does not further worsen splicing abnormality seen in the Hb E gene. Therefore, the lower level of Hb E-Palmerston North due to the increased abnormal splicing is therefore unlikely.

Alternatively, this very low level of Hb E-Palmerston North as compared to Hb E could be associated with Hb instability. As shown in Table 2 , both Hb E and Hb Palmerston North are associated with deleterious, damaging, or disease-causing in most functional predictions. These observations are strongly supported by our MD results, revealing that the double mutant Hb E-Palmerston North (V23F/E26K) exhibits significant destabilization of the α1β1 and α2β2 interfaces compared to the wild-type Hb A and the variants with single mutation, Hb E and Hb Palmerston North (Fig. 4 ). This destabilization is evidenced by a reduction in the number of inter-subunit contact atoms (Fig. 5 A), a decrease in contact frequency (Fig. 5 B), and a disruption of key stabilizing interactions (Fig. 5 C). Specifically, residues known to be crucial for interface stability, such as P114(α1), H116(β1), F117(α1), R30(β1), P119(α1), and V33(β1), showed reduced contact occupancy in Hb E-Palmerston North. Noted that the residues F117 and P119 are essential for the stable interaction between the α-globin chain and the α-hemoglobin stabilizing protein 44 . The combination effect in the double mutant appears to be synergistic, leading to a more significant disruption of the interface. The E26K mutation (Hb E) is located near the inter-subunit interface and introduces a change in charge, potentially affecting electrostatic interactions 45 . Similarly, the V23F mutation (Hb Palmerston North) introduces a bulky hydrophobic residue, which could alter packing and hydrophobic interactions at the interface 46 . When combined in Hb E-Palmerston North, these mutations likely create a combined effect that disrupts the balance of interactions required for interface stability. The destabilization of the α1β1 and α2β2 interfaces in Hb E-Palmerston North may have significant functional implications. Hemoglobin’s cooperative oxygen binding is critically dependent on the integrity of these interfaces 47 . Disruption of these interfaces could potentially affect cooperativity and the efficiency of oxygen transport. Our computational study suggests that the double mutation E26K/V23F in Hb E-Palmerston North leads to destabilization of the α1β1 and α2β2 interfaces, resulting in instability of the overall structure. We do not know exactly the prevalence of Hb E-Palmerston North in the population. It is conceivable that screening this novel variant may be difficult and could be missed by Hb analysis due to its low percentage (Fig. 1 ). In addition, it has been shown that the decreased level of Hb E could result from genetic co-inheritance of α-thalassemia commonly encountered in the region 33 , which could bring more diagnostic confusion. Hb E is the most common β chain variant (α 2 β 2 26Glu> Lys ) in Southeast Asia. The β E chain is synthesized at a reduced rate compared to its corresponding β A chain due to the creation of an abnormal splicing within the exon 1 of the β-globin gene 29 . The heterozygous state of Hb E is asymptomatic, but Hb E homozygote may be associated with mild hypochromic microcytosis 30 . In an area where both Hb E and thalassemia are prevalent, such as Southeast Asia, genetic interactions between them are common 3 . Interaction of Hb E with β-thalassemia leads to the Hb E-β-thalassemia disease with variable clinical phenotype ranging from thalassemia intermedia to transfusion-dependent thalassemia 31 , 32 . Double heterozygosity of Hb E and α 0 -thalassemia can result in a reduction of Hb E levels due to the limited availability of α-globin chains on the αβ-assembly 33 . The association of Hb E with Hb H disease commonly seen in the region leads to a complex thalassemia syndrome known as AEBart’s disease with a further reduction in Hb E level 34 .

At present, six double mutations of Hb E located in cis with other β-hemoglobinopathies have been described, including Hb Corbeil (HBB: c.[79G > A;314G > C]) 35 , Hb T-Cambodia (HBB: c.[79G > A;364G > C]) 36 , Hb E-Udon Thani (HBB: c.[79G > A;92 + 7 A > G]) 10 , HBB: c.[79G > A;92 + 7 A > T] 37 , Hb E-Gurdaspur (HBB: c.[79G > A;313 A > G]) 38 , and Hb E-Myanmar (HBB: c.[79G > A;198G > C]) 39 . We reported for the first time an unusual form of Hb E, namely the Hb E-Palmerston North (HBB: c.70G > T;79G > A), caused by double mutations within exon 1 of the β-globin gene with great reduction in Hb E phenotypic expression.

While Hb E (HBB: c.79G > A) is the most common Hb variant among the Southeast Asian population, Hb Palmerston North (HBB: c.70G > T) is a rare variant that has been reported originally in a New Zealand subject with polycythemia and later in American people 28 , 40 . It has never been found in Thailand 41 . This rare Hb variant has been considered unstable and associated with increased oxygen affinity and secondary erythrocytosis. It is classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic by ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines 2015 28 , 40 , 42 . The replacement of valine to phenylalanine at codon 23 of the Hb Palmerston North does not alter the total charge of the Hb molecule. This should lead to a co-migration of Hb Palmerston North and Hb A on capillary electrophoresis. However, it has been shown that Hb Palmerston North could be separated from Hb A on isoelectric focusing with the amount of 40% of total Hb in the heterozygotic form 28 , 40 .

The finding of Hb E-Palmerston North in a Thai pregnant woman is quite unusual. In this Thai subject, apart from the very low level of Hb E expression [6.9% (Hb E + Hb A 2 ) by HPLC and 3.4% Hb E by capillary electrophoresis] (Fig. 1 ), this Thai subject was generally healthy and had only mild anemia as usually seen among pregnant women with Hb E trait. Hemolytic anemia was suspected in the subject. Unfortunately, due to the lack of fresh blood specimens from the proband and her family members, further investigation of hemolytic markers (such as Heinz bodies, LDH, haptoglobin, and reticulocyte) could not be performed. We have also noted that this Thai subject was positive in the routine dichlorophenolindophenol (DCIP) screening test for Hb E 5 , though with the very low level of Hb E-Palmerston North (3.4%), the data indicating an instability of the Hb E-Palmerston North molecule. It is most likely that Hb E-Palmerston North is migrated at the same position as Hb E on capillary electrophoresis. In contrast, the level of Hb E in heterozygotic form without co-inheritance of α-thalassemia is around 30% 34 , 43 . The lower level of Hb E-Palmerston North, as compared to Hb E, could be associated with a more abnormal mRNA splicing. In the online splice site, potential genes and exons in the human genomic DNA, Hb E-Palmerston North increased the abnormal donor splicing site score at codon 25 by Neural Network prediction (0.54 for wild type vs. 0.99 for Hb E-Palmerston North). In addition, it decreased the normal donor splicing site score at codon 30 by the FGENESH 2.6 program (12.09 for wild type vs. 11.46 for Hb E-Palmerston North) (Table 1 ). In fact, this is not unexpected. It has been shown that mutations at the sequence -GT GGT GAG G- between codons 24 to 27 in exon 1 of β-globin gene e.g. codon 24 GGT > GGA (HBB: c.75T > A), Hb E (codon 26 GAG > AAG), and Hb Knossos (codon 27 GCC > TCC, HBB: c.82G > T) could activate the cryptic donor splice site in exon 1 and affect mRNA processing 1 , 3 , 29 . Pure heterozygous Hb E expresses a Hb E level of around 30% on Hb analysis due to this abnormal mRNA processing. As shown in Table 1 , Hb E mutation alone can increase the abnormal donor splicing site at codon 25 by the Neural Network prediction (0.54 vs. 0.98) and decrease the normal donor splicing site at codon 30 by the FGENESH 2.6 program (12.09 vs. 11.73), quite similar to those of the Hb E-Palmerston North described above. However, the level of 40% of Hb Palmerston North (codon 23 GTT > TTT) found in the New Zealand heterozygote subject without Hb E mutation 40 likely indicated that the Hb Palmerston North mutation alone does not have much effect on the activation of the cryptic donor splice site in exon 1 of β-globin gene. We found that both Hb E and Hb E-Palmerston North had similar scores of cryptic donor splicing site at codon 25 in the Neural Network prediction (0.98 vs. 0.99) and normal donor splicing site at codon 30 (11.73 vs. 11.46) in the FGENESH 2.6 program. This data confirms that the introduction of the Hb Palmerston North mutation at codon 23 onto the Hb E gene does not further worsen splicing abnormality seen in the Hb E gene. Therefore, the lower level of Hb E-Palmerston North due to the increased abnormal splicing is therefore unlikely.

Alternatively, this very low level of Hb E-Palmerston North as compared to Hb E could be associated with Hb instability. As shown in Table 2 , both Hb E and Hb Palmerston North are associated with deleterious, damaging, or disease-causing in most functional predictions. These observations are strongly supported by our MD results, revealing that the double mutant Hb E-Palmerston North (V23F/E26K) exhibits significant destabilization of the α1β1 and α2β2 interfaces compared to the wild-type Hb A and the variants with single mutation, Hb E and Hb Palmerston North (Fig. 4 ). This destabilization is evidenced by a reduction in the number of inter-subunit contact atoms (Fig. 5 A), a decrease in contact frequency (Fig. 5 B), and a disruption of key stabilizing interactions (Fig. 5 C). Specifically, residues known to be crucial for interface stability, such as P114(α1), H116(β1), F117(α1), R30(β1), P119(α1), and V33(β1), showed reduced contact occupancy in Hb E-Palmerston North. Noted that the residues F117 and P119 are essential for the stable interaction between the α-globin chain and the α-hemoglobin stabilizing protein 44 . The combination effect in the double mutant appears to be synergistic, leading to a more significant disruption of the interface. The E26K mutation (Hb E) is located near the inter-subunit interface and introduces a change in charge, potentially affecting electrostatic interactions 45 . Similarly, the V23F mutation (Hb Palmerston North) introduces a bulky hydrophobic residue, which could alter packing and hydrophobic interactions at the interface 46 . When combined in Hb E-Palmerston North, these mutations likely create a combined effect that disrupts the balance of interactions required for interface stability.
