# Neuroimaging and spinal manifestations of mucopolysaccharidosis type I: Insights from a pediatric case

## Abstract

Mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by α-L-iduronidase deficiency, leading to glycosaminoglycan accumulation. We report a 7-year-old child with Hurler syndrome, presenting with growth delay, skeletal deformities, and neurodevelopmental impairment. Brain and spine MRI revealed white matter abnormalities, supratentorial cysts, odontoid dysplasia, and cervical canal stenosis. These findings reflect the multisystemic nature of MPS I and the value of early neuroimaging. Despite available therapies, neurological and skeletal complications remain a major challenge.


## Introduction

Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) are a group of inherited lysosomal storage disorders caused by deficiencies in specific lysosomal enzymes, leading to the accumulation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and resulting in progressive multisystemic impairment [ 1 ].

There are 7 recognized types of MPS (I, II, III, IV, VI, VII, and IX), each subdivided into distinct clinical subtypes depending on the specific enzyme deficiency and the severity of clinical manifestations [ 2 ].

MPS I is a particularly severe form, associated with a high disease burden and early mortality.

Without treatment, children with the severe phenotype typically succumb within the first decade of life, primarily due to multisystemic complications, including central nervous system involvement [ 3 ].

## Case report

A 7-year-old girl with a confirmed diagnosis of mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I) was referred to our department for brain and spine MRI to evaluate potential cerebrospinal involvement, The child had been followed since early childhood for significant statural and ponderal retardation, and exhibited multiple skeletal dysmorphisms, including genu valgum, joint stiffness, kyphosis, and coarse facial features, She had been receiving enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) since early childhood as part of her ongoing treatment.

Clinically, the patient presented with progressive joint contractures, limited range of motion, and mild intellectual disability. There was no history of seizures, but the patient exhibited hypotonia, gait imbalance, and frequent upper airway infections. The child had also developed corneal clouding and mild sensorineural hearing loss, both documented in previous assessments.

Neurological examination revealed no focal motor deficit, but mild unsteadiness on tandem gait and diffuse muscular hypotonia, an MRI of the brain and spine was performed for comprehensive evaluation ( Fig. 1 ). There is diffuse bilateral dilatation of the Virchow–Robin spaces (white arrow). Multiple cystic formations are noted in the supratentorial regions. Fig 1:

The cervical spine shows odontoid process dysplasia and C1 hypoplasia, along with thickening of the posterior longitudinal ligament at the C2–C3 level, leading to narrowing of the cervical spinal canal without associated spinal cord abnormalities (white arrow). Additionally, a rostrum-like configuration is observed in the vertebral bodies of C4, C5, and C6.

In the lumbar spine, there is a grade 1 spondylolisthesis of L3 over L4 (black arrow), accompanied by multilevel lumbar disc slippage, resulting in reduced foraminal spaces.

## Discussion

Mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I) is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder caused by a deficiency of alpha-L-iduronidase, an enzyme involved in the degradation of the glycosaminoglycans dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate. The global incidence is estimated at approximately 0.82 per 100,000 live births, with the severe form occurring in about 1 in 100,000 births and the attenuated forms in roughly 1 in 500,000. In Europe, the incidence is similar, estimated at approximately 1 in 100,000 [ 4 ].

This autosomal recessive disorder affects both sexes equally, Consanguinity, observed in approximately 70% of cases, constitutes a major risk factor. A high proportion of severe forms (up to 60%) has been reported, whereas attenuated phenotypes are likely underdiagnosed due to their subtler clinical presentation.

MPS I is traditionally classified into 3 clinical forms based on symptom severity and the extent of neurological involvement [ 2 ]: Hurler syndrome (severe form), Hurler–Scheie syndrome (intermediate form), and Scheie syndrome (mild form). However, these phenotypes represent a clinical continuum, with overlapping features and no identifiable biochemical differences among them.

At birth, infants with MPS I may appear clinically normal. However, early signs often include progressively coarse facial features, recurrent rhinitis, upper airway obstruction, inguinal or umbilical hernias, thoracolumbar kyphosis, and hepatosplenomegaly. Airway obstruction can lead to sleep apnea, as well as recurrent respiratory and otologic infections.

Cardiac involvement is common and typically manifests as valvular thickening, which may impair valve mobility and lead to regurgitation or stenosis. Dysostosis multiplex, a hallmark skeletal abnormality, involves multiple sites including the intervertebral discs, vertebral bodies, odontoid process, and dura mater, often resulting in spinal deformities and spinal cord compression.

Central nervous system (CNS) involvement may present with white matter abnormalities, enlarged perivascular (Virchow–Robin) spaces, hydrocephalus, cerebral atrophy, subarachnoid space enlargement, and compressive myelopathy, among other features. Neurodevelopmental impairment often begins within the first year of life and progresses after the second year.

Ophthalmologic manifestations include corneal clouding, ocular hypertension or glaucoma, retinal degeneration, optic nerve swelling or atrophy, refractive errors, and ocular motility disorders.

In cases of clinical suspicion, urinary glycosaminoglycan (GAG) analysis is recommended as a rapid and effective screening tool. Definitive diagnosis is established by measuring alpha-L-iduronidase enzyme activity, which remains the gold standard. In patients undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), urinary GAG levels in combination with enzyme activity are useful for treatment monitoring .

Molecular confirmation is achieved by identifying mutations in the IDUA gene, located on chromosome 4p16.3. The most common mutations reported in the European population include c.1205G>A (p.Trp402Ter), c.208C>T (p.Gln70Ter), and c.1598C>G (p.Pro533Arg) [ 3 ].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the imaging modality of choice for evaluating brain and spinal cord abnormalities in patients with mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS), as in other metabolic disorders.

One of the most characteristic findings on brain MRI in young MPS patients is the presence of prominent or enlarged perivascular spaces (PVS). These are frequently described as having a cribriform or sieve-like appearance. Notably, PVS enlargement within the corpus callosum (CC) has been highlighted in the literature as a particularly distinctive feature in MPS. Some authors have also described an atypical peripheral distribution of enlarged PVS at the cortical–subcortical junction, forming a pattern often referred to as “pearl-on-a-string.”

In addition, delayed myelination of the white matter is frequently observed in children with MPS. In early stages, this may be the only detectable abnormality. Over time, it may evolve into patchy T2-weighted hyperintensities within the white matter, which reflect underlying structural changes and are commonly reported [ 3 ].

Brain atrophy is also a common MRI finding in MPS patients, although its underlying physiopathology remains incompletely understood. A widely accepted hypothesis suggests that glycosaminoglycan (GAG) accumulation leads to neuronal death and reactive gliosis. Atrophy is usually evident in the first years of life, primarily involving the cerebral cortex, and can be diffuse, symmetrical, or asymmetrical. On imaging, it manifests as enlargement of cortical sulci and fissures.

Hydrocephalus in patients with mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I) may result from 2 main mechanisms: impaired cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) reabsorption due to glycosaminoglycan (GAG) accumulation within the meninges, and abnormal bone proliferation at the skull base, which reduces cerebral venous outflow. These mechanisms contribute to the development of communicating hydrocephalus, predominantly affecting the lateral and third ventricles. The resulting expansion of CSF spaces often leads to macrocephaly, and if hydrocephalus persists, it can cause optic nerve sheath enlargement, followed by optic atrophy [ 2 ].

One of the most debilitating manifestations of enzyme deficiency in children with MPS I—especially in the Hurler phenotype—is craniocervical junction (CCJ) distortion. Cervical spinal canal stenosis is a hallmark of MPS I, most frequently involving the atlantoaxial joint, although the thoracic and lumbar spine may also be affected to a lesser extent. The etiology of spinal stenosis is multifactorial. MPS I predisposes to atlantoaxial instability, primarily due to odontoid dysplasia (ranging from hypoplasia to aplasia) in conjunction with ligamentous laxity, particularly of the transverse ligament of the atlas. The severity of the instability is directly proportional to the degree of ligamentous hypertrophy—the more unstable the joint, the more pronounced the thickening. This hypertrophy, along with instability, often results in anterior displacement of the posterior arch of C1, further narrowing the spinal canal. Additionally, GAG accumulation within the dura and paraspinal ligaments contributes to foramen magnum narrowing and upper cervical canal stenosis, potentially leading to compressive myelopathy.

On MRI, the odontoid process typically appears dysplastic and is surrounded by a soft tissue mass, which is iso- to hypointense on T1-weighted images and hypointense on T2-weighted sequences. Craniocervical junction abnormalities reflect various stages of dens development, ranging from complete aplasia to mild hypoplasia or normal morphology. Ligamentous laxity around the axis further increases the risk of acute or chronic spinal cord injury [ 2 ].

Skeletal involvement, a key feature of MPS I, is collectively referred to as dysostosis multiplex. The underlying physiopathlogy includes GAG deposition in skeletal tissue and defective bone maturation. In the spine, MRI commonly reveals abnormalities such as platyspondyly, anterior beaking, posterior scalloping, and wedge-shaped vertebral bodies, resulting in a bullet-shaped appearance. Changes in the intervertebral discs may include disc space widening, dehydration, bulging, or herniation, all of which can contribute to spinal deformities like scoliosis and thoracolumbar kyphosis, potentially reducing spinal canal caliber and causing cord compression.

Thoracolumbar kyphosis, often presenting as a gibbus deformity, is particularly suggestive of MPS I. It results from defective growth of the anterosuperior vertebral bodies, leading to wedge-shaped vertebral deformities. Odontoid dysplasia, as previously described, also forms part of the spinal disease spectrum in MPS.

In the skull, imaging may show diploic space thickening, macrocephaly due to CSF space expansion, and abnormal skull base morphology, including the classic J-shaped sella turcica [ 2 ].

Treatment options for MPS I include hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) and enzyme replacement therapy (ERT). HSCT is mainly indicated for patients with severe (Hurler) forms and is most beneficial when performed before the age of 2, as early intervention can help preserve neurocognitive function. However, HSCT does not fully correct skeletal, ocular, or cardiac complications. ERT with laronidase (Aldurazyme®) can improve endurance, respiratory function, and reduce organomegaly, but its efficacy is limited by poor penetration into the central nervous system and bone, making it less effective for neurologic or skeletal disease progression.

Both treatments have significant limitations: ERT requires lifelong weekly infusions and can induce immune responses, and neither therapy fully addresses all systemic manifestations. Consequently, emerging approaches such as gene therapy, intrathecal ERT, substrate reduction therapy, and small molecule treatments are under investigation to overcome these limitations and improve long-term outcomes in patients with MPS I [ 5 ].
