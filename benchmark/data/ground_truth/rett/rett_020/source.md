## Abstract

Objective: To assess the age and variants of recently identified males and set the stage for further study of clinical features in males.

Methods: Genetic information on the specific variant was acquired from the coordinator (KF) of the Parent Group for Males. Data were collected indicating whether these variants were or transmitted from the mother and whether males who appeared to meet the diagnostic criteria for Rett syndrome (RTT) had mosaicism for the variant.

Results: Fifty-nine males were identified through the parent group. Their ages ranged from 2 to 28 years, with the median age being 7.0 years and the mean age being 10.8 years. Of these variants, forty-six (78.0%) were , nine (15.3%) were maternally inherited, and for four (6.8%), inheritance was not known. Eleven (18.6%) were mosaic, 10 with somatic mosaicism and one with Klinefelter syndrome (47XXY). Together with males reported previously from the US Natural History Study, the total group represents 85 males, of whom 27 are deceased.

Conclusion: These data on males with variants are important to caregivers, physicians, and researchers to begin to characterize their historical and clinical features, to improve diagnostic recognition and overall care, and to accelerate access to therapeutic studies including gene replacement strategies. Equal access to such therapies for males is critical.

## Introduction

Originally described in 1966 by Andreas Rett [1] and recognized internationally after the publication by Bengt Hagberg in 1983 [2], Rett syndrome (RTT) is a progressive neurodevelopmental disorder due mainly to loss-of-function variations in the X-linked methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 ( ) gene at Xq28 [3]. RTT was first described in females exclusively, with scarce attention being given to males with variants in this gene. In the original criteria for RTT, males were specifically excluded [4]. Due to the X-linked dominant role of this gene, the presumed result in males with such variants was lethality or early demise. Despite numerous reports of males surviving into their teens and twenties, the idea of male lethality has been persistent [5]. Males with duplications at Xq28 including comprise a different clinical entity and are not included [6]. Individual reports of males having some features resembling those seen in RTT, including some who appeared to meet established clinical criteria for RTT, emerged during the thirty years after the Hagberg publication, particularly after the association with . Males with loss-of-function variants were identified with varying levels of clinical difficulties including a progressive neonatal encephalopathy [7] and a male RTT encephalopathy [8], and males with moderate to severe intellectual disability [8]. In addition, a small number of males had two populations of X chromosomes with different alleles, one normal, the other having a loss-of-function genetic variant. This resulted either from somatic mosaicism due to a post-conception event resulting in two populations of cells with different X chromosomes [9] or in association with Klinefelter (47XXY) syndrome [10].

In 2019, Neul et al. presented data from the RTT US Natural History Study (NHS) linking variants in 30 males with the same features noted above. These results produced a vigorous response from parents in the US and abroad suggesting a far greater number of males with this range of features. Indeed, reviewing the literature from 1980 to the present indicates numerous reports of male involvement, both before and after the association of variants in . The coordinator of the parent-support group of parents with affected males identified fifty-nine males into the present cohort. The combined group from the US NHS and this group represents eighty-five males, the largest single cohort to-date with both genetic and clinical information. Four of these males were reported in the NHS publication [8] and were also reported separately [9, 11, 12], including one contained in both the NHS report and that of Cooley-Coleman et al. [9].

In the years prior to the identification of the genetic basis for RTT, nine males were reported, five with features of classic RTT [13–16] and four with atypical RTT [17–20]. One of the males with classic RTT also had Klinefelter syndrome. Since 1999, aside from the US NHS report, eighty-five unique males have been described: eighteen with classic RTT [10–12, 21–30], four with atypical RTT [31–34], twenty-three with the neonatal encephalopathy [7, 31, 35–43], nine with features consistent with the male RTT encephalopathy [28, 42–46], and thirty-three with intellectual disability [28, 43, 47–53]. Another male is reported to be asymptomatic, representing a benign variant [54] as this variant is also present in his mother, his maternal grandfather, and his mother’s maternal aunts, all of whom were entirely asymptomatic. Ten of the males with classic RTT displayed somatic mosaicism. At least two additional males had Klinefelter syndrome and classic RTT [10].

Disease-modifying or potentially curative therapeutic products including gene therapy have been publicized, and parents and other caregivers of males have raised increasing interest in access to approved products or for acceptance into on-going or planned clinical trials. Thus, the information provided through this study could provide critical information to support the inclusion of males in such avenues of therapy.

## Methods

Fifty-nine males were identified for the present cohort. Parents in the support group led by one of the authors (KF) had been discussing the need for a potential research study on males for some time. Most resided in the US, but others were residents of Australia, Brazil, Canada, Denmark, Egypt, the Netherlands, Norway, Philippines, Poland, Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom. Specific genetic information regarding these variants was collated from individual reports provided by the parents. The parents provided the genetic information and any clinical information for their children does not represent clinician verification.

Families were recruited by a post made by KF in a private parent/caregiver Facebook group. As the need for more accurate information and research about these males had been an ongoing conversation, families were already engaged in the idea. When the opportunity to review the results for males was presented, the parent group leader (KF) identified caregivers who were willing to forward these results to the senior author (AKP). These results included, in most instances, whether each male’s mutation was or inherited. These test results were forwarded by the caregivers to the senior author (AKP) by password protected secure email.

Additionally, several caregivers in this group had previously indicated their son had a mosaic RTT mutation. These males showed signs of classic RTT through parent reports. As a result, for thirteen males, additional testing was obtained through the Greenwood Genetics Center for mothers who could not confirm the status of their sons’ variant or to verify the mosaic status for males who had evidence of classic RTT.

For this group of 13, the Greenwood Genetic Center agreed to provide to interested families this additional testing free of charge. In these instances, the caregivers were asked individually if they were interested in having further testing done for themselves (de novo test) and/or their sons (mosaic) through Greenwood Genetic Center. Throughout this process, the parents only participated if they felt completely comfortable doing so. All were interested and saw it as an opportunity to obtain clear answers they would not ordinarily be able to obtain. The parents agreed to have the results of the additional testing sent to the senior author (AKP).

Exempt regulatory approval was provided by UAB (300011534-004).

## Results

Specific variants were identified in the fifty-nine males who were self-identified to KF and subsequently chose to enroll in this study. Genetic testing was also completed in

fifty-five mothers of these males (Table 1). Eleven males were shown to be mosaic for their respective variant (Table 1). Ten of these demonstrated somatic mosaicism and one had Klinefelter syndrome (47XXY).

Together with the NHS data and the Ferdinandsen group, we now have data on 85 males with variants and including the 13 males with mosaicism. The variants span the entire gene from exon 1 through exon 4 and include variants seen in females with RTT as well as others not previously recognized in females (Tables 2 and 3).

Detailed genetic information on the more recent group (Table 1) revealed that of the 59 males in this group, 46 (78.0%) had variants, 9 (15.3%) were transmitted from the mother, 1 (1.7%) was tested but the results are not known, and 3 (5.1%) were not tested. mosaicism was identified in 11 (18.6%). The age range of these males is 2.0-28.0 years, with a median age of 7.0 and a mean age of 10.8 years.

The reported clinical features resemble the range of involvement described in the Introduction. The males with mosaicism appear to fulfill the clinical criteria for classic RTT. The other males have features ranging from neonatal encephalopathy to RTT male encephalopathy to moderate/severe developmental delay. While some have features similar in part to those in females with RTT, they do not meet the defined [55] diagnostic criteria for typical Rett syndrome.

## Discussion

Following the report from the US Natural History Study of 30 males with variants [8], subsequent evaluation of a second and larger group of males with variants has now provided information indicating whether these variants were inherited or . Further, between the two groups, the significant number of males with mosaicism (11/59 or 18.6%) contrasts sharply with the US NHS group (2 or 6.7%). Of interest, the historical reports of 18 of 88 (20.5%) males with classic RTT is remarkably similar. Many males in the second group were not able to participate in the NHS due to distance from an enrolling site including males who were too medically fragile to travel or were from other countries [12 (21.4%)]. Twenty-seven of these males are deceased, many due to the early onset neonatal encephalopathy form, the most severe phenotype seen in males.

The parents of males who are still living have expressed great interest in being involved with future or on-going trials including gene replacement therapy. It is critical that accurate natural history data be obtained from this group to gain a greater understanding of the phenotypic spectrum in males with loss-of-function variants. Such detailed natural history data may facilitate acceptance into ongoing clinical trials as well as gaining access to the recently approved Acadia therapy for RTT (Daybue) [56]. To obtain data on historical and current clinical features from this second group, specific on-line data forms together with qualitative interviews have been developed and are being completed following IRB approval (COMIRB CO-23-0075, 14MAR2023). This effort is strongly supported by parents and is being pursued by the current group of investigators. It is critical that males with such variants have access to the range of therapeutic possibilities already available to females with RTT.

## Conclusion

Expanding the study of males with phenotypes should provide data to caregivers, physicians, and researchers on the historical and clinical features of these males, improve overall care, and develop the knowledge for advancing treatments including approved pharmaceutical products, pharmacologic agents involved in clinical studies and gene replacement strategies. These males require equal access to such therapies and should be strongly considered for ongoing and future clinical trials.
