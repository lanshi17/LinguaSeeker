# Clinical phenotype and genetic analysis of one child with Rett syndrome caused by MECP2 gene variant

ZHONG Shaojun, LI Chengyan, WANG You, RONG Shiwen, LIU Ling, LIN Yongwen, AO Dang

Children's Medical Center, Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University, Zhanjiang 524000, Guangdong Province, China

DOI: 10.20047/j.issn1673-7210.2024.05.45

## Abstract

This study investigates the clinical phenotype and genetic etiology of Rett syndrome caused by MECP2 gene variation, and analyzes the genotype and clinical phenotype of a female child with developmental delay who attended the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University and was ultimately diagnosed with Rett syndrome. The child had infantile onset with developmental delay, hand stereotypies, seizures, loss of skills and language, intellectual disability, and abnormal gait; physical examination showed a small head circumference and low muscle tone in the limbs. Examination revealed an abnormal video EEG, and genetic testing identified a de novo heterozygous MECP2 missense variant, c.710C>G (p.Pro237Arg). The child was diagnosed with classic Rett syndrome. Treatment with valproate and lamotrigine for seizures and physical-stimulation interventions such as deep brain stimulation and transcranial magnetic stimulation can alleviate the neuronal pathological phenotype of this Rett syndrome patient. Genetic testing is the most distinctive diagnostic basis for Rett syndrome; early genetic diagnosis and treatment can improve prognosis and provide an important reference for genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis.

Keywords: MECP2 gene; Rett syndrome; epilepsy; developmental delay

## Introduction

Rett syndrome (OMIM #312750) is an X-linked dominant monogenic neurodevelopmental disorder. Its main clinical phenotypes include severe intellectual disability, language and learning impairment, repetitive stereotyped hand movements, and developmental regression, with seizures in most patients [1]. It is usually sporadic, occasionally familial, mostly seen in girls, with a prevalence of 1/15000 to 1/10000, and the vast majority are de novo variants [2-3]. Currently known pathogenic genes for Rett syndrome include methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 (MECP2), CDKL5, and FOXG1; 90%-95% of Rett syndrome is caused by MECP2 gene variants on chromosome Xq28 [4-6]. This study retrospectively analyzes one child with Rett syndrome caused by a MECP2 gene variant, to deepen clinicians' understanding of the pathogenesis, raise clinicians' attention, and provide a reference for clinical diagnosis, treatment, and genetic counseling. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University (PJ2021-097), and informed consent was signed by the child's guardian.

## 1 Case report

The patient is female, the first pregnancy and first delivery, born at full term by vaginal delivery, with a birth weight of 2.2 kg and a head circumference of 32 cm. She was small for gestational age at birth, with no family history. She could lift her head at 4 months, and growth and development were normal for a period thereafter; she could roll over at 5 months and sit independently at 6 months. However, growth and development subsequently stagnated. From August 2017 she frequently attended the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University for "developmental delay"; she could walk only at 20 months, with an unsteady gait. At 2 years 8 months, she lost the previously acquired speech ability, could only utter single syllables such as "ba/ma", could not understand the meaning of others' words, could not express her needs, had a relatively monotonous facial expression and low muscle tone, and gradually lost purposeful hand movements. At 3 years, she developed hand stereotypies, liked clasping her hands, accompanied by involuntary tremor of both upper limbs and a habit of sucking her hands. At 3 years 7 months, she developed seizures; seizures persisted despite treatment with valproate at 25 mg/(kg·d), and resolved only after lamotrigine was added, but she still likes the hand-clasping movement to this day. At the current age of 8 years 3 months, her head circumference is 48 cm, both upper limbs are free of involuntary tremor, she cannot understand the meaning of others' words, cannot express her needs, has a relatively monotonous facial expression, cannot care for herself, has low muscle tone, unsteady gait, and no scoliosis, muscle rigidity, or spasticity.

Cranial MRI in December 2017 showed no obvious abnormality (Figure 1A). The EEG in 2018 (at 4 years) showed a moderately abnormal pediatric EEG with abundant sharp waves and sharp-slow waves. The rechecked EEG on June 7, 2021 (at 6 years) remained abnormal: during wakefulness there were many medium-to-high amplitude sharp waves, scattered or clustered, in the left frontal, central, fronto-central, and midline central regions; during wakefulness and sleep there were persistent discharges of widespread medium-to-very-high amplitude spike-slow waves in the left hemisphere, widespread high-amplitude slow waves in the right hemisphere, and high-amplitude spikes in the midline region (Figure 1B). Liver and kidney function, myocardial enzyme profile, blood biochemistry, blood ammonia, blood lactate, blood amino acid and acylcarnitine profile, and urine organic acid analysis were all normal. On December 21, 2020, the Autism Behavior Checklist score was 65, and the Gesell assessment scale / Wechsler intelligence test score was 42. On March 5, 2021, after obtaining informed consent from the child's parents, 2 mL of venous blood was collected from the proband and her parents. Genomic DNA was extracted from whole peripheral blood cells and quality-checked. Whole-exome sequencing was performed on a high-throughput sequencer (MGI, model MGISEQ-2000RS) with an average depth of about 140x, 20x coverage of 99%, and 30x coverage of 98% (completed by Shenzhen Anjikanger). FastQC was used for quality assessment, the reference genome was GRCh37, and variant detection and interpretation were based on transcript NM_001110792.2. High-quality variants were annotated against frequency databases (1000 Genomes, ESP, gnomAD) and OMIM, ClinVar, and HGMD (Figure 2). Sanger sequencing was performed using the ABI BigDye Terminator v3.1 kit on an ABI3730XL automated sequencer for validation. Family trio whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous MECP2 missense variant in the proband, c.710C>G (p.Pro237Arg), confirmed by Sanger sequencing; the variant was de novo, and the parents did not carry this variant site. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, it was classified as a likely pathogenic variant [7], interpreted as PS2+PM2+PP3, and has previously been reported as pathogenic [8-9]. The child was finally diagnosed in March 2021.

## 2 Discussion

Rett syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder mainly affecting females, named after the pediatrician Andreas Rett who first described it [5,10]. In most cases, the disease is caused by loss-of-function variants in the X-linked MECP2 gene; the severity of the phenotype is related to X-chromosome inactivation, mosaicism, variant severity, genetic background, and environmental factors [11-13]. MECP2 mutations have been identified as the major genetic cause of Rett syndrome [14-15].

The MECP2 gene encodes methyl-CpG-binding protein 2, which controls chromatin structure and gene expression by binding methylated DNA; abnormally high or low expression levels, imbalance, and/or gene mutations lead to neurodevelopmental disorders and brain dysfunction [16-18]. The MeCP2 protein has five domains, including the N-terminal domain (NTD), methyl-DNA binding domain (MBD), transcription repression domain (TRD), the interdomain (ID) between the MBD and TRD, and the C-terminal domain (CTD) [19]. Among them, the MBD and TRD are considered the key functional domains. Ehrhart et al. [20] collected 10,968 individuals carrying MECP2 variants from public databases and summarized 863 pathogenic variants and 209 benign variants, finding that among pathogenic variants, nonsense variants account for 54%, missense variants 37%, and insertion and duplication variants 9% each. Most MECP2 missense variants are located in the MBD (68.3%) and TRD; 93.6% of missense variants occur in conserved regions. Benign variants are most common in the CTD (55.1%) and ID (28.1%).

The diagnostic criteria for classic Rett syndrome require four main criteria: loss of purposeful hand skills, loss of language, abnormal gait, and hand stereotypies [21-22]. Slowed head growth may be one of the earliest signs of Rett syndrome. The child in this report is female, with clinical phenotypes of hand stereotypies and loss of hand skills, loss of language, and abnormal gait, so classic Rett syndrome was diagnosed. Many symptoms of Rett syndrome lack specificity, and the complex neurological and psychiatric symptoms are difficult to distinguish from other disorders such as autism. Slowed head growth may be one of the earliest signs of Rett syndrome. Gene mutation may be the most distinctive factor of Rett syndrome; this child carries a de novo MECP2 variant at a site located in a hotspot region of previously reported pathogenic variants, rated pathogenic or likely pathogenic by ACMG, and the MECP2 gene variant is considered the causative gene in this child.

Current treatment for Rett syndrome mainly includes two approaches: one targets genetic and molecular pathology by repairing the mutated MECP2 or regulating downstream molecular target-related signaling pathways, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [20,23-24]. Expression levels are reduced in MECP2-deficient male mice; when expression is restored to a certain level, symptoms and lifespan can be reversed, indicating that therapy targeting the MECP2-BDNF axis in Rett syndrome can alleviate some symptoms and is a potential treatment option. The other approach uses physical-stimulation interventions such as deep brain stimulation and transcranial magnetic stimulation to alleviate pathological symptoms [25-26]. Applying the above approaches can improve the neuronal pathological phenotype of Rett syndrome, improve neuronal growth and development, or repair damaged neurites and synapses; in-depth analysis of the pathogenic mechanism of MECP2 variants will help optimize treatment combinations. Trofinetide is currently in phase III clinical trials, suggesting it improves the core symptoms of Rett syndrome [27-28].

Some clinical manifestations of children with Rett syndrome appear only at a certain age; therefore, diagnosis of young children is more difficult than older children, and genetic testing and mutation spectrum are essential for a definitive diagnosis. Early genetic diagnosis and treatment can improve prognosis and provide an important reference for genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis.
