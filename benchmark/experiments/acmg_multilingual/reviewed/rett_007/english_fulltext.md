# Gene mutation analysis of 5 children with Rett syndrome-like phenotype

LIU Wenjing, JIANG Xiufang, YOU Yuhui, QIN Zhiqiang, HAN Qijun, YUAN Zhaohong

(Department of Child Rehabilitation, Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University, Jining, Shandong 272029, China)

Corresponding author: 13562707506@126.com

DOI: 10.13404/j.cnki.cjbhh.2023.04.008

## Abstract

Objective To investigate the clinical characteristics and genetic results of 5 children with Rett syndrome (RTT)-like phenotype. Methods The clinical data of 5 children with RTT-like phenotype were collected, and the genetic analysis of the children and their parents was carried out by using whole-exome sequencing technology and copy-number variation detection to find pathogenic variants. Results According to the clinical features and gene mutation analysis results, 4 cases were diagnosed as RTT and 1 case was diagnosed as MECP2 duplication syndrome (MDS). Conclusion There may be multiple forms of gene mutation in hereditary diseases with an RTT-like phenotype, and gene diagnosis should be taken as an important auxiliary diagnostic criterion.

Keywords: Rett syndrome; MECP2 duplication syndrome; MECP2 gene

## Introduction

Rett syndrome (RTT) is a rare X-linked neurodevelopmental disorder that mainly affects females; more than 95% of patients carry a de novo mutation in the methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 (MECP2) gene [1]. Many hereditary diseases overlap with RTT and present an RTT-like phenotype; in the past five years more than 80 related genes have been detected, and some of them have been identified as the cause of the RTT-like phenotype [2]. In patients with overlapping phenotypes, the pathogenicity of different genes poses a great challenge to clinical diagnosis. This article reports 5 children with an RTT-like phenotype, discusses their clinical phenotypes and molecular genetic characteristics, and provides a reference for improving clinicians' diagnostic level.

## 1 Materials and methods

### 1.1 Study subjects

Clinical data were collected from 5 children with an RTT-like phenotype admitted to the Department of Child Rehabilitation of the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University from 2017 to 2020. All were female, aged from 1 year 9 months to 2 years 2 months. This project was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University (2022-11-C011), and informed consent was signed by the parents.

Case 1: female, 2 years 1 month old, presented because she "still cannot say 'baba' or 'mama'". Development before 6 months of age showed no obvious abnormality. At present she has a poor response to her name, no meaningful vocalization, cannot use sign language, can walk, likes to spin in circles and watch advertisements, does not like to play with other children, and has hand-wringing, hand-mouthing, shouting, and teeth-grinding behaviors. Hand function has regressed, with loss of thumb-index pincer grasp. Muscle tone in both lower limbs is low. Past history: unilateral abnormal eye movement, diagnosed as "epilepsy", well controlled with antiepileptic drugs. Auxiliary examinations: cranial MRI showed no abnormality; video EEG was abnormal. Birth history: bluish skin at birth, unclear postnatal hypoxia history, not hospitalized. No family history.

Case 2: female, 2 years 2 months old, presented because she "still cannot stand alone". Overall development has regressed. At present she has poor eye contact, poor response to her name, no meaningful vocalization, no intention to grasp objects, cannot stand or walk alone, and cannot identify common people and objects. She likes to suck her hands, pat the bed, pat her head, and pat her buttocks. She has involuntary blinking and head-raising movements that resolve within seconds. Frequent night waking. Head circumference: 45 cm. Auxiliary examinations: cranial MRI and blood/urine metabolic screening showed no obvious abnormality; video EEG suggested epileptiform discharges. Obstetric history: umbilical cord around the neck during pregnancy; no family history.

Case 3: female, 1 year 9 months old, presented because she "still cannot stand alone". At present the child has a poor response to her name, no meaningful vocalization, cannot identify common people and objects, cannot crawl or stand alone, and cannot transfer toys between hands. The hands show purposeless clapping and hand-clasping stereotyped movements, and the mouth shows chewing and teeth-grinding movements. She previously could bring objects to her mouth and use simple sign language; hand function is now lost. Head circumference 44.5 cm, 3rd percentile. Auxiliary examinations: neuropsychological development assessment for children aged 0-6 years showed a developmental quotient of 44.2; cranial MRI and blood/urine metabolic screening showed no obvious abnormality; video EEG suggested epileptiform discharges. No abnormal birth or family history.

Case 4: female, 2 years 2 months old, presented because she "still cannot say 'baba' or 'mama'". Developmental milestones were slightly delayed before 1 year of age, and development regressed after 1 year of age, with decreased dexterity of thumb-index pinch, no response to her name, poor execution of commands, no interest in toys, and liking to wring her hands, suck her hands, and self-injurious behaviors such as shouting, head-banging, and biting her arms. Sleep rhythm is disturbed. She could walk at 1 year 5 months. Auxiliary examinations: neuropsychological development assessment for children aged 0-6 years showed a developmental quotient of 38.4; video EEG suggested epileptiform discharges; cranial MRI considered possible delayed myelination. No abnormal birth or family history.

Case 5: female, 1 year 9 months old, presented because she "still has no meaningful vocalization". Before 6 months of age development was acceptable and she could roll over; up to now she cannot crawl or stand with support, has poor interaction, can complete simple action imitation, and cannot understand commands. Head circumference 47 cm. Muscle tone is low. Auxiliary examinations: blood/urine metabolic screening showed no obvious abnormality; cranial MRI showed brain dysplasia. No abnormal birth or family history.

### 1.2 Genetic mutation detection

Peripheral blood (2 mL) was collected from the children and their parents and sent to Beijing Kangxu Medical Laboratory for whole-exome sequencing and copy-number variation detection (qPCR-SYBR Green I dye method).

## 2 Results

### 2.1 Genetic mutation analysis

Whole-exome detection was performed on the 5 children and their parents. The results are as follows: cases 1-4 carried MECP2 gene mutations in the Xq28 region (see Table 1), and case 5 was found by copy-number variation verification to carry a duplication in the Xq28 region with a fragment size of 0.299 Mb. No mutations were detected in the parents of any of the children (results shown in Figures 1-5).

### 2.2 Diagnosis

According to the 2010 revised RTT diagnostic criteria, combined with the clinical features and genetic mutation analysis of the children (see Table 2), cases 1-4 were diagnosed as classic RTT. Case 5 had clinical features overlapping with RTT, but by genetic mutation analysis was diagnosed as MECP2 duplication syndrome (methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 duplication syndrome, MDS). Female MDS patients have highly variable clinical features and often lack the classic features of male patients (see Table 3).

## 3 Discussion

RTT is a common hereditary disease causing intellectual disability in females, with an incidence of about 1/100,000, clinically divided into classic and atypical types. Its diagnosis is based on clinical criteria. The classic RTT clinical feature is a relatively normal development process at 6-18 months, followed by a rapid regression period manifesting as loss of language and motor skills, hand stereotypies, head growth deceleration, epilepsy, ataxia, breathing abnormalities, sleep disturbance, teeth grinding, chewing difficulty, and autistic-like symptoms, followed by a plateau period and late-stage motor degeneration [4].

In this study, 4 RTT children were detected with MECP2 gene coding-region mutations, including missense, nonsense, and frameshift mutations, all with pathogenicity reported in the literature. MECP2 gene coding-region mutations cause 96% of classic RTT cases; 555 RTT-related MECP2 mutations have been identified in the Human Gene Mutation Database [5]. These mutations lead to loss of MeCP2 function. MeCP2 plays an important role in maintaining neuronal maturation, activity, and plasticity. A small proportion of atypical RTT may be associated with CDKL5 and FOXG1 gene mutations [6].

Studies of the correlation between MECP2 gene mutations and phenotype in RTT children exist both domestically and internationally [7], including the effects of mutation type and mutation position on patients, but the conclusions are inconsistent, possibly due to different scoring criteria, sample size differences, and age factors. However, compared with other mutation groups (R133C, R294X, R306X and C-terminal truncation), the severity of some mutations (early truncating mutations such as R168X, R255X and R270X, large deletions, and specific point mutations such as R106W) is increased [8]. The sample size of this study is small, and no relevant conclusion can be drawn.

Case 5 has clinical features partially overlapping with RTT. Copy-number variation verification found a duplication in the Xq28 region with a size of 0.299 Mb, containing the FLNA, IRAK1, OPN1MW2, TKTL1, OPN1LW, OPN1MW3, TEX28, OPN1MW, MIR718, and MECP2 genes. Previously reported Xq28 duplications differ in size and position, but most are intrachromosomal duplications ranging from 0.3 to 2.3 Mb, among which the MECP2 gene is the key dosage-sensitive gene causing the severe phenotype. Loss of MECP2 gene function is associated with RTT, but duplication of the locus causes another distinct but related neurodevelopmental disorder, MDS, which mainly affects males; nearly 200 MDS cases have been reported worldwide [9]. These patients present with hypotonia, severe psychomotor retardation, limited or absent language, autistic features, and recurrent respiratory infections. Females are usually unaffected or have only a very mild clinical phenotype due to highly skewed X-chromosome inactivation and preferential inactivation of the X chromosome carrying the duplicated variant. However, Reardon et al. [10] considered that when the X-inactivation ratio is below 70:30, female patients may present corresponding clinical manifestations.

The clinical features of female MDS patients are highly variable, including non-specific mild-to-moderate intellectual disability combined with different symptoms (autistic features, recurrent infections in early childhood, constipation, and late-onset neurological features). This case also differs from the classic male clinical features, possibly due to the mutation type, gene content, X-inactivation pattern, and other genetic and environmental factors. Novara et al. [11] reported three female MDS patients, each with mild-to-moderate cognitive impairment accompanied by learning difficulties and speech delay, without stereotyped repetitive behaviors. Two of the cases had mild facial dysmorphic features: broad nasal bridge, prognathism, and mild hypertelorism. EEG and cranial MRI showed no specific findings. Scott et al. [3] reported a 25-year-old female with difficult-to-control epilepsy, intellectual disability, and a happy disposition without developmental regression; genetic mutation analysis found a duplication in the q arm of the X chromosome involving the genes IRAK1, MECP2, OPN1LW, OPN1MW, OPN1MW2, TEX28, and TKTL1. This case shares 6 duplicated gene loci with the present case but has a clearly different phenotype, possibly related to differential gene expression in neurons. Bijlsma et al. [12-13] reported one female MDS case with three copies who presented with severe intellectual disability and seizures but no other symptoms, more severe than two-copy patients, indicating that MECP2 gene copy number correlates with clinical symptoms.

MECP2 is considered a key factor in neurophenotypes; however, other genes in the region may regulate each other and jointly participate in pathogenesis. Descriptions of other genes in this region: IRAK1 participates in cellular immunity, and immune deficiency has been reported in male MDS patients; OPN1LW and OPN1MW are related to color vision and associated with night blindness; FLNA is related to intestinal pseudo-obstruction and congenital megacolon [9]. The mutation origin in patients can be de novo, with both paternal and maternal origin reported [14].

RTT and MDS are developmental encephalopathies with both common and distinct features. Cutri-French et al. [15] conducted a cross-sectional comparison of the clinical features of RTT-like related disorders and found that MDS scored lower on clinical severity, significantly different from classic RTT. The median age of developmental regression in classic RTT is 18 months, while in MDS it is 36 months; although the difference is not significant, MDS has a tendency toward later regression. The median age of epilepsy onset in MDS is older (64 months) with the lowest severity score, while in classic RTT patients the median age of onset is 47.5 months. In classic RTT, the most common seizure types are generalized seizures (26.9%), tonic-clonic seizures (15.1%), and complex partial seizures (13.6%). In MDS, generalized seizures (22.5%), tonic-clonic seizures (22.5%), and atonic seizures (14.3%) are the most commonly reported. In MDS, small head circumference correlates with disease severity and early epilepsy onset. In addition, MDS shows more severe autistic features, later-onset refractory seizures, and recurrent respiratory infections. This MDS case presented with obvious autistic features at the time of consultation and had not yet developed seizures, possibly related to young age and the late-onset nature of MDS epilepsy. Although the two diseases share similarities, the significant developmental, clinical, and genetic differences warrant considering them as distinct disorders.

In summary, hereditary diseases with an RTT-like phenotype may involve multiple forms of gene mutation. A clinical diagnosis may be misleading, and gene diagnosis should be taken as an important auxiliary diagnostic criterion, shifting the clinical definition of hereditary diseases toward a molecular or biological definition. Although such diseases share similarities, the developmental, clinical, and genetic differences warrant considering them as distinct disorders. Based on different diagnostic results, we should provide more precise individualized treatment plans and genetic counseling.
