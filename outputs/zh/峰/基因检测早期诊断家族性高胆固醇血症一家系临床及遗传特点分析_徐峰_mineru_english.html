[Abstract] Objective To achieve early diagnosis of a family with familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) through genetic testing, aiming to enhance clinicians' ability to recognize and diagnose FH and to provide early intervention and treatment. Methods Clinical data were collected from a family with hypercholesterolemia treated in the Department of Neurology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, and genetic sequencing analysis was performed. Results Seven family members were found to have hypercholesterolemia and elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), with ages ranging from 8 to 59 years. Patient I5 underwent coronary artery stent implantation due to recurrent angina and detected coronary artery stenosis. Genetic testing revealed that all patients carried a heterozygous mutation at the LDLR gene Exon13 c.1879G>A (p.A627T) site, leading to a final diagnosis of FH. Conclusion FH has an insidious onset with no obvious clinical symptoms in the early stages, making it highly prone to missed diagnosis. Genetic testing for genetic screening of FH patients and their relatives not only aids in early diagnosis but also enables personalized treatment based on genotype, which is particularly significant for preventive treatment in pediatric patients.

Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH) is a common autosomal dominant genetic disorder, clinically characterized by elevated plasma low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), peripheral tissue xanthomas, and corneal arcus. In China, FH patients have a five-fold increased risk of premature coronary artery disease (CAD) compared to non-FH individuals[1]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the global overall prevalence of FH is approximately 1/500, with a homozygous FH (HoFH) prevalence of about 1/1,000,000[2]. However, the diagnosis rate for FH patients is less than 1% in most countries and regions[3]. FH is a severe life-threatening disease, categorized into heterozygous (HeFH) and homozygous (HoFH) forms based on the mutation type. HoFH patients typically die before the age of 30. HeFH patients experience a series of clinical events, with about 7.1% of premature myocardial infarction (MI) cases attributed to FH[1]. FH has a high incidence and complication risk, but patients often lack obvious clinical features in the early stages. Most are diagnosed during routine check-ups when abnormal lipid levels are detected, by which time LDL-C may have been elevated for years, increasing the risk of atherosclerosis, cardiovascular disease, and mortality[4]. European[5] and American[6] FH guidelines recommend early diagnosis of FH patients to enable LDL-C-lowering treatment early in life and improve prognosis. This study reports the early diagnosis of a familial hypercholesterolemia pedigree through genetic testing, aiming to assist clinicians in early identification, diagnosis, and intervention for familial hypercholesterolemia.

1.2.1 Clinical Data Collection

Detailed records of routine physical examinations and tests for blood lipids, ECG, cardiac color Doppler ultrasound, and carotid artery color Doppler ultrasound were obtained for the proband and family members. Detailed physical examinations were conducted on the proband (II6), the proband's second elder brother (II5), the proband's elder son (III13), younger son (III14), granddaughter (IV16), grandsons (IV17 and V20), and other family members. A pedigree chart was constructed based on the consanguineous relationships of the proband and their family.

1.2.2 Peripheral Blood Genomic DNA Extraction and Detection

After obtaining informed consent, 5 ml of peripheral venous blood was collected from the proband and their family members using EDTA anticoagulation. DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood of the proband and family members using the "Tiangen T-Guide M16" automated nucleic acid extraction instrument. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a micro-spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific NanoDrop 2000). All DNA samples were ensured to have a concentration >100 ng/μl, with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0.

1.2.3 Genetic Sequencing, PCR Amplification, and Sequencing

Whole-exome sequencing was performed on the proband, following methods described in a previous article [7]. Primers were designed using SnapGene based on the LDLR gene (NM_00527.4) sequences from the Ensemble database, and primer specificity was verified using the Primer designing tool database. Exon 13 of the LDLR gene was amplified separately with the following primers: Forward (F): TGGCCTGTGTCTCATCCCAGTGTTTAACGG, Reverse (R): CCCATTTGACAGATGAGCAGAGAGAGGCTC. The PCR reaction system was 25 μl, consisting of 9 μl PCR water, 12.5 μl KOD MIX ONE (Toyobo (Shanghai) Biotech Co., Ltd.), 0.75 μl each of forward and reverse primers, and 2 μl of DNA (>100 ng/μl, A260/A280 between 1.8 and 2.0). PCR amplification conditions: pre-denaturation at 95°C for 1 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing at 55°C, and extension at 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72°C.

2.2 Sanger Sequencing Results

Whole-exome sequencing of the proband II6 revealed a heterozygous variant c.1879G>A (p.A627T) in exon 13 of the LDLR gene. Heterozygous mutations at this site were also found in the proband's second elder brother (II5), eldest son (III13), youngest son (III14), granddaughter (IV16), grandson (IV17), and grandson (IV20), all of whom exhibited the clinical phenotype of hypercholesterolemia. In contrast, other family members with normal lipid profiles had the wild-type allele at this site. Therefore, the findings are consistent with co-segregation within the pedigree (Figure 3), leading to a diagnosis of familial hypercholesterolemia.

The c.1879G>A (p.A627T) variant is a missense mutation, resulting in the substitution of alanine at codon 627 with threonine. This alanine residue is highly conserved and located within the epidermal growth factor precursor homology domain of the LDLR protein. This variation reduces the binding capacity of the LDLR protein to LDL-C, thereby affecting the transport of LDL-C into hepatocytes and leading to elevated serum LDL-C levels. Software predictions for this site are as follows: CADD score 27.2, SIFT (Damaging), Polyphen2-HVAR (Probably Damaging), MutationTaster (Disease Causing). It is recorded in OMIM under code 252101. This mutation site c.1879G>A (p.A627T) is confirmed as a pathogenic mutation site for Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH).

LDLR gene mutations are the most common mutation type in clinically diagnosed FH patients, with approximately 20% to 35% being non-LDLR gene mutations [10]. The LDLR gene is located on chromosome 19p13.2, consisting of 18 exons and 17 introns, and contains five distinct functional domains. Mutations in the exons can cause varying degrees of impairment to the corresponding functional domains of the LDLR protein, leading to its dysfunction. To date, over 3,000 gene mutations have been detected in FH patients, including deletions, insertions, nonsense mutations, missense mutations, and copy number variations [11-14]. The LDLR protein is widely distributed in the liver, arterial wall smooth muscle, vascular endothelial cells, and leukocytes. It specifically binds to and internalizes lipoproteins containing apoB100 or apoE through receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby clearing LDL-C from the blood [10]. Mutations in the LDLR gene cause structural and functional abnormalities of the LDLR protein on the cell membrane surface, impairing the effective metabolism of LDL-C in the body. This leads to excessive accumulation of LDL-C in the blood or other tissues due to clearance障碍, resulting in hypercholesterolemia [12, 15, 16].

This article reports an FH family. The proband sought medical attention due to elevated LDL-C. Subsequent lipid screening and genetic screening of family members revealed that members with hypercholesterolemia all carried a heterozygous LDLR gene mutation, leading to a diagnosis of Heterozygous FH (HeFH). The clinical phenotypes within this family varied significantly. The proband's second elder brother (II5) experienced recurrent angina symptoms. Coronary angiography revealed multiple coronary artery stenoses, and he underwent coronary artery stent implantation. However, other HeFH patients, aside from laboratory findings of elevated LDL-C and hypercholesterolemia, showed no other obvious clinical signs. This poses significant difficulties for clinical diagnosis, making misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis highly likely. Based on the lipid profiles of children in the family carrying the heterozygous mutation, it is speculated that the patient may have had hyperlipidemia since childhood. Prolonged exposure to hyperlipidemia likely contributed to the development of severe cardiovascular disease [17]. Through the proband's laboratory tests, family history analysis, and further genetic testing, we diagnosed the disease in family members within a short period. Genetic testing analysis plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and treatment of this disease, especially for the diagnosis and preventive treatment of pediatric patients. Currently, there is no unified consensus on the screening and diagnosis of FH domestically and internationally, but family screening is generally considered a cost-effective strategy [18]. Furthermore, some individuals, although carrying pathogenic mutations, exhibit low phenotypic penetrance. For example, their LDL-C levels may be elevated but do not meet the diagnostic criteria for FH [19]. The detection rate of clinical screening using genetic testing technology is significantly higher compared to traditional clinical examination and diagnosis [20].

Genetic testing technology is not only valuable for the early diagnosis of FH but also crucial for formulating treatment plans for HeFH or Homozygous FH (HoFH) patients. Treatment for FH primarily involves lifestyle modifications and dietary control, along with pharmacological therapy, lipoprotein apheresis, and liver transplantation [21]. Once FH is diagnosed, lifestyle changes should be initiated immediately. HeFH patients can effectively lower LDL-C concentrations with statin therapy, thereby reducing cardiovascular risk in most patients. However, HoFH patients respond poorly to statin therapy because these patients have little or no upregulation of LDL receptors. They often require lipoprotein apheresis and liver transplantation.

Among the treatment options[2I]. In this study's pedigree, the diagnosis of HeFH was confirmed by genetic testing. The proband's second elder brother (Ⅱ5) had already developed severe complications of hyperlipidemia, with coronary artery stenosis lesions and underwent stent implantation. The proband (Ⅱ6), as well as the proband's sons (Ⅲ13 and Ⅲ14), also already had complications of carotid atherosclerotic plaques, posing a risk of stroke or vascular events at any time. The proband's blood lipids could be satisfactorily reduced with oral atorvastatin tablets, but after self-discontinuation, relying solely on diet and exercise, blood cholesterol levels rose again. Therefore, continued use of oral atorvastatin is necessary, along with monitoring of blood lipids and liver function. For asymptomatic HeFH children, the first step is to adjust diet and exercise; if necessary, atorvastatin tablets can be added, with monitoring of blood lipids and liver function, to minimize complications caused by hyperlipidemia.

In summary, FH has an insidious onset and lacks other obvious clinical symptoms in the early stages, making it highly prone to missed diagnosis. Genetic testing technology for genetic screening of FH patients and their relatives, especially children, not only plays a crucial role in early diagnosis but also allows for personalized treatment based on the patient's genotype. After diagnosis through genetic testing, early intervention, particularly for young children, can avoid long-term exposure to hyperlipidemia and the occurrence of severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases and other vascular events.
