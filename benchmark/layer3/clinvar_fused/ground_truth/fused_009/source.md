# Prevalence of recurrent and non-recurrent BRCA1 and BRCA2 pathogenic variants in Polish breast cancer patients: insights from a regional genetic testing cohort from Lower Silesia

## Abstract

Background The prevalence and spectrum of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants (PVs/LPVs) in BRCA1/2 vary across populations, necessitating region-specific analyses to identify recurrent and non-recurrent variants and to evaluate the effectiveness of current diagnostic strategies. Published experience remains dominated by North American and West-European or Nordic cohorts. To address this gap, we present the first comprehensive analysis of BRCA testing outcomes from Central Europe. Methods A next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis of BRCA1/2 was performed in a regional cohort of 1,021 consecutive breast cancer patients from Lower Silesia (Poland), treated between March 2024 and April 2025. Clinical and pathological characteristics were compared between PV/LPV carriers and non-carriers. Results Nineteen distinct PVs/LPVs (11 in BRCA1 and 8 in BRCA2 ) were identified in 30 patients (2.94%; 95% CI: 2.07%–4.16%, Wilson). The most frequent variant was BRCA1 c.5266dup (30% of all variants), followed by c.181T > G (10%). Most variants (56.7%) were unique. No copy number variants were detected. Carriers were significantly younger at diagnosis (median 47 vs. 66 years; p < 0.0001) and exhibited a distinct tumor phenotype, including a markedly higher prevalence of triple-negative breast cancer (43.3% vs. 8.5%; OR = 8.24; p < 0.0001), lower ER/PR expression, higher Ki-67, larger tumor size, and more advanced stage at diagnosis (all p < 0.05). Conclusions The prevalence of BRCA1/2 PVs/LPVs in this unselected Polish regional cohort was lower than previously reported, with a high proportion of non-recurrent variants. This Central European cohort demonstrates results consistent with international benchmarks. Our findings suggest limited effectiveness of founder mutation–based testing strategies and support the implementation of comprehensive NGS-based diagnostics in routine clinical practice to improve detection of clinically relevant variants and guide treatment decisions.


## Introduction

Genetic tests in breast cancer (BC) patients are increasingly prevalent in oncology, both because of the high incidence of breast cancer (general population risk is 12%) and the potential for effective preventative care in carriers of causative pathogenic (PVs) and likely pathogenic variants (LPVs) [ 1 ]. Moreover the introduction of effective PARP inhibitor therapy in ovarian cancer a decade ago, and subsequently in breast, pancreatic and prostate cancer, has additionally led to an increase in the number of genetic tests being performed in this area. This broader clinical use has increased the demand for genetic testing, including analysis of somatic variants in tumour DNA (tDNA) from tissue or in circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) from liquid biopsy, as well as germline variants in genomic DNA (gDNA) extracted from blood or swabs [ 2 – 5 ].

Molecular profiling in BC is also facilitated by the fact that the PVs and LPVs that are driver mutations in breast, ovarian, prostate and pancreatic cancers and determine hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOCS), tend to occur mostly in several genes: BRCA1 , BRCA2 , PALB2 , ATM , PTEN , RAD51C , RAD51D and TP53 [ 5 ]. It is estimated that a disruption in one of these genes is responsible for 5–10% of breast cancers and about 15–20% of ovarian cancers. Furthermore, 50% of clinically significant variants in breast cancer and 80% in ovarian cancer are BRCA1/2 variants [ 4 , 6 – 8 ].

Large-scale meta-analyses have demonstrated that the prevalence of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants in HBOC-related genes varies by population (geographical location, ethnicity) and should be investigated locally to identify any distinctive recurrent variants, if they exist. Copy number variants (CNVs) (exons and/or whole gene deletions/duplications) and variants in non-coding regions should also be considered, as they may account for 5–10% of all alterations [ 8 ].

Among BRCA1 and BRCA2 pathogenic recurrent variants observed in some populations, including Central and Eastern European populations, the BRCA1 c.5266dup p.(Gln1756fs), variant (according to the Breast Cancer Information Core (BIC): c.5382insC) characteristic of the Ashkenazi Jewish population, is prevalent. Apart from BRCA1 c.5266dup, the most common pathogenic variants reported in Central and Eastern Europe (especially Slavic) populations are also: c.181T > G (BIC: 300T > A) p.(Cys61Gly), c.4035del (BIC: 4153delA) p.(Glu1346fs), c.68_69del (BIC: 185delAG) p.(Glu23fs). The fraction of the most common BRCA1/2 PVs in the overall pool of BRCA1/2 PVs varies by population [ 9 – 12 ]. It is essential to emphasize that, although studies on the frequency of specific PVs and LPVs have been conducted for years before the NGS era, these estimates may be flawed, with inflated percentages for the most common variants. Massive parallel sequencing of the whole BRCA1/2 coding sequence in search of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indel variants, often together with copy number variants (CNV), is now widely used as a fundamental technique for studying selected genes, allowing for a reasonable estimate of the frequency and type of individual alterations in both patients with BRCA1/2 -dependent cancers and the general population.

We used next-generation sequencing (NGS) to assess the prevalence and range of BRCA1/2 pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants in 1,351 breast cancer patients from Lower Silesia District in Poland. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first complete BRCA1/2 analysis from Central Europe, as previous findings have primarily been from North American, Western European, and Nordic cohorts.

## Materials and methods

The study was performed using whole peripheral blood. The extraction of genetic material and its quality assessment were previously described by Doraczynska-Kowalik et al. [ 2 ].

The Devyser BRCA kit (Devyser AB, Sweden) was used to sequence the BRCA1 and BRCA2 exons and exon-intron boundaries, and to analyse exon copy number. Data analysis and confirmation of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants were previously described by Doraczynska-Kowalik et al. [ 2 ].

Samples with unsuccessful NGS tests for CNVs were subsequently examined using the MLPA with SALSA MLPA Probemix P002 BRCA1 and SALSA MLPA Probemix P045 BRCA2/CHEK2 (MRC-Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. No CNVs were found in the study group. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG-AMP) systems were used for variant classification [ 14 ].

Reporting of this cohort study follows the STROBE guidelines [ 15 ].

### Statistical analysis

The prevalence of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants (PVs/LPVs) in g BRCA1/2 is reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated by the Wilson score method and the Clopper–Pearson exact method. Patients without a finalized NGS result (NGS = ‘no data’; n = 330) were excluded from the denominator ( N = 1,021 analyzed). Clinical and pathological characteristics were compared between PV/LPV carriers ( n = 30) and non-carriers ( n = 991) using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables (age, tumor size, Ki-67, ER and PR expression) and Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables (molecular subtype, HER2 status, disease stage). Normality within subgroups was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Effect size for the Mann–Whitney U test was computed as r = 1 − 2U/(n₁·n₂). All tests were two-tailed; α = 0.05. Analyses were performed using Python 3 (SciPy 1.x, pandas 2.x).

## Results

A total of nineteen distinct PV/LPVs (11 in BRCA1 and 8 in BRCA2 ) were identified in 30 of 1,021 BC patients. The estimated prevalence was 2.94%, with a 95% confidence interval of 2.07%–4.16% (Wilson score) and 1.99%–4.17% (Clopper–Pearson) (Table 2 ). BRCA1 c.5266dup was the most common PV in the Lower Silesian population, which accounted for 42.86% (9/21) of all BRCA1 variants and 30% (9/30) of all BRCA1/2 variants. The c.181T > G (C61G) was the second most prevalent BRCA1 PV, accounting for 10% (3/30) of the cases. One non-recurrent PV was identified in two patients: c.658_659del in BRCA2 . The other variants occurred individually. There were no CNVs observed. A significant difference in molecular subtype distribution was observed between PV/LPV carriers and non-carriers (χ² = 41.57, p < 0.0001). In particular, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) was markedly enriched among carriers (43.3% vs. 8.5%), corresponding to an over eightfold increased odds (OR = 8.24, 95% CI: 3.87–17.55; p < 0.0001).

PV/LPV carriers were characterized by a markedly distinct tumor biology compared to non-carriers. Hormone receptor expression was significantly lower in carriers, with median ER and PR levels of 1% and 0%, respectively, compared to 100% and 70% in non-carriers (both p < 0.0001). Proliferative activity was higher in carriers, as reflected by an increased median Ki-67 index (30% vs. 20%, p < 0.001). Tumors in carriers were also significantly larger at diagnosis (median 27.5 mm vs. 18.0 mm, p = 0.013) and more frequently presented at advanced stages (stage IIIA–IV: 20.0% vs. ~ 10.0%, p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed in HER2 positivity between the groups ( p = 0.754).

## Discussion

The relatively low genetic heterogeneity and the predominant importance of the two genes BRCA1 and BRCA2 in both sporadic and hereditary BRCA -related cancers make genetic diagnostics in this area common and well-planned in many countries [ 6 , 17 – 19 ]. The development of diagnostic methods in BRCA- related cancers is currently promoted either by the high incidence of cancer worldwide or the high prevalence of breast cancer (BC) cases in the total number of cancers. In addition to testing oncology patients to determine personalised therapy and possible inherited predispositions, pre-symptomatic tests in healthy people with a positive family history of cancer are also used. Moreover, screening genetic tests in the general population are becoming increasingly popular [ 18 , 20 – 26 ]. Analyses of wide-scale genomic testing results (e.g., WES, RNAseq) conducted across broad, diverse populations of healthy individuals to assess hereditary cancer predispositions demonstrate that the prevalence of PVs is significant. According to Idumah et al., WGS studies of 414,830 individuals of various sexes, ages, races, and ethnicities found unique PVs/LPVs in 72 genes in 5.05% of them. Statistically significant differences in variant frequencies were detected among races, with no differences observed across gender or ethnicity; nevertheless, individual differences in frequencies were demonstrated for 12 genes between Hispanics and non-Hispanics. It was also found that the number of participants with an autosomal recessive hereditary condition was relatively high (8,932 participants, 2.15%), whereas mosaicism affected 0.1% of the subjects [ 27 ]. A further study of 67,692 women, half of whom had been diagnosed with BC, found that PVs in ATM , BRCA1 , BRCA2 , CHEK2 , and PALB2 were significantly associated with BC risk when considered together with the family history of cancer, and recognised epidemiological risk factors. The authors revealed that the lowest probability of developing BC before the age of 50 was 2.4% for women with no PVs and no family history of cancer. In comparison, the highest was 35.5% for women with PVs in PALB2 and a family history of cancer. At age 80, the risk was 11.1% and 70.5%, respectively [ 28 ]. Studies involving specific cancer patient populations, such as a study of 2,445 BC patients whose WGS data were collected from the 100,000 Genomes Project (100kGP), demonstrate that the prevalence of germline mutations with potential significance in personalised therapy is very high (26.8%) [ 29 ].

In many populations, there are pathogenic variants with a substantially higher frequency than the other reported clinically relevant variants and are called recurrent or founder variants (mutations). In some countries, including Poland, patients are offered, as a first diagnostic step, limited genetic testing for a few selected, most prevalent variants [ 30 – 33 ]. In the past, this approach was valid and successful. For instance, the first diagnostic simple tests for recurrent BRCA1 pathogenic variants were implemented in Poland more than 20 years ago [ 9 , 34 ]. Nowadays, with the availability of molecular diagnostic technologies and their low cost, there is no reason to limit diagnostics for cancer patients and their relatives, who should have rapid access to complete genetic diagnostics. This approach will result in fewer ‘cascade’ tests, faster results and the identification of previously unknown rare variants. Given the scarcity of geneticists in Poland, avoiding cascading testing and multiple genetic consultations appears to benefit both patients and the healthcare system [ 35 – 37 ]. Furthermore, the idea to implement NGS National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) screening in patients and their relatives, as well as in healthy people with a family history of cancer, is supported by the fact that more than 80% of people who have a germline pathogenic or potentially pathogenic variant detected were previously unaware that they may had one [ 35 ]. Other large-scale investigations have also revealed that whether individuals fulfil or do not fulfil the NCCN pedigree criteria, the proportion of probands found to have a mutation in BRCA1/2 is identical (49.95% and 50.05%, respectively) [ 18 , 19 , 38 ]. Moreover among patients with invasive breast cancer who developed before the age of 50, with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and/or bilateral breast cancer, that might be candidates for PARP inhibitors, 7.3% had a germline pathogenic variant in one of the breast cancer susceptibility gene ( BRCA1 , BRCA2 , PALB2 , ATM , BARD1 , BRIP1 , CDH1 , CHEK2 , MLH1 , MSH2 , MSH6 , PMS2 , PTEN , RAD51C , RAD51D , STK11 , and TP53 ), with 4.3% positive for BRCA1/BRCA2 and 1% positive for PALB2 [ 39 ]. The increasing use of NGS panels suggests that BRCA -related cancer patients who have been tested for BRCA1/2 but do not have PV/LPVs should be provided an extended test for other BC-related genes, as PV may be present in 2–5% of them [ 39 , 40 ]. In this context, comprehensive multigene panel testing may represent a more efficient diagnostic approach, particularly in oncology where timely diagnosis is critical, as it helps avoid delays associated with stepwise testing strategies while improving the detection of rare pathogenic variants beyond BRCA1/2 [ 41 , 42 ].

The marked enrichment of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) among PV/LPV carriers in our cohort (43.3% vs. 8.5% in non-carriers; OR = 8.24, 95% CI: 3.87–17.55; p < 0.0001) is consistent with the established biological profile of BRCA1 -associated tumors and reflects defects in homologous recombination pathway leading to a basal-like phenotype. This is further supported by significantly reduced hormone receptor expression (median ER 1% vs. 100%, PR 0% vs. 70%; both p < 0.0001) and higher proliferative activity (Ki-67: 30% vs. 20%, p < 0.001) in carriers. Clinically, carriers also presented with larger tumors (27.5 mm vs. 18.0 mm, p = 0.013) and more advanced disease (stage IIIA–IV: 20.0% vs. ~ 10.0%, p < 0.001), indicating a more aggressive course. This phenotype is of direct clinical relevance, as TNBC is associated with limited targeted treatment options but increased sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents, including platinum-based chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors. Consequently, the high prevalence of TNBC among carriers highlights the importance of timely genetic testing to guide therapeutic decision-making and supports broader genetic screening strategies, particularly in younger patients presenting with TNBC [ 41 , 43 – 47 ].

Our study on over 1,021 breast cancer Polish patients revealed that only two BRCA1 PVs: c.5266dup and c.181T > G (C61G) are recurrent. No c.68_69del (p.Glu23fs) (BIC: 185delAG) and c.3700_3704del (p.Val1234fs) (BIC: 3819del5), previously reported as frequent for the Polish population, were found, while c.4035del (BIC: 4153delA) was seen only once. The absence of BRCA1 PVs c.68_69del and c.3700_3704del, and the low frequency of the c.4035del in the study group, should be verified for other subpopulations in Poland, as their frequencies may be locally different. This study reflects a regional population of Lower Silesia District and may not fully represent the national genetic landscape.

Among all detected PV/LPVs, 56.7% were unique. This indicates that the currently used diagnostic PCR test for the most common BRCA1 PVs in Poland is not optimal, as it detects only 43.3% of all BRCA1 PVs, including SNVs and indel variants within the coding sequence and at exon-intron boundaries, and CNVs.

Our results suggest that it is reasonable to revise the set of recurrent BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants used in the Polish population, and the inclusion of the BRCA2 c.658_659del pathogenic variant should be considered. Moreover, an alternative, and potentially more optimal, approach would be to replace founder-mutation testing with comprehensive NGS-based analysis.

In our cohort, BRCA1 variants showed a non-random distribution, with a tendency to localize toward the C-terminal region, including truncating variants affecting or proximal to the BRCT domains. This is consistent with previous studies indicating that pathogenic BRCA1 variants cluster within key functional domains, particularly the RING and BRCT regions, while the large central exon 11 appears more tolerant to variation [ 48 – 51 ].

In contrast, BRCA2 variants were more evenly distributed along the protein, with a noticeable representation within the central region (exon 11, BRC repeats). This pattern aligns with prior reports showing that BRCA2 mutations are broadly dispersed but preferentially affect functionally relevant domains. No subtype-specific clustering was observed, supporting the greater heterogeneity of BRCA2 -associated mutation [ 50 – 53 ].

Although the sample size limits definitive conclusions, these findings are consistent with published mutation-mapping analyses which suggest that variant localization may partially reflect functional domain disruption and tumor phenotype. Therefore, mutation-mapping approaches may enhance the biological interpretation of sequencing data and support the clinical relevance of comprehensive NGS-based diagnostics.

In our study, CNV results were not obtained for 24.3% of samples, which is problematic in a diagnostic setting where turnaround time is critical. Despite sufficient quality metrics for SNV and indel detection, CNV analysis using NGS was not reliable in this subset, likely due to the higher technical requirements of CNV calling and variability in DNA quality. Interestingly, MLPA analysis performed on the same DNA samples yielded informative results, suggesting that this limitation may also be related to the method or specific NGS assay used, although no alternative NGS-based CNV method was available for comparison.

Our study reports a 2.94% prevalence of g BRCA1/2 mutations in 1,021 breast cancer patients [ 54 ]. This finding is largely consistent with the prevalence and spectrum of g BRCA1/2 mutations in European populations [ 10 ]. According to the systematic review of the international prevalence of BRCA1/2 mutation in breast cancer by Armstrong et al., the g BRCA1/2 mutation prevalence in 10 extensive studies that included unselected individuals based on family history of breast cancer, age, sex, or ethnicity ( N > 100) ranged from 2.7 to 15.4% [ 55 ]. In the seven most extensive studies included in this review, BRCA1/2 mutation prevalence varied from 2.9% in a Sardinian unselected population to 26.5% in a selected population for family history of breast cancer in Germany. Unfortunately, the papers mentioned above usually failed to report whether BRCA1/2 PVs were germline or somatic, and which genes ( BRCA1 , BRCA2 , or both) were examined. It was also unclear whether the detected variants had clinical significance, what sequencing method was used, and what the sequencing target was.

An important aspect of sequencing entire coding regions using NGS is the detection of variants of uncertain significance (VUS), which accounted for 5.48% of variants in our cohort. Although clinically challenging, VUS do not influence therapeutic decisions, as current qualification criteria for PARPi therapy are based exclusively on pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants. However, their identification warrants further genetic counseling, including consideration of variant co-segregation analysis in a family to clarify its potential clinical relevance. Variants of unknown significance should be also reevaluated every two years to account for changes in genomic databases and classification algorithms [ 37 , 56 , 57 ].

Previously reported results of BRCA1/2 testing in Poland focused on small groups (e.g. 161, 58 cases) and/or selected patients (e.g., early onset) and/or used sequential diagnostics - first looking for the most prevalent PVs and then, if none were identified, sequencing the whole BRCA1/2 coding sequence [ 30 , 33 , 37 , 58 ]. Our NGS results for over 1,000 consecutive BCs reveal the actual frequency of BRCA1/2 PVs/LPVs. Although our dataset is not free from limitations, this Central European cohort demonstrates patterns that are consistent with international benchmarks, supporting the robustness and generalizability of our findings.

### Limitations

A major limitation is the composition of the cohort. Patients ( n = 330; 24.43%) have been excluded due to a lack of informed consent, with only a small number not re-tested because to prior extensive BRCA1/2 NGS. Selected individuals with familial pathogenic variants discovered using non-NGS approaches were retested and included. Although removing previously NGS-tested patients, who are possibly enriched for mutation carriers, may modestly underestimate variant occurrence, the overall impact is expected to be limited given the prevalence of BRCA1/2 PVs/LPVs in the population. Whereas this should be considered when interpreting the findings and comparing them to studies with other defined cohorts, it also mimics real-world clinical practice in Poland, where previously tested individuals are not routinely retested.

The prevalence of BRCA1 and BRCA2 in the subpopulations of Polish breast cancer patients may differ depending on the geographical area and ethnic group studied. The population of Lower Silesia consists mainly of the descendants of World War II repatriates from today’s western Ukraine (formerly Eastern Galicia, Volhynia and Polesia) and the population gradually migrating in the post-war period from other regions of Poland, hence the distribution of PV in BRCA may differ from that in other Polish or European areas.

Although our findings indicate that a substantial proportion of detected BRCA1/2 variants were non-recurrent, suggesting potential limitations of founder mutation–based testing strategies, the present study was not designed to directly compare the diagnostic performance of targeted founder panels with comprehensive NGS approaches.

The study cohort is relatively small, limiting the ability to fully appreciate the full range of variants that could be detected.

Whereas this should be considered when interpreting the findings and comparing them to studies with other defined cohorts, it also mimics real-world clinical practice in Poland, where previously tested individuals are not routinely retested.

The prevalence of BRCA1 and BRCA2 in the subpopulations of Polish breast cancer patients may differ depending on the geographical area and ethnic group studied. The population of Lower Silesia consists mainly of the descendants of World War II repatriates from today’s western Ukraine (formerly Eastern Galicia, Volhynia and Polesia) and the population gradually migrating in the post-war period from other regions of Poland, hence the distribution of PV in BRCA may differ from that in other Polish or European areas.

Although our findings indicate that a substantial proportion of detected BRCA1/2 variants were non-recurrent, suggesting potential limitations of founder mutation–based testing strategies, the present study was not designed to directly compare the diagnostic performance of targeted founder panels with comprehensive NGS approaches.

The study cohort is relatively small, limiting the ability to fully appreciate the full range of variants that could be detected.


## Conclusion

The widespread use of high-throughput sequencing technology has enabled the testing of complete gene coding sequences, allowing all potential variants to be examined in a single test. Given the opportunities provided by NGS, limiting the cancer patients’ diagnostics to a few of the most common PVs may be detrimental to the patients and their relatives, because it limits either therapeutic or prophylactic options. Notably, current international guidelines, including those from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, increasingly recommend multigene panel testing and broader eligibility criteria for genetic testing in breast cancer patients, reflecting both the growing recognition of genetic heterogeneity and the decreasing cost of NGS technologies. Based on the available data, it is possible to conclude that universal screening programs in individual countries should be considered by decision-makers in terms of citizens’ health and well-being, as well as the potential reduction in future cancer treatment costs through preventive surgery and early tumour detection, as well as additional risk-reducing strategies such as lifestyle modification, chemoprevention, and adherence to tailored surveillance protocols.
