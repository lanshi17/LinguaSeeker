# Severe leukemoid reaction and spontaneous tumor lysis syndrome in a young patient with undifferentiated Lynch syndrome-associated ovarian cancer

## Abstract

Highlights • Lynch syndrome-associated ovarian cancer can present at a young age and be of high-grade histology. • Spontaneous tumor lysis syndrome can occur in ovarian cancer and may be the initial presentation. • Severe leukemoid reaction can occur as a paraneoplastic phenomenon in ovarian cancer and may portend a poor prognosis. • Leukemoid reaction and tumor lysis syndrome occurring together in ovarian cancer may signal aggressive tumor biology.


## Introduction

Approximately 15 to 20% of all ovarian cancers are linked to inherited gene mutations and Lynch syndrome accounts for 10 to 15% of hereditary ovarian cancers ( Toss et al., 2015 ). Lynch syndrome is an autosomal dominant condition caused by germline mutations in one of the four DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes, MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6 , or by mutations in a non-mismatch repair gene, EPCAM . The lifetime risk of ovarian cancer in patients with Lynch syndrome is 6 to 12%, typically presenting at early stages with non-serous epithelial histologies, most commonly endometrioid or clear cell. The average age of diagnosis of Lynch syndrome-associated ovarian cancer is 45 ( Helder-Woolderink et al., 2016 ).

Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) is a life-threatening oncologic emergency characterized by severe metabolic disturbances. It is caused by extensive tumor cell lysis and is most often observed in patients with hematologic malignancies at initiation of chemotherapy, but it can also occur spontaneously. TLS in solid tumors is rare and particularly uncommon in ovarian cancers, with only a few case reports in the literature ( Weber et al., 2023 Oct , Okamoto et al., 2015 , Shukla et al., 2017 ).

Hyperleukocytosis is defined as a white blood cell (WBC) count greater than 100,000/µL in patients with hematologic malignancies. When hematologic malignancy has been ruled out, persistent leukocytosis above 50,000/µL is defined as a leukemoid reaction, which can occur in the settings of severe infections, cancers, drug reactions, or hemorrhage. The presence of leukemoid reactions seen in malignancies other than leukemia is associated with a poorer prognosis ( Sakka et al., 2006 ).

Here we present the case of a 32-year-old with a Lynch syndrome-associated high grade ovarian cancer who had a severe leukemoid reaction and spontaneous TLS. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an aggressive Lynch syndrome-associated ovarian cancer presenting with leukemoid reaction and spontaneous TLS.

## Case report

A 32-year-old gravida 0 presented as a transfer reporting diffuse abdominal pain associated with nausea and loss of appetite. Her medical history was insignificant and family history was notable for a maternal grandfather with colorectal cancer. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a 32 x 26 x 18 cm solid and cystic heterogeneous mass filling the abdomen ( Fig. 1A ). CT of the chest was unremarkable. Laboratory studies were remarkable for WBC 105,000/µL, platelets 814,000/µL, hemoglobin 8.7 g/dL, potassium 5.1 mmol/L, creatinine 2.01 mg/dL, lactate dehydrogenase 509 U/L, ferritin 1,284 ng/mL, corrected calcium 11.4 mg/dL and uric acid 13.3 mg/dL. Urinalysis was remarkable for uric acid crystals. Tumor markers were notable for CA-125 91.7 U/mL, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) less than 0.6 ng/mL, CA 19-9 12.9 U/mL. Fig. 1 ( A) Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis at initial presentation demonstrating a large cystic and solid pelvic mass. (B) CT of the abdomen and pelvis three weeks after initial surgery demonstrating rapid recurrence of pelvic mass.

Hyperleukocytosis accompanied by laboratory findings of TLS raised concern for an underlying hematologic malignancy. A hematology consultation prompted immediate initiation of allopurinol and hydroxyurea. Bone marrow aspiration, BCR-ABL testing and myeloid fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) panel were subsequently obtained and were all negative for hematologic malignancy. She subsequently underwent an exploratory laparotomy with lysis of adhesions, right salpingo-oophorectomy and right ureterolysis. The procedure was complicated by intraoperative hypotension which was poorly responsive to fluid resuscitation and vasopressors, thus the procedure was ended prior to complete staging due to the patient’s tenuous status. Operative findings included a friable solid component of tumor approximately 15 cm in size arising from the right ovary, with a large fluid component filling the abdomen adhered to surrounding structures. Gross residual disease included a flat tumor plaque in the posterior cul-de-sac and scattered miliary nodules in the pelvis. Final pathology with expert review favored an undifferentiated carcinoma of the right ovary with somatic mutations in MLH1, TP53, ARID1A, CDKN2A, CYLD, LRP1B, and amplifications in MET and SMARCA4. During the post-operative course, the patient underwent germline genetic testing which demonstrated a germline MLH1 mutation c.676C > T, confirming the diagnosis of Lynch syndrome.

Following surgery, WBC count decreased to a nadir of 23,000/µL on post-operative day two and laboratory findings for TLS were initially resolved. During post-operative recovery three weeks later, she presented to the hospital with abdominal pain. WBC count was found to be 232,000/µL and CT abdomen and pelvis showed rapidly recurrent and progressive disease including an 18 cm solid pelvic mass, omental thickening, and ascites ( Fig. 1B ). Chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel was promptly initiated inpatient. She subsequently had pancytopenia with a WBC nadir of 200/µL. She underwent three total cycles of carboplatin/paclitaxel, and WBC count continued to range from 40,000–50,000/µL ( Fig. 2 ). Later in the chemotherapy course, laboratory studies demonstrated coagulopathy with elevated INR, low fibrinogen, elevated D-Dimer, and low platelets, findings suspected to be secondary to ongoing TLS and progressive malignancy. CT abdomen and pelvis after the third cycle of chemotherapy showed improved size of solid pelvic mass but worsened peritoneal carcinomatosis, ascites and new hepatic metastasis. The patient died at home after the third cycle of chemotherapy, presumably secondary to complications from rapidly progressive malignancy. Fig. 2 White blood cell (WBC) count over time. WBC count initially improved after surgery then rapidly increased when extensive progression was found on imaging. After chemotherapy, WBC improved though never normalized.

## Discussion

Lynch syndrome is caused by germline mutations in one of the four MMR genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2 , or by non-MMR gene EPCAM deletions that result in epigenetic silencing of MSH2 . Approximately 90% of mutations causing Lynch syndrome are located in MLH1 and MSH2 . The specific gene mutation leading to Lynch syndrome has been found to confer variable absolute risks of ovarian cancer. Mutations in MSH2 or EPCAM are associated with an 8–38% absolute risk of ovarian cancer, and mutations in MLH1 are associated with a 4–20% absolute risk of ovarian cancer. Mutations in MSH6 and PMS2 have a lower absolute risk of ovarian cancer ( Engel et al., 2012 Dec 10 , Bonadona et al., 2011 ). The patient presented here was ultimately found to have a germline mutation in MLH1 .

Lynch-associated ovarian cancers present earlier than sporadic ovarian cancers, with an average age of 45, and typically at earlier stages of less common epithelial histologies such as endometrioid or clear cell. The prevalence of high-grade histologies in Lynch-associated ovarian cancer is <25% (Helder-Woolderink et al., 2016). National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines indicate that data does not support routine ovarian cancer screening for Lynch syndrome. Risk-reducing surgery for ovarian cancer with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is recommended starting as early as age 40, particularly for mutations with a higher risk of ovarian cancer, such as MLH1 and MSH2 / EPCAM ( NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment: Colorectal, Endometrial, and Gastric V.1.2025 ). The case presented here is unique given the undifferentiated ovarian cancer histology that occurred at a much younger age than is typical and prior to the recommended age for risk-reducing surgery.

TLS is most often associated with hematologic malignancies after initiation of chemotherapy. Only a few cases of spontaneous TLS have been reported in patients with ovarian cancer, and mortality in such cases can be high ( Weber et al., 2023 Oct , Okamoto et al., 2015 ). The patient presented here had laboratory and clinical findings consistent with TLS at presentation, and evidence of coagulopathy suspected secondary to ongoing TLS later in her treatment course.

The initial evaluation of a patient with an extremely elevated WBC count includes workup for hematologic malignancy in consultation with hematologic oncology experts. Testing for hematologic malignancy can include blood sampling and bone marrow aspiration and biopsy with the performance of immunohistochemistry stains, flow cytometry, cytogenetic analysis, FISH, and next generation sequencing (NGS) (Sakka et al., 2006). The presented patient had thorough evaluation that was negative for hematologic malignancy including bone marrow aspiration, cytogenetic analysis, and a FISH panel. Once hematologic malignancy has been ruled out in a patient with a WBC count of 50,000/µL or greater, a leukemoid reaction is designated as a diagnosis of exclusion. Leukemoid reactions are most commonly secondary to severe infection, drug reactions, significant hemorrhage, acute hemolysis, or malignancy. History, physical examination, and additional evaluation such as imaging should be performed to ascertain the etiology. The patient presented here had a pelvic mass on imaging, thus gynecologic malignancy was suspected as the cause of the leukemoid reaction.

Leukemoid reactions in the setting of malignancy are thought to be paraneoplastic phenomena caused by cytokine-releasing tumors and have been most often reported in association with cancers of the lung and stomach ( Abukhiran, et al., 2020 ). Leukemoid reactions are associated with a poorer prognosis, which is consistent with this patient’s overall course ( Sakka et al., 2006 ). To our knowledge, there is only one other reported case of leukemoid reaction in association with undifferentiated ovarian carcinoma ( Chuang et al., 2001 ). The most unique aspect of this patient’s presentation was the profound leukemoid reaction that was found at initial diagnosis and in association with rapid recurrence. The WBC count was greater than 100,000/µL prior to initial surgery, and only three weeks postoperatively was greater than 200,000/µL when marked progression was noted on imaging. WBC count subsequently improved with chemotherapy treatment, though never completely normalized.

In this case, a Lynch syndrome-associated ovarian cancer occurred at a much younger age than average and with a high-grade histology. Spontaneous TLS, although rare, can occur with ovarian cancer and should be promptly identified and treated given its high mortality rate. Further, leukemoid reaction seen in the setting of ovarian malignancy is also rare but possible, and in this context appears to be a marker of aggressive tumor behavior. Concomitant leukemoid reaction and TLS in patients with ovarian cancer indicates a poor prognosis and should be considered during the disease and treatment course.
