# Advances in gene therapy for mitochondrial genetic disorders: current status and clinical implementation challenges

## Abstract

Mitochondria function as the primary energy hubs of cells and possess semi-autonomous genetic characteristic. Mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) frequently lead to severe illness and even premature death. The rapid advancement of gene therapy offers promising potential for correcting such disorders. This review first aims to delineate the mechanisms of gene therapy strategies applicable to mitochondrial diseases, including the allotopic expression of mtDNA in the nucleus, mitochondrial-targeted nuclease cleavage, and mtDNA-targeted base editing. It also discusses in detail the clinical efficacy of mtDNA allotopic expression and the preclinical progress of other strategies. Furthermore, the unique physiological features of mitochondria, such as heteroplasmy and independent molecular transport mechanisms, pose distinct challenges for the clinical implementation of mitochondrial gene therapy strategies. Accordingly, this review elaborates on the current limitations of each approach. Finally, it highlights potential optimization directions to address these challenges, emphasizing that understanding heteroplasmy dynamics and their corresponding phenotypes, ensuring the safe delivery and tissue-specific expression of therapeutic elements, and maintaining long-term therapeutic specificity and efficiency are essential for the clinical translation of mitochondrial gene therapy.


## Introduction

Compared to the nuclear DNA (nDNA), mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) exhibits higher mutation rates due to the exposure to a ROS-rich environment [ 1 – 5 ]. Consequently, mtDNA is more prone to mutations under stress conditions and manifests as point mutations or structural rearrangements [ 6 ]. Point mutations may occur within protein-coding genes, as well as tRNA and rRNA genes [ 7 ]. Rearrangements of mtDNA include deletions, insertions, and inversions that vary in size and position, often spanning multiple genes [ 7 , 8 ] (Fig. 1 ). Such rearrangements are generally sporadic, arising de novo early in embryogenesis or within the ovum, although they can also be transmitted maternally [ 7 , 9 – 11 ]. On account of the multicopy pattern of mtDNA, the mutations are characterized by heteroplasmy, where a cell contains both mutant and wild-type mtDNA, and exhibit threshold effects, meaning phenotypic symptoms only manifest when the proportion of mutant mtDNA surpasses a specific cellular or tissue-specific threshold [ 12 ]. Typically, the threshold levels of mtDNA mutations range from 60% to 95%, depending on the severity of the disease. This threshold level also varies across different organs and tissues based on their energy requirements, being lower in tissues that are highly dependent on OXPHOS metabolism, including muscle, heart, eye, and central nervous system (CNS) tissues, which are particularly susceptible to mtDNA mutations [ 12 , 13 ]. Among the identified pathogenic mutations, over half reside within protein-coding genes. A paradigmatic example is Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), an inherited mitochondrial disorder characterized by acute or subacute bilateral optic nerve dysfunction, followed by the development of optic atrophy [ 14 ]. Additionally, m.8993T > G is also a prevalent genotype, affecting the mtDNA gene encoding ATP synthase subunit 6 (MT-ATP6) and impairing ATP synthesis [ 13 ]. A key defining feature of this mutation lies in its strong correlation between heteroplasmy levels and clinical severity. Disorders caused by mtDNA mutations frequently resulting in severe illness and even premature death [ 17 ]. With the growing understanding of drug and target molecular structures and the advancement of computer-aided drug screening and design [ 18 – 21 ], research into treating mitochondrial genetic diseases has evolved from initial symptomatic support and antioxidant supplementation to nucleoside supplementation for specific genetic defects, dietary metabolic intervention, and molecularly targeted drugs now in clinical trials [ 22 ]. However, due to the genetic and phenotypic diversity of mitochondrial diseases themselves, the limited number of patients, the lack of unified efficacy evaluation indicators, and technical bottlenecks in drug delivery, there is still a lack of widely recognized disease-modifying therapies at present [ 23 , 24 ]. Mitochondrial replacement therapies, including maternal spindle transfer, pronuclear transfer, the first and second polar body transfer, are novel reproductive research directions in recent years for genetic diseases caused by mtDNA mutations [ 25 ]. However, these methods still facing multiple technical limitations and legal challenges, especially the embryo manipulation and long-term health risks [ 26 , 27 ], alongside issues of identity recognition and social-ethical controversies stemming from the involvement of three genetic material contributors [ 28 , 29 ].

In contrast, as a therapy that achieves long-term disease correction through a single genome‑editing intervention, thereby reducing the chronic medication burden inherent to conventional regimens, gene therapy offers significant advantages in its capacity to directly target diseases caused by nDNA mutations. This capability holds the potential for precision treatment and even the possibility of curing such disorders by directly addressing the root cause [ 30 ]. Thus, it is reasonable to infer that gene therapy targeting mtDNA could hold significant promise for both intervening in embryo mutations and curing individuals affected by mitochondrial genetic diseases. Indeed, substantial progress has been made in recent years across multiple approaches to mtDNA manipulation, including the allotopic expression of mtDNA in the nucleus for gene replacement, the elimination of mutant mtDNA copies via mtDNA-specific nucleases, and mtDNA base editing technologies [ 31 – 36 ]. However, the striking differences in molecular transport mechanisms and DNA damage repair pathways between mitochondria and the nucleus necessitate the development of novel therapeutic tools and delivery strategies for mtDNA gene therapy—approaches that diverge fundamentally from those used in nDNA therapy [ 37 – 39 ]. Notably, the majority of these mtDNA-targeted interventions remain in the preclinical development stage.

Given the advancements in basic research related to mtDNA in recent years, particularly the rapid emergence of new versions of base editing methods, progress in innovative delivery techniques, and controversies surrounding the application of RNA-based gene editing tools for mitochondrial gene editing, all of which are beyond the scope of previous reviews, it is necessary to summarize and update the current progress, existing challenges, and potential clinical applications of these methods. The purpose of this review is to address these needs through a comprehensive analysis of relevant research articles retrieved from the PubMed database using the keywords (Gene Therapy OR Gene Edit*) AND Mitochondr*, covering the period from 2010 to the present, as well as essential secondary literature cited in the identified papers during the literature search. First, it will briefly outline the genome repair mechanisms inherent to mitochondria, thereby establishing a foundational understanding of therapeutic mechanisms in this field. Next, the review will summarize recent advancements in novel therapeutic tools, highlighting their potential to transform the treatment paradigm for mtDNA mutations. In addition, it will discuss the unique challenges currently confronting mtDNA gene therapy, including packaging limitations, off-target effects, and mitochondria-specific delivery, along with potential solutions.

Disorders caused by mtDNA mutations frequently resulting in severe illness and even premature death [ 17 ]. With the growing understanding of drug and target molecular structures and the advancement of computer-aided drug screening and design [ 18 – 21 ], research into treating mitochondrial genetic diseases has evolved from initial symptomatic support and antioxidant supplementation to nucleoside supplementation for specific genetic defects, dietary metabolic intervention, and molecularly targeted drugs now in clinical trials [ 22 ]. However, due to the genetic and phenotypic diversity of mitochondrial diseases themselves, the limited number of patients, the lack of unified efficacy evaluation indicators, and technical bottlenecks in drug delivery, there is still a lack of widely recognized disease-modifying therapies at present [ 23 , 24 ]. Mitochondrial replacement therapies, including maternal spindle transfer, pronuclear transfer, the first and second polar body transfer, are novel reproductive research directions in recent years for genetic diseases caused by mtDNA mutations [ 25 ]. However, these methods still facing multiple technical limitations and legal challenges, especially the embryo manipulation and long-term health risks [ 26 , 27 ], alongside issues of identity recognition and social-ethical controversies stemming from the involvement of three genetic material contributors [ 28 , 29 ].

In contrast, as a therapy that achieves long-term disease correction through a single genome‑editing intervention, thereby reducing the chronic medication burden inherent to conventional regimens, gene therapy offers significant advantages in its capacity to directly target diseases caused by nDNA mutations. This capability holds the potential for precision treatment and even the possibility of curing such disorders by directly addressing the root cause [ 30 ]. Thus, it is reasonable to infer that gene therapy targeting mtDNA could hold significant promise for both intervening in embryo mutations and curing individuals affected by mitochondrial genetic diseases. Indeed, substantial progress has been made in recent years across multiple approaches to mtDNA manipulation, including the allotopic expression of mtDNA in the nucleus for gene replacement, the elimination of mutant mtDNA copies via mtDNA-specific nucleases, and mtDNA base editing technologies [ 31 – 36 ]. However, the striking differences in molecular transport mechanisms and DNA damage repair pathways between mitochondria and the nucleus necessitate the development of novel therapeutic tools and delivery strategies for mtDNA gene therapy—approaches that diverge fundamentally from those used in nDNA therapy [ 37 – 39 ]. Notably, the majority of these mtDNA-targeted interventions remain in the preclinical development stage.

Given the advancements in basic research related to mtDNA in recent years, particularly the rapid emergence of new versions of base editing methods, progress in innovative delivery techniques, and controversies surrounding the application of RNA-based gene editing tools for mitochondrial gene editing, all of which are beyond the scope of previous reviews, it is necessary to summarize and update the current progress, existing challenges, and potential clinical applications of these methods. The purpose of this review is to address these needs through a comprehensive analysis of relevant research articles retrieved from the PubMed database using the keywords (Gene Therapy OR Gene Edit*) AND Mitochondr*, covering the period from 2010 to the present, as well as essential secondary literature cited in the identified papers during the literature search. First, it will briefly outline the genome repair mechanisms inherent to mitochondria, thereby establishing a foundational understanding of therapeutic mechanisms in this field. Next, the review will summarize recent advancements in novel therapeutic tools, highlighting their potential to transform the treatment paradigm for mtDNA mutations. In addition, it will discuss the unique challenges currently confronting mtDNA gene therapy, including packaging limitations, off-target effects, and mitochondria-specific delivery, along with potential solutions.


## Mechanisms of mitochondrial genome maintenance

Growing evidence suggests that mitochondria share specific DNA repair pathways with the nucleus, enabling efficient repair of damaged bases and thereby offering molecular mechanistic support for the feasibility of gene therapy [ 38 ]. Meanwhile, as a circular, multicopy genome, the linear mtDNA generated by double-strand breaks (DSBs) in mitochondria undergoes unique clearance pathways [ 37 ]. This intrinsic feature presents both distinct opportunities and challenges for designing mitochondrial gene therapy strategies.

### Base excision repair

Numerous studies have established that base excision repair (BER) serves as the primary repair pathway in mitochondria [ 40 ], specifically designed to counteract DNA damage induced by oxidation, alkylation, and deamination, among which deamination is a key step in mtDNA base editing [ 41 ]. This process starts with mitochondrially targeted DNA glycosylases. These enzymes recognize damaged bases, “flip out” the lesion from the DNA helix, and cleave the N-glycosidic bond to form apurinic (AP) sites [ 42 – 45 ].

In mitochondrial BER, key DNA glycosylases perform specialized roles. Among them, uracil-DNA glycosylase (UDG) mediates the excision of uracil residues from DNA, which arise from cytosine deamination or erroneous incorporation of dUMP during replication [ 46 ]. Consequently, inhibition of UDG activity by uracil glycosylase inhibitor (UGI) is frequently employed during C-to-U(T) base editing, a mainstream method of mtDNA gene therapy, to mitigate repair-mediated editing reversal and enhance conversion efficiency [ 47 ].

### MtDNA degradation following DSBs

Similar to the nuclear genome, many DNA-damaging factors, such as defective replication, ROS, chemotherapeutic agents or ionizing radiations, can cause DSBs in mtDNA. However, unlike nDNA, little evidence supported the presence of classical non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) during mtDNA repair. Instead, the linearized mtDNA damaged by DSBs in mammalian cells were observed to undergo rapid degradation and a decrease in mtDNA content with no sign of mitochondria loss [ 48 ]. Further evidence suggested that the degradation of these linearized mtDNA shared the same machinery with mtDNA replication, which relied on the 5’-3’ exonuclease activity of MGME1, the 3’-5’ exonuclease activity of POLγ and mitochondrial DNA helicase TWNK [ 49 ].

Due to the multicopy nature of the mitochondrial genome, the elimination of a subset of mtDNA molecules is unlikely to compromise cellular function. Furthermore, studies have shown that when mtDNA copy number drops below a certain threshold, replication is activated through an undefined mechanism [ 50 ]. Notably, mtDNA replication operates independently of the cell cycle, displaying rapid turnover even in nonproliferative cells [ 51 ]. Integrating these findings with the established threshold effect of mutant mtDNA [ 12 ], it is theoretically plausible that mitochondrially localized nucleases designed to recognize specific sequences and induce DSBs could selectively degrade mtDNA, thereby offering a novel strategy for mtDNA-targeted gene therapy.

### Microhomology-mediated end joining

Although classical NHEJ was not detected in the DSB repair of mtDNA, microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) has been established as a key pathway in mtDNA DSB repair. MMEJ is an error-prone DNA DSB repair pathway that operates independently of classical NHEJ and homologous recombination (HR) [ 52 ]. Biochemical evidence, including the presence of core factors CtIP, FEN1, MRE11, PARP1, and Ligase III, along with diminished end-joining efficiency upon their immunoprecipitation, and the identification of mitochondrial-localization of POLθ, collectively confirms the existence of an MMEJ pathway within mitochondria [ 53 – 55 ]. These findings, aligned with the report that approximately 85% of deletions in the mtDNA of human cells are flanked by short repetitive sequences [ 56 ], suggest that in the absence of classical NHEJ, MMEJ serves as the primary mtDNA DSB repair mechanism and is directly implicated in the frequent mtDNA deletions observed in mitochondrial genomes.

### Homologous recombination

Mitochondrial HR, theoretically enabled by the multiple copies of mtDNA and potential cell-cycle independence, was long debated [ 57 ]. However, growing evidence now supports its role in mitochondrial DSB repair.

Recent investigations have revealed that plasmids harboring DNA-protein crosslinks could undergo HR-mediated repair in mammalian mitochondria, a process dependent on mitochondrial genome homology and RAD51 polymerization [ 58 ]. These findings establish the existence of HR-dependent repair pathways in mitochondria. Despite the fact that the precise roles of these proteins and the potential requirement for additional cofactors remain to be elucidated, this knowledge is critical for the rational design of HR-based gene therapy strategies in mitochondrial contexts.

## Overview of gene therapy towards mtDNA

As a promising therapeutic strategy, gene therapy has been extensively investigated for treating numerous genetic disorders caused by nDNA mutations, with over a dozen medicines approved for clinical use. Mitochondrial dysfunctions mediated by nDNA mutations, which share the fundamental principles of gene therapy targeting the nuclear genome, have been comprehensively reviewed elsewhere [ 25 , 30 , 59 ].

In contrast, genetic modification of mtDNA presents unique technical challenges, primarily due to several distinctive barriers: codon bias between the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes [ 60 ], the requirement for subcellular localization of therapeutic components to mitochondria [ 61 ], the multicopy nature of mtDNA within each cell, and the challenges associated with delivering nucleic acids across mitochondrial membranes [ 62 , 63 ].

Currently, allotopic expression of specific functional mtDNA in the nucleus, combined with modified mRNA encoding to ensure mitochondrial targeting, remains the approach closest to clinical translation in this field. Meanwhile, therapies based on programmable nucleases and base editors—technologies that rely on RNA-free components for both organelle and mtDNA sequence targeting—have advanced rapidly in recent years. These methods can induce DNA double-strand breaks in mtDNA to promote heteroplasmic shifts, or precisely correct genetic defects in mtDNA, respectively [ 25 , 59 ] (Table 2 ). ## Allotopic expression of mtDNA

In this approach, the mtDNA sequence is expressed allotopically in the nucleus, followed by cytoplasmic translation of the re-encoded DNA and subsequent transport to mitochondria, mirroring the pathway of mitochondrial proteins encoded by nuclear genes [ 65 ]. Thus, it shares many features in common with nuclear gene replacement therapy, including the selection of delivery systems and transcriptional regulatory elements [ 30 ]. In contrast, unique elements should also be designed to achieve the nuclear transcription and mitochondrial localization of the these artificially imported genes (Fig. 2 ). ### The common features of allotopic expression therapy

To prevent the formation of missense and/or truncated products when mtDNA-derived sequences are expressed in the nucleus, the non-universal codons within the corresponding mitochondrial coding genes must first undergo mutagenesis [ 66 ]. Meanwhile, effective mitochondrial targeting signals (MTS) should be fused to achieve the mitochondrial import of the functional allotopic expression products. MTS from the N-terminal of nuclear encoded mitochondrial proteins, including COX8, ATP5G1, ATP1, ALDH, COX10 and SOD2 were reported to promote the allotopic expression and mitochondrial import of MT-ATP6 [ 61 , 66 – 68 ], MT-ATP8 [ 68 ], MT-ND1 [ 31 ], MT-ND3 [ 69 ], MT-ND4 [ 31 , 65 , 67 , 70 , 71 ], and MT-ND6 [ 72 ]. These strategies have effectively alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction caused by corresponding mutations in patient-derived cells or animal models. In addition, the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of the transcripts were also demonstrated to be important to the delivery of mRNAs to the surface of mitochondria and the following organelle biogenesis [ 61 ]. For example, the absence of 3’ UTR in ATP2 mRNA was observed to impair its import even though the precursor was addressed to the organelle surface and was associated with a severe respiratory deficiency [ 73 ]. As a result, the fusing of 3’UTR of SOD2 or COX10 during the allotopic expression of mtDNAs were usually performed in different studies [ 31 , 61 , 67 , 69 , 70 ].

### Clinical applications on LHON

Despite the success of the proof-of-concept for such mitochondrial gene replacement therapy in patient-derived cells, few animal and clinical studies have been reported, except for the allotopic expression of MT-ND4. The m.11778 G > A mutation of MT-ND4 is responsible for 70% of LHON (Table 1 ). This mutation is characterized by a defect in respiratory chain complex I, which leads to the loss of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), optic atrophy, and subsequent vision impairment [ 74 ]. The significant progress in gene therapy research towards LHON is probably due to the special disease pattern. The change of nerve fiber density and the vision loss, which happens in the majority of patients with only eye involvement [ 74 ], could be assessed much easier in LHON models than other neuromuscular and motor deficiencies. Moreover, it is easier to deliver therapy components through intravitreal injection of viral vectors with minimal immune response and systemic toxicity, which has been proved by the first FDA approved gene therapy medicine towards Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA), a severe nuclear inherited retinal dystrophy [ 30 , 75 ].

In fact, after validation in rodent and nonhuman primate models [ 70 , 71 ], multiple clinical trials targeting LHON have been conducted using adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated nuclear expression of human mtDNAs and summarized here (Table 3 ). Among these registrations, the most extensively investigated and significantly advanced trials utilized human MT-DN4 fused with COX10 MTS and 3’ UTR (rAAV2-CMV-MTS COX10- hND4-3’UTR COX10 ). An additional trial targeting mutated MT-ND1 was also registered, though no results have been published to date [ 25 , 59 ]. Notably, two independent research groups have reported consistent findings that demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of allotopically expressed MT-ND4 [ 76 – 78 ]. These results were further confirmed by the comparation of disease progression between these treated patients and natural history cohort [ 81 ]. Long-term follow-up studies up to 5 years of these trials showed a sustained improvement in visual acuity from baseline among these patients, which was quantified by best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) [ 84 ]. Besides these progresses, some special features of these trials need to be noted. It was observed that bilateral improvement in BCVA was observed after unilaterally injection of the COX10 MTS guided MT-ND4 in both clinical trials and nonclinical investigations, suggesting the success of gene transduction among RGCs [ 85 – 87 ]. On the other hand, another MT-ND4 trial employed the N-terminal of ATP1 as the MTS failed to achieve the therapeutic goal [ 71 , 80 ]. The longitudinal results from this trial highlighted the challenges of therapeutic window according to the observation that steady-state pattern electroretinography (SS-PERG) dysfunction and RGC layer damage were detectable even in the asymptomatic eyes of LHON patients [ 80 ], consistent with the concerns about the irreversibility of oxidative damage, RGC apoptosis, and axonal loss during long-term mitochondrial dysfunction. However, considering the reported therapeutic effect of the COX10 MTS guided MT-ND4, additional comparation between these two strategies may be needed under the same inclusion rules before drawing a conclusion.

Nonetheless, all these trials showed well tolerance with no systemic adverse event. Only mild and well controlled intraocular inflammation was recorded and unrelated to the medicine, since no difference of the inflammation was observed between bilaterally and unilaterally treated patients [ 76 , 79 , 88 , 89 ].

### Challenges and prospects of mtDNA allotopic expression


#### Mitochondrial delivery limitations

A major concern of the nuclear allotopic expression of mtDNA products was that the hydrophobic characteristics of native mitochondrial proteins restrict their import from the cytosol. For instance, allotopic expression of apocytochrome b, a product of high mesohydrophobicity encoded by the CYB gene, has been observed to accumulate at the mitochondrial periphery, disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential, and ultimately induce cell death [ 90 ]. Similarly, another study observed that nuclear-expressed MT-ND6 aggregates outside mitochondria, even when the allotopically expressed protein was fused with a mitochondrial targeting element, consisting of the 25-amino-acid MTS plus the first two amino acids of mature COX8 [ 91 ].

#### Optimization of gene codons

In contrast, a more recent study showed a success of MT-ND6 allotopic expression with the fusion of 25-amino acid MTS from COX8 and the change of 6 non-universal codons with universal ones [ 72 ]. The striking differences in outcomes between the two MT-ND6 studies suggest that optimizing gene codons and the functional fusion components in allotopic expression constructs should be essential considerations during the design process.

Consistently, a codon optimization study has demonstrated the re-engineer of all 13 mitochondrial protein encoding genes resulted in higher protein expressions [ 60 ]. In contrast to minimally recoded mitochondrial genes, where only non-universal genetic codes (Trp, Met, Stop) are altered, the present study incorporated nuclear codon preferences into codon optimization. The observed discrepancies in mRNA enrichment and expression stability were attributed to the divergence between minimally recoded genes and nuclear genes, which leads to inefficient translation by nuclear machinery [ 60 ].

Furthermore, based on these discoveries, an intriguing hypothesis regarding codon optimization may be proposed for further investigation: that the translation speed of mtDNA-encoded products could be slowed down by using relatively rare codons prior to the translation of hydrophobic regions, thereby minimizing unfavorable folding in the cytoplasm.

#### Optimization of MTS and 3’ UTR

On the other hand, multiple patterns of MTS and 3’ UTR, including the charge, length, and structure, were important for protein import into the mitochondria in different species [ 92 ]. A high-throughput screening platform based on immunofluorescence and high-content imaging was reported for the screen of MTS and 3’ UTR for different mtDNA products. In this study, 31 MTS, either used individually or in tandem, and 15 3’UTRs were analyzed. The findings demonstrated that the selection of MTS plays a more critical role in localizing alloplasmically expressed products to the mitochondria [ 93 ]. The variation of mitochondrial import of proteins fused with different MTS provides a feasible direction for the efficiency improvement of this type of gene therapy.

#### The progress of direct mitochondrial delivery


##### Viral vectors

An alternative approach to overcoming the limitations of allotopic expression could involve directly delivering therapeutic genes into mitochondria using appropriate vectors. Conventional rAAV particles, upon infecting cells, can escape from endosomes and the trans-Golgi network before entering the nucleus via nuclear pore complexes [ 94 ]. In contrast, AAV fused with MTS was demonstrated to be able to redirect to mitochondria instead of nucleus [ 95 ] (Fig. 2 ). COX8 MTS fused with the VP2 capsid protein of AAV2 has been found to successfully deliver expression vectors to mitochondria. The delivered hND4, driven by the heavy strand promoter (HSP) and utilizing mitochondrial codons, was detected to be expressed in cells carrying the m.11778 G > A mutation, thereby restoring the ATP synthesis defect [ 95 ]. A mouse LHON model was successfully constructed by zygote infection with MTS-AAV carrying mutant hND4 [ 96 ]. Furthermore, the successful establishment of mouse disease models could also be achieved by delivering mutant human ND1, ND4, or ND6 through MTS-AAV via travitreal injection, while the phenotype could be rescued by an additional injection of MTS-AAV delivered wild-type (WT) genes [ 97 – 99 ]. In addition to demonstrating a superior LHON rescue effect compared with allotopic MT-ND4 expression in the same disease model [ 100 ], these results highlight the potential of direct mitochondrial delivery of therapeutic sequences for future clinical.

In addition to demonstrating a superior LHON rescue effect compared with allotopic MT-ND4 expression in the same disease model [ 100 ], these results highlight the potential of direct mitochondrial delivery of therapeutic sequences for future clinical.


##### Non-viral vectors

Non-viral vectors, alongside their viral counterparts, represent a pivotal frontier in gene therapy application research, attributed to their cost-effectiveness and diminished immunogenicity [ 30 ]. Mitochondrial targeting non-viral vectors have been developed by different approaches (Fig. 2 ). For example, TISUH, a mitochondrial gene delivery vector consists of triphenylphosphine-terminated poly and Ide-terminated poly, was engineered to target mitochondria in LHON. It achieves this targeting through the reduced membrane potential of diseased mitochondria and releases the therapeutic sequence in response to elevated intracellular ROS. The delivery and therapy efficiency of this approach was also demonstrated by the disease phenotype induced by the intraocular injection of the non-viral packaged mutant ND4 and the following rescue by WT gene [ 101 ]. Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) conjugated with MTS also showed the ability in mediating exogenous gene into the mitochondria. In cells treated with a nanocomplex composed of ND1-FITC-expressing plasmids and MTS-conjugated PEGylated WRAP (a tryptophan- and arginine-rich peptide), notable expression of ND1 was observed. Furthermore, this MTS-CPP conjugate could internalize into zebrafish embryos without inducing toxicity or developmental defects [ 102 ]. In addition, RD3AD, another CCP conjugated with the mitochondriotropic dye Rho123 as the MTS, further showed the ability to deliver intact mtDNA into the mitochondria and restore the production of mitochondrial RNAs and proteins in mtDNA-depleted ARPE-19 cells, with no sign of cellular toxicity mediated by naked mtDNA [ 103 ]. Taken together, these reported advantages further emphasized the potential of non-viral vectors in gene replacement therapy for not only single gene mutation caused mitochondrial disorders but also diseases caused by multiple mutations and deletions.

##### Safety concern of systemic delivery

Immunotoxicities caused by the delivery tools or therapy cargos are common challenges for gene therapy, especially during systemic therapy [ 30 ]. In a mouse model, the nuclear genomic integration and successful allotopic expression of ATP8, driven by the CAG promoter, provided preliminary evidence for the safety of systemic administration in this type of gene replacement therapy [ 104 ]. However, as most successful models and solutions have been limited to LHON, the construction of novel disease models and the assessment of the safety and therapeutic efficiency of other systemically expressed nuclear mtDNAs should be carefully designed. More importantly, the selection and functional analysis of low-toxicity, tissue-specific viral and non-viral delivery tools for mitochondrial disorders requiring systemic delivery warrant further investigation.

## MtDNA specific nucleases

Although allotopic expression of mtDNA has demonstrated to be able to ameliorate the phenotype of some mitochondrial diseases, it fails to alter the heterozygous pattern of pathogenic mitochondria. In contrast, the autonomous replicative capacity of the mitochondrial genome, combined with observations that linearized mtDNA damaged by DSBs undergoes rapid degradation, offers a potential strategy to reduce, or even eliminate, pathogenic gene copies [ 48 ] (Fig. 3 A). In fact, mitochondria-targeted restriction endonucleases (mtREs) have been reported to alter the degree of heteroplasmy by recognizing and cleaving mtDNA at specific sites generated by pathogenic mutations [ 13 , 15 , 16 ]. Several studies have demonstrated the specific elimination capacity of this approach in vivo in mouse models harboring two neutral mtDNA variants, NZB and BALB, with the latter containing an ApaLI restriction site [ 105 – 108 ]. ### MtZFNs

The customized engineered zinc fingers (ZF) from Cys2His2 family utilize their tandemly arranged units to recognize and bind to consecutive 3-base triplets in the major groove of DNA. Sufficient evidence in nuclear gene expression modulations and sequence-specific DNA modifications have been shown in these engineered proteins [ 111 ]. The selective binding capacity of zinc finger peptides (ZFPs) to predefined mtDNA sequences was first demonstrated in a ZFP fused with both a MTS and a nuclear export signal (NES). This was confirmed by detecting selective cytosine methylation near the m.8993T > G sequence, mediated by ZFP-guided hDNMT3a methyltransferase [ 112 ].

#### Single-chain mtZFN

Soon after that, mtZFN was constructed by the fusing of mitochondrial targeted ZFP with cleavage domain of the type IIs restriction enzyme FokI, which requires dimerization to cleave double-stranded DNA. However, the expression of regular dimeric mtZFNs that recognize both the mutant sequence and the adjacent normal complementary sequences often resulted in the cleavage of WT mtDNA due to the non-specific dimerization and activation of traditional FokI. Thus, a single-chain mtZFN was first created by the connection of two FokI nuclease catalytic domains to a single ZFP with a flexible linker. The efficiency and specificity of this mtZFN was demonstrated by the increased proportion of WT mtDNA in heteroplasmic cells [ 109 ] (Fig. 3 B). However, single-chain ZFNs were limited by the incapacity of elimination against large mtDNA deletions, as well as the safety concerns due to the non-specific nuclear DNA breaks [ 109 ].

#### Paired mtZfns

To overcome these limitations, paired mtZFNs were recommissioned, with the sequence of fusion protein structures been adjusted. In addition, each connected FokI domain were separately modified through mutagenesis of either ELD (Q486E, N496D, I499L) or KKR (E490K, H537R, I538K). The heterodimer exhibited efficient DNA cleavage comparable to the WT architecture, but was 40 times less active with either homodimer activity [ 33 ] (Fig. 3 C).

The efficiency of the modified mtZFN was demonstrated by the increase of WT mtDNA proportion in cybrid cell models harboring either the m.8993T > G point mutation or a 4977-bp repeat-flanked deletion (KSS in Fig. 1 ) [ 33 ]. Further research revealed that both the efficiency of mutant mtDNA elimination and the non-specific nuclease-DNA interactions were dose-dependent on mtZFN. Specifically, high expression levels of mtZFN transiently saturated the import of mitochondrial proteins, whereas controlled expression minimized off-target degradation of the WT genome and achieved near-complete elimination of mutant mtDNA [ 113 ].

#### In vivo application of mtZfns

Subsequently, the therapeutic potential of mtZFN was confirmed in animal models. Paired mtZFNs targeting the m.5024C > T site (MTM25) and an adjacent sequence on the opposite strand (WTM1) were delivered via the cardiotropic AAV9.45 serotype into a mouse model harboring this point mutation, which faithfully recapitulates key molecular features of related mitochondrial disorders in cardiac tissue [ 114 , 115 ]. The most effective elimination of mutant mtDNA was achieved at an intermediate dose (5 × 10^12 vg/monomer), aligning with the finding that high expression levels of mtZFNs led to the degradation of WT mtDNA. In addition, the best elimination of mutant mtDNA also led to the most significant increase in mt-tRNA ALA steady-state levels and the improvement of mitochondrial respiration, with no sign of off-target in the nuclear genome [ 115 ].

This approach was further optimized through the development of a tandem-linked mtZFN construct, in which the paired MTM25 and WTM1 components were linked via an 18-amino acid T2A ‘self-cleaving’ sequence. This design enabled the packaging and delivery of all therapeutic elements within a single AAV vector, eliminating the need for co-infection with separate vectors and thereby reducing dosing requirements [ 116 ]. It should be noticed that the sequence homology between the paired mtZFNs in this approach may resulted in the DNA strand slippage during replication and large-scale deletions of the transgene, which could be reduced by the recoding of one mtZFN sequence exploiting DNA codon redundancy. After such optimization, the effective dose of AAV delivered MTM25–T2A-WTM1 could reach to 10-fold lower (5 × 10^11 vg/mouse) than that of the separate monomers, while simultaneously achieving equivalent rescue effects with no nuclear off-target effects either [ 115 , 116 ]. Furthermore, small but significant increases in Il1b and Isg20 was detected only in mice with systemic treatment of separated capsid at the dosage of 5 × 10^12 vg/monomer [ 116 ], suggesting the immune safety of systemic administration of tandem-mtZFN strategy and the promising prospects for future clinical applications.

### MitoTALENs

MitoTALENs have also been demonstrated as effective tools to induce mtDNA heteroplasmy shift. Similar to ZFNs, DSBs were also induced by the dimerization of the nuclease domain of FokI in TALENs. Differently, the guidance of nucleases is achieved through transcription activator-like effectors (TALEs), which originate from Xanthomonas bacteria and serve as components to activate promoters of invaded plant cells, thereby enabling efficient infection and propagation [ 117 ]. Each DNA sequence-specific binding repeat unit on TALE is composed of 34 amino acids, which recognizes a 1-bp site through repeat variable diresidues (RVDs) containing two highly variable amino acids at positions 12 and 13 [ 118 ]. Despite the significant larger size compared to ZFNs, TALENs have exhibited advantages including higher specificity, lower cytotoxicity, and simpler design due to the one-RVD-to-one-base recognition code, thus were also considered as a potential platform in the field of mitochondrial gene therapy [ 117 ].

#### Full-length mitoTALENs

The first mitoTALENs applied to research was constructed by the removal of nuclear localization signal and the employment of SOD2 or COX8A MTS and a 3’ UTR from a mitochondrial gene (Fig. 3 D). The targeting effect was analyzed in cybrid cells harboring heteroplasmic deletion (m.8483_13459del4977) or m. 14,459 G > A mutation in the MT-ND6 gene. Transfection of mitoTALENs targeted to the upstream and downstream regions of the deletion sequence resulted in a permanent reduction in mtDNA harboring deletion mutations. Meanwhile, monomer designed to identify point mutation sites also induced the heteroplasmy shift cooperated with the other monomer bound to the complementary common sequence adjacent to the mutation [ 32 ]. Shortly thereafter, the heteroplasmy-shifting function of mitoTALENs was validated in the germline. Following mRNA injection of specifically designed mitoTALENs, the elimination of NZB mtDNA was observed in NZB/BALB mouse oocytes, along with the clearance of m.14459 G > A and m.9176T > C mutations in MII mouse oocytes fused with osteosarcoma cybrid cells harboring these mutations [ 119 ]. Similar results were also observed in the porcine oocytes harboring m.3243A > G mutation, implied the ability of mitoTALENs to prevent the germline transmission of mutant mtDNA [ 120 ]. In addition, the function of mitoTALENs was further proved in mitochondrial disease patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells (MiPSCs) in this study. The m.3243A > G mutation in MiPSCs was also successfully eliminated with no off-target mutagenesis in mtDNA or nuclear genome [ 120 ]. Furthermore, the metabolic profiles of differentiated neuronal progenitor cells from MiPSCs were rescued, suggested the therapeutic potential in the treatment of heteroplasmic mitochondrial disorders.

#### Short mitoTALENs

There were also efforts to reduce the size of mtTALENs to reduce the difficulty of packaging. One approach was to design smaller TALE domains according to the hypothesis that the lower complexity of mtDNA might allow for shorter TALE binding sequences without significant non-specific cleavage. Compared to the conventional (15.5 and 9.5 RVDs) mitoTALENs, the shorter (10.5 and 7.5 RVDs) ones still showed significant elimination effect towards m.8344A > G mutation in cybrid cells [ 121 ]. This work was confirmed by another study in which shorter monomer (8.5 RVDs) binding to the normal DNA sequences resulted in similar heteroplasmy shift of m.5024C > T mutation in cells and no significant mtDNA depletion compared to the conventional one (15.5 RVDs) [ 122 ].

On the other hand, the employment of smaller nuclease was also attempted. The fusion of mitochondrial targeting TALE with monomeric GIY-YIG homing nuclease from T4 phage (I-TevI), which recognizes and cleaves CNNN G (mitoTev-TALE), was found to be able to bind and cleave mtDNA with m.8344A > G mutation in cybrid cells (Fig. 3 E). However, the observation of mtDNA depletion in WT cells suggested significant off-target effect, probably caused by the monomeric pattern of the nuclease [ 123 ]. Further research is needed on off-target dose-dependent effects of mitoTev-TALE, as well as the modification of nucleases to enhance specificity.

#### In vivo application of mitoTALENs

The successful in vivo application of mitoTALENs has also been reported after the generation of the mouse model with heteroplasmic mitochondrial tRNA Ala gene mutation [ 114 ]. MitoTALEN monomers targeting mutant m.5024C > T allele (9.5 RVDs) and the adjacent WT sequence (8.5 RVDs) were packaged into AAV9 separately, fused with the modified heterodimeric FokI nuclease domains [ 33 ]. The shift of heteroplasmy and recovery of tRNA Ala levels were observed following the injection of particles into the right tibialis anterior muscle (1.0–1.5 × 10^12 vg/monomer) of the mouse model, relative to the left leg control. These findings were consistent with results from systemic administration via retro-orbital and intraperitoneal injections at the same dosage [ 33 ]. The absence of total mtDNA decrease confirmed the autonomous replication ability of mitochondrial genome after rapid degradation of linearized mtDNA damaged by DSBs [ 48 , 122 ].

A more recent study has further demonstrated that mitoTALENs, delivered via the neurotropic AAV-PHP.eB and driven by the neuron-specific synapsin promoter, could eliminate mutant mtDNA within the CNS [ 124 ]. Intravenous administration of the viral particles (2–3 × 10^13 vg/kg) showed brain expression of mitoTALENs, as well as the decreased m.5024C > T mtDNA levels and increased tRNA Ala levels in the brain of mouse model, with no overall variation in total mtDNA copy number. Additionally, no mitoTALENs related insertions and deletions (INDELS) and single nucleotide variants (SNVs) were detected using multiple methods.

#### Novel animal model constructed by mitoTALEN

A notable limitation of mtDNA gene therapy in clinical research is the scarcity of effective animal models for the preclinical research of mitochondrial disorders. Compared with nuclear transgenesis, constructing animal disease models harboring mtDNA mutations is far more challenging.

Beside traditional approaches, including nuclear allotopic expression of mutated mtDNA [ 70 , 71 , 125 , 126 ], phenotypic screening using PolgA mutant animals [ 114 ], and xenograft mouse model utilizing cybrid cell lines derived from human tumors [ 34 ], an increasing number of novel ones are being developed via more efficient methods. A recent study successfully engineered defined mtDNA deletions using a simple, efficient, and tractable system comprising mitochondrial end-joining (mito-EJ) machinery and targeted endonucleases [ 127 ]. The mito-EJ system, containing the DNA-binding proteins Ku and ligase D, was shown to ligate broken mtDNA ends cleaved by mitochondrial-targeting nucleases in cell models deficient in MGME1 activity [ 49 ], thereby establishing a novel method for modeling disease-associated mtDNA deletions across cell types. Further combination of this system with iPSC and embryo manipulation techniques may enable the construction of animal models harboring such mutations.

### MitoARCUS

MitoARCUS was a recently developed nuclease based on the I-CreI meganuclease which originates from the chloroplast genome of Chlamydomonas reinhardtii [ 128 ]. The engineered I-CreI monomer could be designed to recognize a specific 22-bp DNA sequence, activate its enzymatic activity, and induce DSBs [ 129 ] (Fig. 3 F). In addition to the relatively small size (1092 bp compared to approximately 3000 bp of TALEN), it is promising for mtDNA editing.

The specific elimination of specific mtDNA mutation in mouse model has been detected using mitoARCUS [ 110 ]. Fused with Cox8/Su9 MTS, the engineered nuclease, designed to target the 22-bp sequence containing the m.5024C > T mutation at the eighth nucleotide, was packaged into AAV9. Systemic administration of the particles at the dosages ranged 4–6.67 × 10^13 vg/kg showed effective hereroplasmy shift in the liver and skeletal muscle ensued with restoration of tRNA Ala levels.

A more recent study highlighted the potential off-target effect of nuclear genome by high dosages of mitoARCUS in a cell model, and demonstrated that the additional construction of a NES could circumvent such off-target editing.

Furthermore, the therapeutic potential of AAV delivered mitoARCUS containing both MTS and NES in a novel m.3243A > G xenograft mouse model was verified [ 34 ]. A significantly higher level of WT mtDNA was detected in the engrafted human tumor with the systemic administration of 5 × 10^13 vg/kg AAV9, while no mtDNA depletion was observed in mouse liver or heart, despite the abundance of AAV copies in these tissues, suggesting the specificity and safety of the modified mitoARCUS and its clinical application potential. However, a relatively low heteroplasmy shift was observed, probably attributed to either poor tumor vascularization or insufficient tissue specificity of the delivery tool, highlighting the need to further optimize this model.

MitoARCUS was a recently developed nuclease based on the I-CreI meganuclease which originates from the chloroplast genome of Chlamydomonas reinhardtii [ 128 ]. The engineered I-CreI monomer could be designed to recognize a specific 22-bp DNA sequence, activate its enzymatic activity, and induce DSBs [ 129 ] (Fig. 3 F). In addition to the relatively small size (1092 bp compared to approximately 3000 bp of TALEN), it is promising for mtDNA editing.

The specific elimination of specific mtDNA mutation in mouse model has been detected using mitoARCUS [ 110 ]. Fused with Cox8/Su9 MTS, the engineered nuclease, designed to target the 22-bp sequence containing the m.5024C > T mutation at the eighth nucleotide, was packaged into AAV9. Systemic administration of the particles at the dosages ranged 4–6.67 × 10^13 vg/kg showed effective hereroplasmy shift in the liver and skeletal muscle ensued with restoration of tRNA Ala levels.

A more recent study highlighted the potential off-target effect of nuclear genome by high dosages of mitoARCUS in a cell model, and demonstrated that the additional construction of a NES could circumvent such off-target editing.

### Challenges and prospects of mitochondrial-targeted nucleases

While these basic research findings have demonstrated advantages, several challenges must be considered prior to the clinical translation of these mitochondrial-targeted nuclease strategies.

#### Limitations of mtZFN

The efficiency of designing ZFNs that target specific sequences is very low due to the absence of a consistent correspondence between ZF motifs and bases [ 130 ]. In addition, although the reengineered obligate heterodimer variants of the FokI cleavage domain reduced the off-target activity, cytotoxicity remains a concern due to the potential formation of homodimers within monomers and unintended targeting caused by interactions between adjacent fingers [ 131 , 132 ]. The selectively diminished activity of the engineered FokI catalytic domain at low-affinity off-target sites, when combined with the reduced non-specific DNA binding affinity of mutagenic zinc fingers as reported in a nuclear ZFN modification study, may also prove suitable for mtZFN applications [ 133 ].

#### Limitations of mitoTALENs

In contrast, as noted above, TALENs exhibit lower off-target activity and cytotoxicity due to the high specificity of their RVDs [ 117 ]. However, their relatively large size remains a barrier to in vivo delivery. Thus, despite the positive correlation between longer TALE array lengths and higher specificity, shorter arrays are preferred for mtDNA targeting owing to their lower complexity [ 121 , 122 ]. An additional potential benefit of this strategy is reduced mismatches, as longer arrays also increase mismatch tolerance [ 117 ].

Furthermore, conventional TALEs require the 5’ end of the target sequence to be a thymine to maintain activity [ 134 ]. While this requirement facilitates the recognition of T-gain mutations, it restricts TALENs from targeting a broader range of sequences. Notably, evolved novel TALE N-terminal domains that accommodate any base at the 5’ terminal may enable targeting of more types of heteroplasmic mtDNA mutations [ 135 ].

#### Limitations of mitoARUCS

MitoARCUS is the smallest nuclease among the three, with a total length of only 1400 bp including the MTS and NES, making it a promising tool for clinical translation [ 34 ]. However, similar to ZFN, each new target sequence requires a novel enzyme, and re-engineering of I-CreI remains challenging. Additionally, although mitoARCUS could theoretically target any 22-bp DNA sequence, its enzymatic activity is constrained by the structure formed by the middle 4-bp region [ 136 ]. Specifically, the central two bases preferably follow a pyrimidine-purine order, a consideration that must be incorporated into the design process.

#### Off-target risks

There is no evidence which can rule out the possibility that these mitochondrial-targeted nucleases cleaved a part of the WT mtDNA owing to the quick degradation of linearized mtDNA. This is particularly evident when the nucleases were monomerized and also fused with monomeric mtDNA targeting elements [ 109 , 123 ].

Fortunately, owing to the multicopy nature and autonomous replicative capacity of mtDNA [ 48 , 137 ], WT mtDNA copy numbers increase over time following the elimination of mutant genomes. Even in models with a high degree of heteroplasmy, only limited adverse events associated with the temporary degradation of mtDNA copies have been observed [ 32 , 122 ].

In addition to the fact that a complete shift to 100% WT mtDNA is unnecessary for improving mitochondrial physiological function, and that unsaturated binding of nucleases to their target sequences can reduce off-target effects, the well-controlled expression levels of these engineered nucleases during clinical application may be possible to guarantee the safe and effective therapeutic outcomes [ 113 ].

#### Insufficient universality

On the other hand, there is insufficient evidence that any of these nuclease strategies is effective for the elimination of all heteroplasmic mtDNA mutation. For example, despite multiple evidences indicating that m.8993T > G mtDNA could be eliminated by both mtREs and mtZFNs, mitoTALENs were found to be unable to effectively target this mutation [ 113 ]. Further studies should be carried out to detect suitable mutation candidates for each therapeutic tool.

#### Delivery issues

Consistent with the allotopic expression of mtDNA, there are also limitations in the delivery system. For example, immune response has been detected with the systemic delivery dosage at 5 × 10^12 vg/monomer in mouse models [ 116 ]. Not to mention that the success in mouse models could not dilute the further investigation of the safe AAV titers at human doses [ 30 ]. Novel strategies such as immunogenicity reduction design of the viral particles, MTS-AAV and non-viral vectors with high tissue specificity and low immunogenicity should also be analyzed in this part.

### DdCBEs

DdCBE is the first tool capable of precise precise mtDNA base editing [ 47 ]. This paired system consists of two MTS-linked TALE arrays, which target complementary sequences upstream and downstream of the editing site. Split DddA halves are attached to the C-termini of these arrays, and an additional UGI protein is fused to the DddA half associated with the N-terminal portion.

#### Canonical DdCBEs

The first DddA enzyme utilized in this context was recently identified from Burkholderia cenocepacia (BcDddA). It exhibits deaminase activity toward cytosine in double-stranded DNA, preferentially targeting 5’-T C sequence contexts, thereby enabling the induction of C-to-T mutations. To address the cytotoxicity associated with the intact BcDddA enzyme, it was split at residues G1333 or G1397. When guided by mito-TALE arrays, the split variants reconstituted deamination activity on either mtDNA strand within a 14–18 base-pair editing window between the TALE array-targeted sequences. The added UGI impeded the uracil excision after cytosine deamination probably by mitochondrial UDG1 inhibition and promote the incorporation of A opposite U during mtDNA replication, and thereby further enhanced editing efficiencies [ 142 ] (Fig. 5 A).

Preliminary data on BcDdCBEs showed editing efficiencies ranging from 4.6% to 49%, which varied based on structural differences among DdCBEs and the positions of target sequences. These findings revealed that the G1397-split and G1333-split DdCBEs exhibit distinct preferences for T C motifs, the former favors TCs located 4–7 nucleotides upstream of the 3’ end of the spacing region, while the latter prefers TCs within 4–10 nucleotides from the 5’ end of this region. Evidence also supports efficient mtDNA base editing in non-dividing cells, which relies on the independently regulated replication of mtDNA. Furthermore, the m.11922 G > A mutation induced by DdCBE within MT-ND4 impairs the enzymatic activity of complex I, thereby raising the possibility of a novel mitochondrial disease model created by mtDNA editing strategy.

However, optimizations are still needed for this canonical version to improve its targeting range and specificity. These include lifting the restriction of T C sequence preference through modified BcDddA and other orthologs, enhancing the specificity of deamination site recognition, and designing more specific TALE arrays.

#### BcDddA variants

Through the analysis of the crystal structure of the BcDddA-dsDNA complex, and the phage-assisted non-continuous and continuous evolution, improvements in the editing efficiency of DdCBEs have been achieved using two evolved BcDddA variants [ 143 , 144 ] (Fig. 5 A). The evolved DddA6 (Q1310R + S1330I + T1380I + T1413I) demonstrated an average 3.4-fold enhancement in T C editing activity compared to the WT DddA. In contrast, DddA11 (S1330I, A1341V, N1342S, E1370K, T1380I and T1413I) exhibited an expanded targeting range, with broadened editing compatibility for H C sequences (H = A, C or T) while retaining incompatibility with G C sequences in human cells. However, the evolved enzymes were reported to induce more bystander mutations within the spacing regions [ 145 ].

Furthermore, based on the theory that DddA may render dsDNA accessible to other single-stranded DNA (ssDNA)-specific cytosine deaminases, the ssDNA deaminase AID from Xenopus laevis was further fused with DddA11 (T1391A) to generate an optimized DdCBE (DddA11–T1391A-xAID-NES2) (Fig. 5 B). Compared with DddA11 (T1391A), this optimized variant exhibited higher activity and broader sequence compatibility, manifested by its ability to edit G C sites, along with a further enhanced efficiency in editing H C sites [ 35 ].

Efforts were also made to screen DdCBE combinations with other DddA mutations, BcDddAs with left G1333C (L1333C) and right G1333N (R1333N) pair were identified to mediate C to T conversion at A C sites with higher editing preference and lower bystander editing effects. The editing capability of the screened pair was further validated by generating rat models carrying G007A or G11714 /5A mtDNA mutations. These models exhibit distinct cardiomyopathy phenotypes, with minimal off-target effects [ 146 ]. Similarly, charge reversal through K1402D or K1402E in the split BcDddA have also been reported to reduce off-target effects by approximately 400-fold compared to the canonical version [ 147 ].

On the other hand, the research on the development of high-fidelity DdCBEs (HiFi-DdCBEs) was also carried out. To prevent the spontaneous assembly of split DddA halves independently of TALE-DNA interactions, a series of mutations were introduced at the dimer interface. These mutations, designed to potentially impair assembly capacity, were informed by the three-dimensional structural insights of the DddA complex bound to its Ddd inhibitor [ 145 ]. Evidence has shown that introducing either K1389A, T1391A, V1411A, or T1413A mutations into the G1397-split BcDddA significantly suppresses non-specific dimerization. These mutants exhibit high activity only when paired with WT DdCBE partners. Similar results were observed for the K1389A, T1391A, or V1393A mutations in G1333-split BcDddA. Additionally, the editing window was found to be narrowed due to the enhanced cytosine selectivity in HiFi-DdCBEs harboring some of these individual mutations, which further improves editing specificity.

It is also encouraging that DddA6 and DddA11, when modified using the HiFi-DdCBE strategy, retain enhanced activity and a broadened targeting range, while largely avoiding off-target base editing [ 143 , 145 ] (Fig. 5 A).

#### DddA homologs

In addition to the improvement of BcDddA, other homologs were also screened on the purpose of improving editing efficiency, specificity and universality (Fig. 5 A). Several DddA homologs were identified with different split sites and context preferences [ 148 – 152 ].

Among them, the orthologs containing SPKK related motifs showed relatively high deamination activity, such as DddA homolog from Simiaoa sunii (Ddd_Ss) [ 151 ]. DdCBE_Ss was found to exhibit high activity and preferences for the D C sequence context (D = A, T or G). Moreover, introducing the T77I and T110I mutations further enhanced its editing efficiency.

Another dsDNA deaminase originated from Roseburia intestinalis (riDddA) was identified at the same year and catalyzed C-to-T editing at N C ( N = A, T, C or G) targets [ 152 ]. The DdCBE constructed with riDddA and further modified by the fusing of Rta at the C terminus of UGI was named mitoCBE2.1 by the researchers. It improved mtDNA editing efficiencies by up to 1.7-fold compared to the version in the absence of Rta, probably due to the chromatin accessibility regulated by this transactivation domain [ 153 ].

Subsequently, the same research team developed mitoCBE3.1, which exhibits enhanced editing activity, utilizing another DddA ortholog derived from Burkholderia gladioli (BgDddA) [ 148 ]. MitoCBE3.2 was further evolved by introducing six mutations (S1328I, A1339V, N1340S, E1368K, T1378I, and T1411I) into BgDddA that were referred to BcDddA11 and enhanced the editing efficiency at N C sites [ 143 ] (Fig. 5 C). The latest version exhibited an exceptionally high editing activity reaching up to 99.2%, providing a potent tool for the facilitation of mitochondrial disease modeling and potential mutation correction.

The latest version exhibited an exceptionally high editing activity reaching up to 99.2%, providing a potent tool for the facilitation of mitochondrial disease modeling and potential mutation correction.


#### Progress in TALE arrays engineering

The specificity of TALE-DNA interaction was another necessary condition for successful mtDNA base editing. Computational tools have been reported to enhance the design efficiency of TALE arrays, owing to their ability to rapidly identify potential target sites for base editing within the mitochondrial genomes of humans and zebrafish [ 154 ]. In addition, streamlined TALE assembly system such as FusX was also applied for the TALE guided base editing (FusXTBE), providing a cost-effective approach for the manufacture of DdCBEs [ 154 , 155 ].

On the other hand, the flexibility of TALE array to target mtDNA is constrained by the requirement of a 5’-T in binding sites, which restricts them to over 150 loci within the human mitochondrial genome [ 156 ]. Building on a previously developed engineered TALE N-terminal domain capable of accommodating any 5’ base [ 135 ], unconstrained αDdCBEs have been generated (Fig. 5 D). Additionally, their compatibility with engineered DddA variants such as DddA6 and DddA11 has been demonstrated. Analysis of editing outcomes for MT-ND2, MT-ND4, and MT-CO1 revealed that αDdCBEs tend to perform comparably to 5’-T-compliant DdCBEs while outperforming 5’-T-noncompliant variants. Consequently, they exhibit greater design flexibility than canonical DdCBEs [ 156 ].

#### Novel disease models created by DdCBEs

As soon as the development of mtDNA base editing complexes, they became ideal tools for the creation of novel animal models that simulating human mitochondrial diseases.

Numerous models were created by the zygote injection of DdCBE pair mRNAs, including mouse, rat and zebrafish [ 146 , 157 – 160 ]. However, none of these animal models have exhibited high levels of mtDNA editing. This observation underscores the need to optimize both in vivo DdCBE base editing strategies and the animal breeding process following editing.

To achieve precise ablation of gene expression in the mouse mitochondrial genome, a library of base editors has been designed to introduce premature stop codons for all mitochondrial protein-coding genes. In vivo editing of mouse mtDNA using this MitoKO library, specifically targeting the m.8069 G > A nonsense mutation in Atp6, exhibited low efficiency (3% to 10%) in F0 offspring. However, by the F4 generation, higher heteroplasmy levels ( > 50%) were achieved. Notably, the complex V subcomplexes in these F4 mice showed characteristics corresponding to those in patients harboring truncating MT-Atp6 mutations [ 161 , 162 ].

The suspension of mtDNA replication during preimplantation development may also contribute to the low base-editing efficiency. Consequently, when mRNAs encoding DdCBE pairs were injected into oocytes of secondary follicles in animal models, efficient G-to-A conversion was achieved at a hard-to-edit site, offering a viable option for efficient manipulation of mtDNA sites in the germline [ 163 ].

#### Preclinical researches with DdCBEs

An in vitro proof-of-concept study investigating the feasibility and efficacy of DdCBEs for treating mitochondrial genetic diseases first utilized a panel of screened DdCBEs to correct the homoplasmic m. 4300A > G mutation from iPSC derived from a patient with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The screened DdCBE pair showed an 80% editing efficiency and over 2% bystander editing in the clones harbored G4300A conversion with frequencies over 60%. All the edited bases remained after 20 times of passage and restored the tRNA Ile level and mitochondrial function [ 164 ].

Despite these advancements, base editing still has a long way to go before it can achieve therapeutic purposes due to limitations in delivery and editing efficiency in vivo, as well as issues such as bystander editing and concerns regarding off-target effects.

The preliminary in vivo study analyzed the editing efficiency of the screened DdCBE pair targeting the glycine 40 codon (GGA) in mouse MT-Nd3. The systemic administration of the editor delivered by AAV9.45 serotype at the dosage of 1 × 10^12 vg/monomer in adult mice showed robust editing on both cytosine (10–20%) in the cardiac tissue 24 weeks post injection, while a higher efficiency was detected in neonate group at 3-weeks post-injection. However, off-targets on mtDNA were observed in this study, especially when the on-target modification was high [ 165 ].

Similarly, in a study aimed at treating animal models with m.5024C > T mutation, DdCBE pairs were screened to induce a compensatory m.5081 G > A conversion in order to restore the secondary structure of the tRNA Ala aminoacyl stem. Retro-orbital injection of AAV9 vectors packaging the selected DdCBE pair led to a dose-dependent restoration of total mt-tRNA Ala levels in the heart and muscle. However, the highest dose (3.1 × 10^12 vg/mouse) also triggered severe adverse effects, which were attributed to dose-dependent off-target editing of mtDNA [ 166 ].

Taken together, these data suggest that while DdCBEs have shown promising potential as a therapeutic tool for mitochondrial disorders, further improvements in precision and the development of a robust dose-control strategy are necessary prior to clinical investigation.

### MitoZFDs

Similarly, the relatively smaller mitoZFDs composed of MTS and NES fused custom-designed ZF protein, split DddA and UGI also exhibited mtDNA cytosine editing ability [ 138 ] (Fig. 6 A). The off-target editing on mtDNA was also observed in mitoZFDs with a dosage dependent manner but was improved by the mutation of R(−5)Q in each ZF, indicating its potential in mtDNA gene therapy [ 133 ].

Another group developed a similar complex named ZF-DdCBE through a more complex engineering process and successfully delivered the tool with single AAV9 vector into the heart, liver and skeletal muscle in mouse (Fig. 6 B). The length of ZF arrays, the orientation of DddA splitting, single-point mutations in DddA (N18K, N20E, P25A, P25K), and alternative ZF scaffolds have all been shown to contribute to improvements in editing efficiency. It is recommended that these components be modified according to different targets to optimize ZF-DdCBE, though this ZF-based tool still exhibits a relatively high off-target effect [ 167 ].

Furthermore, the preference for C-to-T conversion patterns differed between DdCBEs and mitoZFDs when targeting the same sequence. This suggests that mitoZFDs and DdCBEs can be used in combination to generate unique mutation patterns in mitochondrial mtDNA [ 138 ].

### TALEDs

Of the 130 confirmed pathogenic mutations in human mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), only 31 could theoretically be corrected using C-to-T editing tools. In contrast, an additional 51 targets could be rescued through A-to-G conversions, including those responsible for LHON, mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, myopathy, encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and MELAS, and Leigh syndrome (Table 1 ). TALEDs, a group of TALE-fused adenine deaminases, was reported to induce such transitions in mtDNA.

#### The components of TALEDs

Similar to DdCBEs, TALEDs also contain custom-designed TALE DNA-binding arrays with MTS and/or NES, and a cytotoxicity-free, full-length BcDddA variant or split BcDddA. The key difference is that TALEDs lack a UGI component. Instead, they are fused with an engineered deoxyadenosine deaminase derived from Escherichia coli TadA (TadA8e) [ 139 ]. TadA8e is much less efficient in catalyzing adenine deamination in dsDNA than in ssDNA and requires DddA assistance to exert its activity on mtDNA.

During the adenosine editing, DddA was demonstrated to induce C to U deamination within the spacer region, followed by uracil(s) removal and AP site generation depending on UDGs in the absence of UGI. These AP sites are then cleaved by APE1 or via spontaneous DNA breakage, leaving a ssDNA sequence as the target of TadA8e [ 168 ]. Given the underlying principle of inducing ssDNA generation, the DddA component in TALEDs typically does not require a strict TC context at the target site.

#### Different configurations of TALEDs

Based on these components, three distinct TALEN structures were generated. The monomeric TALEDs (mTALEDs) consist of a single TALE array fused with TadA8e, followed by the full-length E1347A BcDddA variant [ 139 ] (Fig. 7 A). The E1347A in BcDddA was designed and demonstrated to reduce the cellular toxicity of the monomeric DddA in an earlier study [ 169 ]. Alternatively, the E1347A BcDddA and TadA8e could be fused to separate TALE arrays, resulting in the generation of dimeric TALEDs (dTALEDs) (Fig. 7 B). In addition, the G1397-split BcDddA variants could be fused to a third split TALEDs architecture (sTALEDs), with either of the split segment of the TALE array followed by a deaminase domain (Fig. 7 C).

These three TALEDs exhibit distinct patterns in their preferences for adenosine editing positions and shifts in editing windows, indicating that these tools can be selected based on desired editing patterns [ 139 ].

#### TadA8e variants in TALEDs

Unlike the dsDNA cytosine base editors (CBEs), adenosine deaminases such as TadA8e exhibit enzymatic activity toward RNA substrates. Consequently, TALEDs have been found to induce transcriptome-wide off-target effects and unwanted cytotoxicity, which cause cell growth arrest and impaired embryonic development [ 139 , 170 ].

To address these drawbacks, TadA8e variants carrying the V28R and R111S mutations were identified. Compared to the original sTALED or sTALED-V106W (a variant previously reported to reduce off-target RNA editing), these mutants display only minimal RNA off-target edits [ 170 , 171 ]. Furthermore, these engineered TALEDs with site-specific V28R and R111S mutations at the TadA8e substrate-binding site could also reduce bystander editing and off-target editing in mtDNA, probably due to the fine-tune of the ssDNA deaminase activity [ 170 ].

In addition, considering the fact that clinical manifestation of mtDNA diseases were associated with high degree of heteroplasmy [ 15 , 16 ], and that adenine base editors was usually less efficiently compared to cytosine editors [ 47 , 139 ], recent studies have explored laboratory evolution as a means to further enhance the editing efficiency of TadA8e [ 172 ]. The L145 residue was found to be critical for TadA8e to distinguish between adenine and cytosine substrates. Notably, editors incorporating TadA8e-A142R/L145W (−RW) (eTd1-mtABEs) induced higher A-to-G conversion rates at the same targets compared to sTALED, particularly for adenines following G or A, which were disfavored by the original version. Additionally, the introduction of the V28A mutation into TadA8e-RW preserved the specificity of ssDNA deamination [ 172 ] (Fig. 7 A).

#### DddA variants in TALEDs

With the continuous understanding of the A to G editing mechanism in mtDNA, more efforts were made for the higher editing efficiency and lower off-target.

As the tool for ssDNA induction through deamination on TALED, the replacement of DddA with DddA6 (sTALED6), which showed a higher editing efficiency during C to T editing in DdCBE [ 143 ], also induced higher A to G editing efficiencies up to 40% frequencies than the original sTALED (less than 20%) [ 35 , 168 ].

On the other hand, the eTd-mtABEs with the DddA6 variant (eTd6-mtABEs) was reported to induced up to 76% A-to-G edits at a MT-ND5 site. This represents an average 4.3-fold increase compared to sTALED and a 1.6-fold enhancement relative to eTd1-mtABEs [ 172 ].

Beyond sTALED, mTALED and dTALEDs can also be engineered by employing nontoxic full-length DddA variants, such as DddA_GSVG (S1326G, G1348S, A1398V, S1418G) and DddA_E1347A [ 169 ] (Fig. 7 A, B).

#### Other modifications in TALEDs

It has been found that the additional hUDG fused onto sTALED6 further enhances editing efficiency by facilitating uracil excision, resulting in the generation of esTALED6. However, the efficiency improvement is accompanied by a notable increase in mtDNA-wide A-to-G off-target editing, a phenomenon observed during the generation of a m.13514A > G mutation cell model (72.45% editing efficiency vs. up to 53.65% bystander editing). In addition, it should be noted that fusing hUDG does not further enhance the efficiency of mTALED6_GSVG, likely due to spatial constraints [ 168 ].

To address this off-target issue, introducing the TadA8e V28 mutations into esTALED6 (yielding esTALED6R) (48.88% editing efficiency vs. up to 10.89% bystander editing) reduced such off-target events, highlights a promising direction for future engineering of TALED-based systems [ 168 , 170 ] (Fig. 7 C, D).

### Base editing independent of DddA

The development of DdCEBs and TALEDs enabled the precise base editing in mtDNA. However, both of them rely on DddA, a dsDNA deaminase, to deaminate any cytosines on both strands to induce C to U transition or create ssDNA [ 47 , 168 ], leading to the lack of DNA target strand specificity. Recent advances in selective nickases, enzymes capable of generating targeted ssDNA lesions, have facilitated the development of novel strand-selective DNA editing tools, which incorporate single-stranded DNA deaminases.

#### MtCyDENT

MtCyDENT was such a tool developed recently for the strand specificity deamination of cytosines [ 140 ]. In this approach, MTS TALE arrays were fused with FokI nickases, generated by introducing a catalytically deactivating mutation (D450A) into either monomer, to induce nicking on the complementary strand. Trex2 exonuclease was fused to the N-terminus of TALE arrays linked to the active domain of the FokI nickase to facilitate further nucleotide digestion around the nick, whereas newly identified ssDNA-specific cytosine deaminases such as Sdd7 or Sdd3 were fused to the equivalent position on the other array [ 173 ]. Additionally, UGIs were fused to the C-terminus of FokI-L and the N-terminus of FokI-R, respectively [ 140 ] (Fig. 8 A).

Preliminary data of mtCyDENT in cell models showed a base editing efficiency of around 20% in several mtDNA targets with more than 90% of these editing sites located on the selective DNA strand. The small number of editing sites on the complementary strand could be a result of enzyme activity leakage from D450A monomer [ 140 ]. Further study and optimization of CyDENT should be continued to evaluate the efficiency and off-target effects in different models and targets with variable CyDENT components combinations.

#### mitoBes

Another research team developed mitoBEs by combining distinct nickases and deaminases. In this strategy, one TALE array is fused with a nickase, which creates a nick to transiently generate ssDNA without the involvement of exonucleases. Meanwhile, the other TALE array, fused with an ssDNA deaminase, mediates base editing on the intact ssDNA [ 36 , 141 ].

#### mitoABEs

Specifically, mitoABEs are generated by fusing TALE arrays with two key components: a deaminase variant such as TadA8e-V106W and a nickase, either MutH or Nt.BspD6I (C) (Fig. 8 B).

Among these nickase components, MutH acts as a sequence-specific nickase that recognizes the 5’-GATC motif. Its sequence preference is shaped by both the structural pattern of the target sequences and the spatial distance between the TALE binding site and the 5’-GATC motif [ 174 ]. In addition, MutH*, the variant that containing E91A and F94A mutations largely expanded the editing range by the recognizing the broader 5’-GATN motif [ 175 ].

By contrast, Nt.BspD6I(C), which originated from the C-terminal cleavage domain of Nt.BspD6I [ 176 ], showed no sequence-context constraint. Unlike MutH, it generates nicks specifically on the binding DNA strand of its fused TALE, offering flexibility in target site selection.

Notably, despite their high mtDNA editing specificity, multiple reports indicate that mitoABEs induce significant transcriptome-wide off-target effects during editing. This finding highlights the requirement of further precision optimizations of the base editor, primarily by modifying the deaminase component [ 177 ].

Based on the insights from TALED optimization, TadA8e-V106W-V28F was substituted in mtioABEs. This modification further enhanced the editing efficiency while reduced transcriptome-level off-target effects to near the negative control level. Additionally, the employment of the aforementioned TadA8e-RW/V28A variant resulted in a 2-fold average increase in editing frequencies for mitoABE MutH (from 24% to 47% on average at a MT-ND4 site) and a 3.2-fold increase for mitoABE Nt.BspD6I(C) . Critically, editing efficiency at previously poorly edited positions rose from 9.2% to 39%, a improvement that underscores the critical importance of deaminase optimization in advancing the clinical application of mitoBEs [ 172 ].

By contrast, Nt.BspD6I(C), which originated from the C-terminal cleavage domain of Nt.BspD6I [ 176 ], showed no sequence-context constraint. Unlike MutH, it generates nicks specifically on the binding DNA strand of its fused TALE, offering flexibility in target site selection.

Notably, despite their high mtDNA editing specificity, multiple reports indicate that mitoABEs induce significant transcriptome-wide off-target effects during editing. This finding highlights the requirement of further precision optimizations of the base editor, primarily by modifying the deaminase component [ 177 ].

Based on the insights from TALED optimization, TadA8e-V106W-V28F was substituted in mtioABEs. This modification further enhanced the editing efficiency while reduced transcriptome-level off-target effects to near the negative control level. Additionally, the employment of the aforementioned TadA8e-RW/V28A variant resulted in a 2-fold average increase in editing frequencies for mitoABE MutH (from 24% to 47% on average at a MT-ND4 site) and a 3.2-fold increase for mitoABE Nt.BspD6I(C) . Critically, editing efficiency at previously poorly edited positions rose from 9.2% to 39%, a improvement that underscores the critical importance of deaminase optimization in advancing the clinical application of mitoBEs [ 172 ].


#### MitoCBEs

MitoCBEs were constructed using the strategy similar to that of mtioABEs, with the only difference being that their deaminase component was replaced by rAPOBEC1 fused to UGI [ 178 ] (Fig. 8 C). Additionally, monomeric versions of both mitoCBEs and mitoABEs were also successfully developed by fusing nickases and deaminases to the C-terminus of a single TALE array, regardless of which enzyme is positioned at the N-terminus. This compact design yields smaller editors that are easier to deliver [ 175 ] (Fig. 8 D).

Notably, while mitoCBEs exhibit higher editing efficiency compared to mitoABEs, they induce elevated off-target editing events in mtDNA. Critically, this increase in off-target effects is independent of the TALE array specificity and is likely caused by random deamination mediated by rAPOBEC1 [ 177 ].

In address this rAPOBEC1-associated off-target issue, APOBEC1 was replaced with CBE6d, a cytosine deaminase derived from TadA family enzymes [ 179 ]. This modification not only maintained high editing efficiency but also narrowed the editing window and minimized mtDNA off-target effects during cytosine base editing in mtDNA [ 177 ] (Fig. 8 C).

Building on these optimizations, preliminary analyses of these optimized mitoBEs demonstrated high editing efficiencies (average ~20% in mtDNA) for at least 8 pathogenic point mutations in mouse cells. Furthermore, a single-base-editing mouse model harboring the MT-Nd5 m.12784A > G mutation was successfully generated with this system, which exhibited disease-corresponding phenotypes. This work not only validates a highly efficient and precise strategy for constructing mitochondrial disease models but also highlights the broad therapeutic potential of optimized mitoBEs, though their clinical applicability still awaits confirmation of in vivo delivery efficacy and editing specificity across diverse pathogenic point mutations.

### Current challenges and prospects of base editing for mtDNA

These encouraging progress in mtDNA base editing studies shed light on the development of precise and curative therapeutics for mtDNA related diseases. Nevertheless, numerous challenges remain in optimizing editing efficiency, accuracy, and safety, factors that currently constrain their clinical investigation and translation.

#### Editing efficiency

Editing efficiency is critical for the treatment of mitochondrial disorders and the generation of disease models, primarily due to the high heteroplasmy or even homoplasmy of the pathogenic mutations [ 15 , 16 ]. This makes robust editing a prerequisite for achieving therapeutic effects or constructing accurate disease models, as low efficiency often fails to reverse pathogenic mtDNA dominance.

As noted above, the relatively large size of TALE array-guided editors has constrained their efficiency in in vivo delivery. Monomeric editors, such as mDdCBE (engineered using non-toxic, full-length DddA_GSVG or DddA_E1347A) [ 168 , 169 ], mTALED [ 139 , 168 ] (Fig. 7 A) and monomeric mitoBEs [ 175 ] (Fig. 8 D), can be packaged into a single AAV. However, rigorous and comprehensive analyses of their editing efficiency and off-target effects in animal models are needed prior to clinical studies.

Beyond optimizing editor size to improve delivery, innovative expression and delivery strategies have also been developed to enhance editing efficiency. For instance, studies have reported that editors encoded by mRNA and circular RNA yield higher efficiency and better cellular viability compared to DNA-mediated editing. Additionally, lipid nanoparticles have demonstrated effective delivery capacity for these RNA cargos, offering valuable references for the design of expression and delivery systems in future therapeutic tools [ 177 , 180 ].

Notably, although some of the recent reported editors show dramatic high editing efficiencies, their performance remains largely relied on target site selection. The same editor can yield efficiencies ranging from below 10% to higher than 70% across different target sites [ 168 , 172 , 177 ], indicating further structural studies on these different editor-DNA interactions may reveal the most suitable editing conditions and promote the improvement of editing efficiency.

In addition to these potential improvement directions, combining base editors with the current mitochondrially targeted nuclease tools could represent a viable supplementary strategy. By employing TALENs to degrade copies harboring these unwanted deleterious mutations, this approach may not only enhance editing efficiency by reducing competitive mutant mtDNA but also mitigate the unavoidable bystander editing products [ 181 ].

#### Bystander editing issues

Whether relying on DddA-dependent or independent base editors, bystander editing remains difficult to avoid due to the presence of multiple deamination sites within the editing window [ 141 , 160 , 182 ]. This inherent limitation poses a key challenge for the precision of mtDNA base editing, as unintended edits within the window can disrupt non-target sequences.

While this bystander editing is not necessarily a critical issue for generating animal disease model since the editing features and efficiencies of each embryo could be analyzed before transfer, it becomes a significant barrier for the application of disease therapies [ 172 , 177 ]. Efforts have been made to increase the accuracy through the evolution of deaminases that exhibit narrower editing windows and minimal mtDNA off-target effects, which have been mentioned above [ 143 , 145 , 170 – 172 , 177 ].

Beyond deaminase evolution, the TALE shifting strategy proposed in a study on aDdCBEs offers an additional approach to mitigate the risks [ 156 ]. This strategy uses different pairs of TALE arrays to target the same site, then compares the proportion of each C-to-G conversion within the editing window. By identifying TALE array binding sequences that minimize unintended edits for specific targets, this method can also facilitate the development of target-specific accessible databases. However, given the molecular mechanism of action of these deaminases, absolute elimination of the bystander editing may not be possible. Thus, the clinical benefits and risks of edited therapies need to be carefully evaluated before application.

#### Off-target issues

Off-target issues should always be strictly assessed during gene therapy. During mtDNA base editing, off-target editing within the mtDNA has been reported in both the DdCBE and mitoZFD approaches, with frequencies in a dose-dependent manner [ 166 , 167 , 182 ]. However, with the evolution of deaminases and the development of newer tools such as mtioBEs, mtDNA off-target events have been significantly reduced. Given the high threshold of pathogenic heteroplasmy, these residual off-target edits are unlikely to cause clinically significant adverse effects.

Beyond mtDNA, nuclear off-target editing has also been extensively documented in DdCBE studies [ 151 , 152 , 182 – 184 ]. A targeted study analyzing off-target editing patterns in the nuclear genome identified both TALE array sequence (TAS)-dependent off-target sites and TAS-independent sites. Notably, the TAS-independent sites strongly co-localized with binding sites of the transcription factor CTCF, though the underlying mechanism remains unclear [ 183 ]. The fusing of a NES sequence on DdCBE, along with the co-expression of the corresponding nuclear-localized DddIA, has been reported to minimize the nuclear off-target effects caused by DdCBE [ 181 , 183 ].

Despite these advances in mitigating off-target effects and claims of low off-target rates in recent reports, more reliable, standardized strategies for assessing off-target editing are still needed to quantify rates across different approaches. For instance, the Selict-seq strategy, designed to evaluate genome-wide off-target editing of nuclear ABEs by precisely identifying deoxyadenosine-to-deoxyinosine mutation sites, could potentially be adapted for detecting nuclear off-target effects during mtDNA editing, establishing consistent benchmarks for off-target risk assessment [ 185 ].

#### Recombination of mtDNA segments on nDNA

Another safety concern in mtDNA editing is the potential transfer of mtDNA segments into the nuclear genome. Notably, a several-fold increase in mitochondrial-nuclear DNA (mt-nDNA) fusions has been observed under conditions of mitochondrial stress and during mtDNA editing, likely attributable to DNA breaks that occur during the DNA repair process [ 186 ]. These mt-nDNA fusion segments are widely observed in somatic and cancer cells and may activate the transcription of oncogenes [ 187 , 188 ]. Given this oncogenic risk, such mt-nDNA recombination events should also be carefully assessed before the clinical translation of gene therapy for mtDNA.

#### Replacement therapy with edited mitochondria

As a proof-of-concept study, a novel mitochondrial replacement method combining mtDNA editing has been reported and may represent a promising solution to avoid the aforementioned off-target effects [ 148 ]. In this approach, mtDNA gene editing is first performed in easily editable donor cells, followed by the isolation and quality control of edited mitochondria, which are then transferred into recipient cells either in vitro or in vivo. This approach avoided any entrance of editing tools or free mtDNA segments into the recipient cells, thus making it suitable for the generation of mitochondrial disease model. Additionally, it is reasonable to speculate that corrected mitochondria derived from gene-edited patient-derived or maternal iPSCs could address the ethical concerns associated with mitochondrial replacement therapies [ 28 , 29 ].

## CRISPR system

Despite the remarkable success of RNA-guided CRISPR systems in nDNA editing, studies have shown that the importing of endogenous RNA into mammalian mitochondria is not essential for maintaining normal cellular function, raising concerns about the challenges of delivering the RNA component of CRISPR across the mitochondrial double membrane [ 64 ]. Nonetheless, studies still have attempted to adapt the CRISPR system for mitochondrial genome editing. For example, as early as 2015, a report claimed that regular Cas9 maintaining the nuclear localization signal and sgRNA with no modification successfully edited mitoDNA [ 189 ], although it was questioned due to the lack of mtDNA sequencing and mtDNA copy number data [ 64 ].

### Mitochondrial transport of sgRNA

Mammalian mitochondria have been reported to import several types of small non-coding RNAs, and a series of earlier studies have suggested that rearrangements of RNA secondary structures, involving components derived from mitochondrially localized tRNAs, could enhance the selectivity of mitochondrial import [ 190 – 192 ].

However, additional evidence supported the finding that regular sgRNAs or crRNAs possess mitochondrial localization ability, based on the comparable mitochondrial localization observed between unmodified guide RNAs and those with secondary structure modifications [ 193 – 195 ].

Alternatively, gRNA fused with a 20-nucleotide stem-loop sequence of nuclear RNAse P was also reported to promote the entrance to the mitochondria in a PNPase-dependent manner [ 196 , 197 ]. In these studies, targeted depletion of mtDNA was observed when MTS-Cas9 was used in conjunction with these sgRNAs [ 195 , 196 ].

### Evidence of CRISPR-based mtDNA editing

Two more recent studies may help dispel concerns about potential editing artifacts during mitochondrial isolation. It has been reported that HR was successfully induced in cell lines and zebrafish using the CRISPR system, consisting of a pair of regular sgRNAs, a ssDNA template, and mitochondrially targeted MTS-Cas9. Notably, in zebrafish, the gene knock-in was observed to transmit from the F0 to the F1 generation, though the details of RNA import and editing efficiency require further investigation [ 198 , 199 ].

Another study further provided direct evidence of CRISPR-mediated mtDNA insertion. Transfection of MTS-Cas9 together with sgRNA targeting MT-ND1 in a cell line significantly reduced the mtDNA copy number. Meanwhile, insertion of additional single-stranded DNA oligonucleotides at the designed cleavage site of MT-ND1 was identified via PCR-free third-generation sequencing, albeit with relatively low efficiency (0.026%). Stimulation of RAD51, a known recombinase related to HR-mediated repair in mammalian mitochondria, by its agonist RS-1 further increased the knock-in efficiency by 5-fold (to 0.03%) [ 58 , 200 ].

### Challenges and prospects of CRISPR-based mtDNA editing

Despite this evidence, the application of CRISPR systems in mtDNA editing faces significant limitations. Current data indicate that this strategy exhibits low editing efficiency, and further details regarding sgRNA and Cas protein import remain to be clarified [ 199 , 200 ].

#### Inefficient rna import

This conclusion was supported by a CRISPR-mediated mtDNA base editing study. #### Mitochondrial toxicity of Cas protein

On the other hand, the impairment of mitochondrial proteostasis in Cas9-expressing cells may also account for the reduction in mtDNA copy number observed in some previous studies. This is supported by the finding that cells transfected with MTS-Cas9-GFP exhibit severe disruptions to mitochondrial membrane potential and morphology [ 193 ]. Other Cas proteins with smaller sizes and distinct charge properties, such as MTS-LbCas12a and MTS-AsCas12a [ 193 , 194 ], show higher mitochondrial import efficiency. However, they still suffer from mitochondrial damage caused by prolonged expression and are limited in editing sites due to the lack of suitable PAM sequences.

Taken together, both the extremely low delivery efficiency of RNA and the mitochondrial toxicity of Cas proteins have significantly limited the application of CRISPR systems in mtDNA editing. To advance their practical use in this field, further optimizations are essential, including in-depth mechanistic investigations, the development of novel delivery strategies, and structural modifications of Cas proteins to enhance mitochondrial import and reduce toxicity.

## Conclusion

This review synthesizes current advances in gene therapy for mitochondrial genetic diseases and outlines persistent challenges. While dozens of gene therapies have been approved for nDNA mutation-related disorders [ 30 ], only one mitochondrial genetic disorder (allotopic expression of MT-ND1/4 for LHON) has reached clinical trials (Table 3 ), underscoring the distinct obstacles in mtDNA therapy. The relative progress of MT-ND4 therapy compared to other mtDNA-targeted strategies, such as nucleases or base editors, can be attributed to the inherent advantages of local administration, including reduced immune reactivity and easily observable phenotypic outcomes in LHON [ 30 , 74 , 75 , 205 ]. However, most other mitochondrial disorders involve systemic and CNS manifestations, necessitating more complex delivery approaches (Table 1 ). Consequently, allotopic expression, as well as other strategies, share several challenges with nDNA-targeted gene therapies when administrated systematically, such as immunogenicity of viral vectors [ 206 , 207 ], difficulties in CNS delivery [ 208 – 210 ], and high treatment costs [ 211 ]. Strategies developed for nDNA therapies, including engineering viral and non-viral vectors to enhance specificity [ 95 , 101 – 103 ], reduce immunogenicity [ 116 , 212 – 216 ], and improve delivery efficiency [ 97 – 99 , 217 ], may offer valuable insights for mitochondrial applications.

Beyond the delivery hurdles to the nucleus, the mitochondrial import of nuclear-encoded therapeutic proteins presents additional challenges. Hydrophobic proteins may misfold or mislocalize, leading to potential cytotoxicity [ 90 , 91 ]. Future efforts should focus on optimizing coding sequences and cis-regulatory elements to improve mitochondrial targeting and functional integration [ 93 ]. Alternatively, developing mitochondrial-directed delivery systems, such as those employing MTS-tagged AAV or non-viral vectors, could offer a more direct solution [ 95 ].

Identifying optimal therapeutic windows and establishing clinically relevant biomarkers are also essential. Currently, such assessments rely heavily on understanding the natural history of mitochondrial diseases and data from preclinical models. However, due to complexities in heteroplasmy dynamics and tissue-specific threshold effects, more accurate and reliable disease models are needed [ 15 , 16 ]. Recent advances in mitochondrial-targeted nucleases and base editors now allow for the generation of specific mtDNA mutations in animal models [ 146 , 157 – 160 ], offering unprecedented opportunities to dissect genotype-phenotype relationships. Complementarily, iPSC-derived organoids may provide more physiologically relevant in vitro platforms than traditional cybrid cells.

It is important to note that mitochondrial-targeted nucleases and base editors face substantial barriers to clinical translation beyond delivery limitations and the scarcity of in vivo data. Current editing tools are restricted to a limited set of target sites due to constraints in DNA-binding domain design [ 168 , 172 , 177 ]. Expanding the targeting scope requires improved design frameworks and databases for engineered proteins such as mito-TALEs and mito-ZF arrays.

Safety validation remains another critical gap. Preclinical studies must confirm the absence of off-target nDNA cleavage, recovery of mtDNA copy number after partial depletion, and organ safety during transient copy number reduction. For base editors, improvements in editing efficiency and precision are still needed: even at reported sites, editing rates remain suboptimal, calling for optimization of deaminase and binding domains, as well as novel delivery strategies [ 168 , 172 , 177 ]. While off-target editing has been mitigated through protein engineering, bystander editing remains a concern [ 141 ]. Clinical translation will require the development of editors with narrower activity windows and comprehensive databases correlating editing outcomes with binding configurations.

The application of CRISPR-derived editors in mitochondria represents another promising avenue, contingent on overcoming barriers to mitochondrial RNA import [ 64 ]. CRISPR-mediated base editing and prime editing systems, given their programmability and efficiency, could greatly expand the toolbox for mtDNA modification.

Finally, the long-term efficacy of these therapies requires careful evaluation. Single-cell studies suggest that heteroplasmy shifts are influenced by cellular selection pressures rather than genetic drift alone [ 218 ]. This is corroborated by the observed dominance of m.13268 G > A mutant mtDNA in transplant models [ 148 ], raising the concern that residual mutant mtDNA may repopulate under certain conditions due to a replicative advantage.

Notably, the delivery and editing technologies discussed in this review have not yet entered clinical trials, which greatly limits the significance of this review in evaluating the clinical treatment efficiency and safety of mitochondrial genetic diseases. Moreover, current optimization strategies for delivery systems are primarily derived from nDNA editing studies. Their applicability, efficiency, and effects in mitochondrial contexts require further validation.

In summary, while current research has outlined a developmental roadmap for mtDNA gene therapy, several limitations must be addressed. Key priorities include deepening the understanding of heteroplasmy dynamics and phenotype correlation, ensuring safe and efficient in vivo delivery and expression, and enhancing the specificity, efficiency, and long-term stability of gene editing outcomes in mitochondria.

However, due to complexities in heteroplasmy dynamics and tissue-specific threshold effects, more accurate and reliable disease models are needed [ 15 , 16 ]. Recent advances in mitochondrial-targeted nucleases and base editors now allow for the generation of specific mtDNA mutations in animal models [ 146 , 157 – 160 ], offering unprecedented opportunities to dissect genotype-phenotype relationships. Complementarily, iPSC-derived organoids may provide more physiologically relevant in vitro platforms than traditional cybrid cells.

It is important to note that mitochondrial-targeted nucleases and base editors face substantial barriers to clinical translation beyond delivery limitations and the scarcity of in vivo data. Current editing tools are restricted to a limited set of target sites due to constraints in DNA-binding domain design [ 168 , 172 , 177 ]. Expanding the targeting scope requires improved design frameworks and databases for engineered proteins such as mito-TALEs and mito-ZF arrays.

Safety validation remains another critical gap. Preclinical studies must confirm the absence of off-target nDNA cleavage, recovery of mtDNA copy number after partial depletion, and organ safety during transient copy number reduction. For base editors, improvements in editing efficiency and precision are still needed: even at reported sites, editing rates remain suboptimal, calling for optimization of deaminase and binding domains, as well as novel delivery strategies [ 168 , 172 , 177 ]. While off-target editing has been mitigated through protein engineering, bystander editing remains a concern [ 141 ]. Clinical translation will require the development of editors with narrower activity windows and comprehensive databases correlating editing outcomes with binding configurations.

The application of CRISPR-derived editors in mitochondria represents another promising avenue, contingent on overcoming barriers to mitochondrial RNA import [ 64 ]. CRISPR-mediated base editing and prime editing systems, given their programmability and efficiency, could greatly expand the toolbox for mtDNA modification.

Finally, the long-term efficacy of these therapies requires careful evaluation. Single-cell studies suggest that heteroplasmy shifts are influenced by cellular selection pressures rather than genetic drift alone [ 218 ]. This is corroborated by the observed dominance of m.13268 G > A mutant mtDNA in transplant models [ 148 ], raising the concern that residual mutant mtDNA may repopulate under certain conditions due to a replicative advantage.

Notably, the delivery and editing technologies discussed in this review have not yet entered clinical trials, which greatly limits the significance of this review in evaluating the clinical treatment efficiency and safety of mitochondrial genetic diseases. Moreover, current optimization strategies for delivery systems are primarily derived from nDNA editing studies. Their applicability, efficiency, and effects in mitochondrial contexts require further validation.

In summary, while current research has outlined a developmental roadmap for mtDNA gene therapy, several limitations must be addressed. Key priorities include deepening the understanding of heteroplasmy dynamics and phenotype correlation, ensuring safe and efficient in vivo delivery and expression, and enhancing the specificity, efficiency, and long-term stability of gene editing outcomes in mitochondria.
