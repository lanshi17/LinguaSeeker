# From confusion to diagnosis: a rare case of melas syndrome in a patient with familial consanguinity, recurrent stroke-like episodes, and concurrent FSGS

## Abstract

Introduction MELAS syndrome (Mitochondrial Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like Episodes) is a rare mitochondrial disease characterized by a collection of symptoms including stroke-like episodes, seizures, visual disturbances, motor weakness, and headache with an estimated prevalence of 0.18 per 100,000 individuals. Approximately 80% of instances are linked to the m.3243 A > G mutation in the MT-TL1 gene. Case presentation A 45-year-old female with a history of diabetes mellitus, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and hypertension presented to the Emergency Department with a 6-day history of acute-onset confusion, headache, progressive vision loss, and right-sided hemiparesis, accompanied by language impairment reported by family members and a relevant past medical history . On examination, the patient was disoriented, with impaired comprehension and an inability to follow commands. Family history was significant to focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), diabetes, hypertension, and cerebrovascular accidents (CVA). Brain MRI revealed widespread and diffuse tissue changes with restrictive heterogeneous features, predominantly involving the left temporal lobe. A significant lactate peak was demonstrated in Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS). The diagnosis of MELAS syndrome was presumed after imaging findings and confirmed with genetic testing, where it revealed the presence of a mutation in the m.3243 A > G mitochondrial gene. The patient initiated treatment with intravenous L-arginine along with oral taurine as prophylaxis. Despite titrating her treatment to the maximum dosage, she experienced minimal improvement and subsequently developed generalized seizures and altered mental status. Conclusion This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of late-onset MELAS syndrome, emphasizing its multisystemic nature and phenotypic variability. Despite standard treatments, partial clinical improvement underscores the need for novel, targeted therapies and multidisciplinary care to address the limitations of current management approaches.


## Introduction

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) dysfunction, inherited exclusively through the maternal ovum, represents a major cause of impaired oxidative phosphorylation within cells [ 1 ]. This dysfunction leads to the accumulation of lactic acid, resulting in multi-system involvement and a wide range of clinical manifestations [ 2 ]. Among these disorders, MELAS syndrome (Mitochondrial Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like Episodes) stands out as a rare mitochondrial disease most frequently linked to the m.3243 A > G mutation in the MT-TL1 gene, accounting for approximately 80% of cases [ 3 ]. With an estimated prevalence of 0.18 per 100,000 individuals, identifying and diagnosing MELAS is of significant clinical importance [ 4 ]. The disease exhibits significant genetic heterogeneity and clinical variability due to heteroplasmy , where variable proportions of mutant and wild-type mtDNA across tissues influence disease severity and presentation [ 5 ].

We are reporting on the diagnostic journey of a 48-year-old female with MELAS syndrome, exploring her clinical presentation and challenging treatment. This case aims to contribute to the growing body of literature on MELAS, providing insights into the complexities of diagnosis and management in this rare mitochondrial disorder.

## Case presentation

A 45-year-old female with a history of diabetes mellitus, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, hypertension, and sensorineural hearing loss presented to the Emergency Department with a 6-day history of acute-onset confusion, headache, progressive vision loss, and right-sided weakness involving both upper and lower extremities, accompanied by language impairment reported by family members and a relevant past medical history. On examination, she was disoriented to person, place, and time. She was afebrile, with vital signs within normal limits. A systemic examination revealed no significant abnormalities apart from the neurological findings, which included right-sided upper and lower extremity weakness, impaired comprehension, and an inability to follow commands. The patient’s family history is significant for a hereditary predisposition to focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), diabetes, hypertension, and cerebrovascular accidents (CVA). Her pedigree is further complicated by consanguinity, with her parents being double first cousins and her marital union occurring with a second-degree relative.

Laboratory test results were within the patient’s baseline range. Lumbar puncture revealed cerebrospinal fluid with 2 cells/µL white blood cells (WBC), normal glucose and protein levels, and an elevated lactate level of 5.7 mmol/L; herpes simplex virus PCR was negative.

MRI of the brain revealed diffuse tissue changes with heterogeneous features, predominantly in the left temporal and temporo-occipital regions (Fig. 1 ). Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS) demonstrated a significant lactate peak, consistent with metabolic dysfunction (Fig. 2 ). Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) showed dilation of branches of the left middle cerebral artery (MCA) and posterior cerebral artery (PCA) without evidence of significant narrowing or stenosis in the cerebral vasculature.

 Fig. 2 A brain MRS : A Lt basal ganglia (normal parenchyma)—shows Normal lactate value. B Lt occipital area (pathologic area)—Pathologic MRS—demonstrated a significant lactate peak value at 1.3 ppm 

Multiple differential diagnoses were investigated, including vascular and structural abnormalities, which were excluded by brain MRI and MRS. ECG and ECHO showed no embolic source. The blood film was normal, which ruled out sickle cell disease. Genetic testing excludes Fabry disease and other metabolic disorders. In addition, thrombophilia and autoimmune markers were negative .

A diagnosis of MELAS syndrome was presumed after imaging findings and confirmed with genetic testing, where it revealed the presence of a mutation in the m.3243 A > G mitochondrial gene via Sanger sequencing, and then the patient was initiated on intravenous L-arginine at a dose of 50 mL/day, following the MELAS acute protocol, along with oral taurine as prophylaxis to reduce further episodes. Chronic medication therapy was maintained. Initially, the patient demonstrated partial clinical improvement correlating with MRI findings showing  partial resolution of edema and enhancement. However, despite titrating her treatment to the maximum dosage, she continued to experience progressive episodes of facial muscle weakness, sensory and motor aphasia, mental status alterations, and generalized seizures. A subsequent brain MRI revealed persistent symmetrical basal ganglia T2 hyperintensities and expanded cortical T2 abnormalities. MRS findings revealed T2 shine-through effects in the basal ganglia and thalamus regions, elevated lactate levels, decreased NAA (N-acetylaspartate), and mildly elevated choline peaks (Fig. 3 ).

 Fig. 3 A Brain MRI showing persistent symmetrical hyperintensities on T2-weighted imaging in the basal ganglia, along with an expansion of cortical areas exhibiting abnormal T2 hyperintensities 

During the most recent follow-up visit, the patient remained clinically stable while receiving arginine therapy, although she reported occasional headaches. The recent brain MRI shows radiological progression with bilateral cerebellar involvement; however, no significant clinical deterioration was observed. Seizures also remain stable, with no recent episodes reported under treatment with lacosamide.

## Discussion

MELAS syndrome, which was identified in 1984 by Pavlik et al., consists of a constellation of symptoms that include stroke-like episodes, seizures, visual disturbances, motor weakness, and headache. The age of onset for MELAS symptoms varies; 65–76% of patients present with clinical manifestations at or before the age of 20, with only 1–6% presenting after the age of 40 [ 6 , 7 ]. In the present case, we report on a late-onset patient who presented with a significant history of consanguinity and comorbidities.

The family history of our 45-year-old patient strongly supports the diagnosis of a maternally inherited mitochondrial disorder, consistent with MELAS syndrome. The presence of type 2 diabetes mellitus in four of her siblings, proteinuria in two siblings and her niece, hearing loss in one sibling, and early-onset dementia in her mother aligns with findings in published case reports of families with the m.3243 A > G mutation. Diabetes, renal involvement, particularly focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), and hearing impairment are well-documented manifestations of this mutation [ 8 ].

The variability in clinical presentation across the family—ranging from isolated proteinuria or hearing loss to more severe systemic involvement such as diabetes and dementia—is explained by heteroplasmy [ 3 , 4 ].

This maternal inheritance pattern, characterized by the absence of paternal transmission, is consistent with mitochondrial diseases and distinguishes them from other hereditary conditions like Alport syndrome. These findings underscore the importance of genetic testing, which has been instrumental in confirming the m.3243 A > G mutation in similar cases and preventing misdiagnosis, ensuring appropriate management, and counseling for affected families. Despite our patient carrying the maternally inherited m.3243 A > G mutation , which raises suspicion of familial involvement , neither genetic counseling for MELAS nor nephrogenetic counseling to assess the risk of familial FSGS was followed up by the patient or her family.

Diabetes is a common manifestation of MELAS syndrome, affecting 25–69% of patients, with a notably higher prevalence (69.2%) in late-onset cases (onset after age 40) [ 9 ]. It may present as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, occurring before or after the onset of primary symptoms, and is strongly associated with the m.3243 A > G mutation [ 8 ]. Additionally, studies suggest that diabetes contributes to the accelerated cognitive decline observed in MELAS patients [ 8 ].

Renal involvement in MELAS syndrome is rare but represents a significant exacerbating factor in morbidity [ 10 ]. It typically presents as tubulopathy, interstitial nephritis, or focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) [ 11 ]. FSGS in MELAS is hypothesized to result from altered mitochondrial function in podocytes, characterized microscopically by the significant presence of abnormal mitochondria [ 12 ]. This leads to steroid-resistant proteinuria that is generally lower in severity [ 13 ]. Approximately 80% of patients with renal involvement also present with hearing impairment, often leading to a misdiagnosis of Alport syndrome [ 14 ].

Interestingly, renal involvement may precede the onset of stroke-like episodes in some cases. Alcubilla-Prats et al. reported two cases of MELAS syndrome where patients presented with isolated renal findings—elevated creatinine in one case and proteinuria in the other—without classic symptoms such as lactic acidosis, exercise intolerance, myopathy, or neurological manifestations. The diagnosis was confirmed through biopsy and genetic testing, emphasizing the importance of considering MELAS in atypical presentations [ 15 ].

Lim et al. described a case similar to ours, involving a 34-year-old man with multisystem involvement, including diabetes, sensorineural hearing loss, hypertension, mild developmental delay, and FSGS, progressing to end-stage renal disease and requiring kidney transplantation. He also presented with static encephalopathy, behavioral challenges, and mild cerebellar gait. Whole exome sequencing revealed a mitochondrial MT-TW tRNA mutation (m.5538G > A) with 30% heteroplasmy in blood, highlighting the complexity and variability of mitochondrial disorders [ 16 ].

Treatment for MELAS syndrome remains challenging due to its complex and multifaceted nature. The absence of randomized controlled trials limits evidence-based therapies, leaving clinicians to rely on symptomatic management and mitochondrial support [ 17 ]. In our patient, standard treatments, including L-arginine, taurine, and supportive therapies, led to only partial improvement, highlighting the variability of treatment responses driven by heteroplasmy. While L-arginine has shown promise in improving symptoms in about 50% of patients, its efficacy is inconsistent, often requiring adjunctive treatments such as antiepileptic drugs for optimal outcomes [ 18 ]. Emerging evidence suggests that L-citrulline, with its superior bioavailability and ability to elevate plasma arginine levels more effectively, may be a better alternative and warrants further exploration [ 7 ].

Future treatment approaches for MELAS aim to address the root cause of the disease through advanced technologies such as epigenetic and gene-editing therapies, which target histone modifications and mtDNA methylation. However, these methods remain theoretical at this stage. Mitochondrial replacement therapy has been applied in animal models and some mitochondrial diseases, but its use is restricted by significant ethical and technical challenges [ 17 ]. Additionally, clinical trials with EPI-743, a synthetic antioxidant targeting mitochondrial dysfunction, have demonstrated reduced disease progression, severity, and hospitalizations in patients with various mitochondrial diseases, including MELAS. However, none of these trials have specifically focused on MELAS, leaving its precise impact on this condition unclear [ 16 ].

This case underscores the limitations of current treatments and emphasizes the need for novel, targeted approaches. Multidisciplinary care and ongoing research are essential to develop more effective therapies for this devastating disorder.
