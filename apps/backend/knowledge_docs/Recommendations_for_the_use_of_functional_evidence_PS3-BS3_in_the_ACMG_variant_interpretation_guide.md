　　**Description of functional experiments in the ACMG/AMP variant classification guide:**   
Functional experimental studies are a very powerful tool for studying the pathogenicity of variants, however, not all functional studies are effective in predicting the function of genes or proteins. For example, some enzymatic experiments use well-established methods that can be used to assess the effects of missense variations on enzyme activity in metabolic pathways (e.g., α-galactosidase function experiments); On the other hand, some functional experiments lack consistency in assessing the effects of variation on protein function. When evaluating the effectiveness of a functional detection method, it is important to consider the extent to which the functional experiment reflects the biological environment in which it performs. For example, functional experiments of enzymes directly in biopsy tissue from patients or animal models are more convincing than in vitro expression of proteins.  
Similarly, experiments that reflect the full biological function of a protein, such as the function of enzymatic degradation of substrates, are more evidentiary than experiments that reflect only a subset of functions, such as the function of proteolytic ATP with collateral binding capacity. The validity, repeatability and stability of functional experiments should be considered as important parameters to evaluate the analytical performance of functional experiments and to determine the completeness of sample diagnostic information, which is easily affected by the method and time of specimen collection, storage and transportation. [CLIA] (https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=115157713&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=CLIA&zd_token=eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJpc3MiOiJ6aGlkYV9zZXJ2ZXIiLCJleHAiOjE3NzA1MjI0MTIsInEiOiJDTElBIiwiemhpZGFfc291cmNlIjoiZW50aXR5IiwiY29udGVudF9pZCI6MTE1MTU3NzEzLCJjb250ZW50X3R5cGUiOiJBcnRpY2xlIiwibWF0Y2hfb3JkZXIiOjEsInpkX3Rva2VuIjpudWxsfQ.sGs7m4QZ2UZZtkp2zStnE00mVeSRAZwpI7bHLnYm2nw&zhida_source=entity (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendment) Certified laboratories establish assays or commercialized kits to reduce the impact of these factors on experiments. Evaluating variants at the messenger RNA level, such as messenger RNA stability, processing, or translation, can be informative when assessing the effects of variants on splicing sites, coding sequences, untranslated regions, and deeper intron regions. Related technical methods include direct analysis of RNA and/or complementary DNA derivatives, as well as in vitro microgene splicing analysis.

　　**Description of PS3 Evidence:**   
In vivo and in vitro functional experiments have identified variants that cause impaired gene function. Note: Functional experiments need to be verified to be valid, repeatable, and stable.

　　Description of BS3 Evidence:**   
Variants that have no effect on protein function and splicing have been identified in vitro and in vitro experiments.

　　Functional studies provide powerful insights into the impact of variants on protein function and contribute to the reclassification of variants of unclear clinical significance (VUS), so it is important to use accurate and consistent functional experimental evidence in the interpretation of different variants. However, the ACMG/AMP guidelines do not provide detailed guidance on how to evaluate evidence for functional experiments, and differences in the use of PS3/BS3 evidence are major factors contributing to inconsistencies in interpretation of variants between clinical laboratories.

　　In response to calls for further standardization of variant interpretation, Clinical Genomic Resources (ClinGen) has established a Variant Interpretation Working Group ([SVI]( https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=115157713&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=SVI&zd_token=eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJpc3MiOiJ6aGlkYV9zZXJ2ZXIiLCJleHAiOjE3NzA1MjI0MTIsInEiOiJTVkkiLCJ6aGlkYV9zb3VyY2UiOiJlbnRpdHkiLCJjb250ZW50X2lkIjoxMTUxNTc3MTMsImNvbnRlbnRfdHlwZSI6IkFydGljbGUiLCJtYXRjaF9vcmRlciI6MSwiemRfdG9rZW4iOm51bGx9.ZIBNkzLEccyEgdq8Md4UPwric7dpOocnQhyRvF1NbHg&zhida_source=entity and different expert groups on interpretation of variations ([VCEP]( https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=115157713&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=VCEP&zd_token=eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJpc3MiOiJ6aGlkYV9zZXJ2ZXIiLCJleHAiOjE3NzA1MjI0MTIsInEiOiJWQ0VQIiwiemhpZGFfc291cmNlIjoiZW50aXR5IiwiY29udGVudF9pZCI6MTE1MTU3NzEzLCJjb250ZW50X3R5cGUiOiJBcnRpY2xlIiwibWF0Y2hfb3JkZXIiOjEsInpkX3Rva2VuIjpudWxsfQ.lfQ4UQk12ycjHhAGVn8Bk4juAeO3vQO3GGfajQbiqHA&zhida_source=entity )), optimising for each evidence criterion in the ACMG/AMP guidelines. So far, VCEP has issued recommendations for six different disease areas, including what functional experiments can be used in the PS3/BS3 evidence and criteria for use. Experimental methods approved by VCEP vary widely, including splicing experiments, animal and cell models, and different in vitro systems. VCEP-approved experimental methods usually take into account the mechanism of the disease, and most include wild-type controls, but the criteria for statistical analysis and inclusion are less consistent. VCEP differs significantly in how to define "defined" experiments, including parameter considerations such as experimental design, replication, controls, and validation, which indicates some subjectivity in assessing the quality and applicability of functional evidence, which can lead to inconsistencies in the classification of variants.  
This article describes the guidelines developed by SVI on the evaluation of the clinical effectiveness of functional experiments, as well as an interim framework for determining appropriate levels of evidence strength to guide the use of functional experimental evidence in the interpretation of clinical variation to meet the basic quality requirements.

　　To assess the use of evidence strength in the absence of rigorous statistical analysis for a functional experiment, by assessing the number of variants in a theoretical experiment that have previously been classified as controls, to calculate the probability of pathogenicity ([OddsPath]( https://zhida.zhihu.com/search?content_id=115157713&content_type=Article&match_order=1&q=OddsPath&zd_token=eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJpc3MiOiJ6aGlkYV9zZXJ2ZXIiLCJleHAiOjE3NzA1MjI0MTIsInEiOiJPZGRzUGF0aCIsInpoaWRhX3NvdXJjZSI6ImVudGl0eSIsImNvbnRlbnRfaWQiOjExNTE1NzcxMywiY29udGVudF90eXBlIjoiQXJ0aWNsZSIsIm1hdGNoX29yZGVyIjoxLCJ6ZF90b2tlbiI6bnVsbH0.cKIgfV7f_7FRN82VQ3Wz4NYgRwI0VYTAbzI3N_MNrVA&zhida_source=entity)）。 The proportion of pathogenic variants in the overall model data was defined as a priori probability (P1), and the proportion of pathogenic variants in the experimental results of abnormal or functional normal variants was defined as a posterior probability (P2). The panel initially evaluated the OddsPath that an ideal binary classifier could achieve, where the experimental results of all control variants were consistent with the pathogenic classification prior to the variant (see Table S1 of the original text). The panel then evaluated a more conservative OddsPath in which one control variant had an intermediate or inconclusive experimental result, but the experimental results of the remaining pathogenic and benign variant controls were consistent with their previous pathogenicity classification (see Table S2 of the original text). To avoid situations where the posterior probability is zero or infinity, and to consider that variation in the experiment may produce results that are inconsistent with previous ratings, the panel added a misclassified variant to each set.

　　**OddsPath Calculation Method:**   
OddsPath\=[P2 ×(1−P1)]/[(1−P2)×P1]  
The corresponding level of evidence strength (support, moderate, strong, very strong) was then selected based on the calculated OddsPath value.

　　The SVI Working Group recommends that interpreters use a four-step process in the interpretation of clinical variation to determine the applicability and strength of evidence for functional experiments:  
(1) Clarify the pathogenic mechanism of the disease,  
(2) evaluate the applicability of general types of functional experimental methods used in the field,  
(3) evaluate the effectiveness of functional experiments in specific cases,  
(4) Apply evidence to the interpretation of specific variants.  
In the ACMG/AMP guidelines, well-established functional experiments can be directly defaulted to strong levels of evidence (PS3/BS3), and SVI recommends using different strengths of PS3/BS3 evidence (including supportive, moderate, strong, very strong) based on the completeness of functional experiments.

　　**1. Clarify the pathogenic mechanism of the disease:**   
For functional experiments to work in the interpretation of clinical variation, the mechanisms of the underlying gene-disease must be well understood to determine which functional experiments are applicable. This is a very important first step because some genes are associated with different diseases, depending on their different pathogenic mechanisms (e.g., gain-of-function, loss of function, and dominant negative effects).

　　2. Evaluate the applicability of general types of functional experimental methods used in this field:   
Evaluate the general types of experiments used in this field, including model systems, experimental methods, and functional experimental results. Pathogenesis of general types of analytical models (e.g., loss of function, gain-of-function, pathway-specific output) were evaluated based on the pathogenesis of gene-related diseases. The relative strengths and weaknesses of model systems should be evaluated, and whether animal, cellular, and in vitro models are suitable for the study of specific disease mechanisms. The purpose of this step is to describe the applicable types of functional experiments that can be used for clinical variation interpretation. It is important to note that the strength of the evidence is not determined by the category of the experiment, but by the experimental validation indicators.

　　**3. Evaluate the effectiveness of functional experiments in specific cases:**   
For the applicable general types of experimental methods, the next step should be to evaluate the effectiveness of functional experiments in specific cases. Many different laboratories may produce functional experimental evidence using the same routine experimental methods, but given differences in the specific methods used and the level of validation, each individual replicate experiment needs to be evaluated before functional experimental data can be applied to clinical interpretation. Using a sufficient number of validation controls to calculate positive predictive values or determine OddsPath can provide the most reliable evidence for functional analysis. Without this level of clinical validation, the predictive value of this analysis is limited.

　　▶ Functional evidence should not be used unless the dynamic range of the experiment and the defined thresholds for normal, uncertain, or functionally abnormal results are already very clear:

1. Excludes negative (normal or wild-type) and positive (abnormal or non-functional) control experiments;
2. Experiments that do not include technical and/or biological replicates;

　　▶ A supportive level of evidence of causality (PS3\_supporting) or benign evidence (BS3\_supporting) may be used in the following cases:

1. Includes experimental controls and replicates, but with 10 or fewer validation controls;
2. Experimental methods have been widely accepted, have been previously validated, or use a kit with clear operating parameters, but controls and replicates are not documented.

　　▶ Moderate causative evidence (PS3\_moderate) or benign evidence (BS3\_moderate) may be used in the following cases:

1. At least 11 validation controlled experiments, including benign and pathogenic variants, were conducted, but no formal statistical analysis was performed on the ability to distinguish between pathogenic and benign variants;

　　When rigorous statistical analysis can calculate the OddsPath, the corresponding strength of pathogenicity or benign evidence can be used based on the calculated OddsPath value.

　　4. Apply evidence to the interpretation of specific variants:   
Once the experiment in a specific instance is evaluated as a whole, the experimental results of a specific variant can be used as the basis for interpreting the variation.  
If experiments demonstrate the results of functional abnormalities consistent with the pathogenesis of the disease, evidence of different strengths of PS3 can be used depending on the degree of validation described above.  
If the results of this variant experiment show normal function, evidence of different strengths of BS3 can be used depending on the degree of validation above.

　　Special attention is required if the variant results show a median effect on function, either because the experiment does not fully reflect protein function (evidence can be downgraded based on the results) or can support the effect of subpotency alleles or partial loss of function, such as in a disease with incomplete visibility and/or less severe manifestation. Consideration of disease mechanisms should help guide the use of evidence of appropriate strength in the interpretation of these types of variants.

　　Cumulative evidence When multiple functional experimental results are available for the same variant (same experimental results in different laboratories, or multiple pieces of evidence from different experimental types), interpreters should use evidence from functional experiments that validate the clearest and best measure of disease mechanisms.

　　1. Variations analyzed by multiple experiments (same or different types of experiments):

1. If the results of the experiment are consistent (both show abnormal effects or both show functional effects), use the different strengths of the PS3/BS3 evidence based on the validation of the most definitive experiment.
2. Use different strengths of PS3/BS3 evidence based on the results of the experiment if the experimental results are contradictory and the experimental method that best reflects the mechanism of the disease and is more well validated can override the conflicting results of other experimental methods. If the verification of experimental results is basically at the same level, conflicting functional evidence cannot be applied to the interpretation of variation.

　　2. The expert group did not reach a consensus on whether the results of different types of functional experiments could be combined (e.g., two supporting levels of evidence from different types of experiments were upgraded to PS3\_moderate). The main concern is that it is difficult to determine that the two experimental methods measure independent functions, which may lead to double counting. Another concern is that evidence accumulated from multiple experiments may lead to a combined explanation of the disease risk for a particular variant (e.g., two PS3\_supporting results can be superimposed on PS3\_moderate as high-risk variants). On the other hand, if experiments measure different functions, the evidence may be complementary and increase confidence in the overall results, especially for the BS3 evidence.
