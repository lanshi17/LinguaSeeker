Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) is a key endosomal sensor that detects single-stranded RNA, linking innate and adaptive immunity through the induction of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines. Recent studies have underscored the pivotal role of TLR7 in shaping immune responses to respiratory viral infections, including SARS-CoV-2, influenza A virus, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), as well as in the pathogenesis of systemic autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which can be triggered by the respiratory viral infections. In COVID-19, TLR7 deficiency is associated with severe disease, particularly in males, due to impaired interferon responses and antibody production. In influenza, TLR7 enhances humoral and cytotoxic responses, though its overactivation may contribute to immunopathology. The role of TLR7 in RSV remains controversial, with both protective and detrimental effects reported depending on host and experimental context. In contrast, TLR7 plays a pathogenic role in SLE by amplifying type I interferon signaling and promoting autoreactive B cell activation. This review synthesizes current knowledge on TLR7-mediated signaling across these diseases, highlighting its context-dependent functions and dualistic nature in immunity and disease. We will discuss mechanistic insights, clinical relevance, and emerging therapeutic strategies targeting TLR7, emphasizing the need for precision modulation of this pathway in the treatment of viral infections and autoimmune disorders. The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the devastating impact of emerging viruses, while influenza A and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) continues to circulate globally, driven by antigenic drift and shift [ 1 – 3 ]. Although the respiratory viruses infect airway epithelium, significant gaps remain in our understanding of their pathogenic mechanisms and host immune responses. In addition, respiratory viral infections, such as those caused by influenza viruses, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2, have been increasingly recognized as potential triggers or modulators of autoimmune diseases [ 4 – 7 ]. The interplay between these infections and autoimmunity is complex, involving both innate and adaptive immune dysregulation [ 8 , 9 ]. Respiratory viral infections can act as environmental triggers for autoimmunity in genetically susceptible individuals through immune activation, interferon dysregulation, and chronic inflammation [ 10 , 11 ]. Autoimmune diseases involve in an inappropriate immune response against the body's own components, but their precise mechanisms remain elusive and require further investigation. The Toll-like receptors (TLRs) represent a class of evolutionarily conserved proteins pivotal in the innate immune system's arsenal against pathogens [ 12 ]. Among TLRs, the role of Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) in innate immunity against viral infection has drawn great attention since the onset of SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in 2020 [ 13 , 14 ]. Extensive clinical evidence clearly highlights the critical role of TLR7 and TLR7-downstrean signaling in initiating the innate immunity against SARS-CoV-2 infection but not influenza virus and RSV infection. The loss of TLR7 function mutations has been recently identified as a contributor to 2% of severe male COVID-19 cases under 60 years old [ 15 ]. However, nearly all current evidence connecting TLR7 to COVID-19 stems from clinical studies; mechanistic studies on how TLR7 regulates immune responses during SARS-CoV-2 infection are notably rare. In addition, TLR7-mediated downstream signaling activation and IFN production also enhance antibody production, which contributes to development of adaptive immunity against the respiratory viral infection such as influenza [ 16 ]. Experimentally, we and others recently report that TLR7 and its down-signaling activation play a crucial role in mounting effective immune responses against SARS-CoV-2 [ 13 , 14 ]. Despite these insights, the molecular and cellular mechanisms by which TLR7 contribute to the innate and adaptive immunity against the infection of respiratory viruses such as SARS-CoV2, influenza virus and RSV remain elusive and warrants further investigation. Furthermore, TLR7's implication in autoimmune diseases that can result from the respiratory viral infection has long been recognized. Variants in the TLR7 gene are associated with a heightened risk of the clinical outcomes of autoimmune diseases [ 17 ]. The receptor’s role in modulating autoimmunity, particularly through the mechanism of antibody production, provides a compelling area of study [ 18 ]. Such autoimmune responses, mediated by TLR7, underscore its dualistic nature in health and disease. This review will examine the role of TLR7 across viral infections and autoimmune diseases, exploring experimental findings, clinical evidence, and unresolved questions. The timeline of research will be considered, reflecting several decades of influenza A studies, the rapid expansion of COVID-19 research, and the longer history of autoimmune disease investigations. By integrating findings from these domains, this review aims to provide a framework for understanding TLR7’s role and TLR7-mediated signaling in disease progression and immune regulation. TLR7 and its downstream signaling The TLRs represent a class of evolutionarily conserved proteins pivotal in the innate immune system's arsenal against pathogens [ 19 ]. These receptors detect both PAMPs and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), initiating a cascade of signaling pathways that activate inflammatory responses and upregulate genes crucial for host defense [ 19 ]. By bridging innate and adaptive immunity, TLRs orchestrate a comprehensive immune response, ensuring a rapid and efficient reaction to microbial invasions [ 20 ]. TLRs are broadly categorized into two groups based on their localization: cell-surface TLRs (e.g., TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, TLR5, and TLR6), which detect extracellular microbial components such as lipoproteins and lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and endosomal TLRs (e.g., TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9), which specialize in recognizing nucleic acids from viruses and bacteria. These differences in localization and ligand specificity reflect their specialized roles in immune surveillance [ 12 ]. Each TLR contributes uniquely to immune responses. TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, and TLR6 form heterodimers to detect bacterial and fungal components such as lipoproteins, peptidoglycans, and lipoteichoic acid, playing critical roles in bacterial infections and inflammatory diseases like sepsis [ 21 ]. TLR3, localized in endosomes, recognizes double-stranded RNA and triggers antiviral responses via the TRIF pathway, with implications in conditions like herpes simplex encephalitis and inflammatory bowel disease [ 22 ]. TLR4 is specialized for detecting LPS, initiating strong inflammatory responses that are essential in bacterial infections but can lead to pathological conditions such as sepsis and atherosclerosis when dysregulated [ 22 ]. TLR5 is predominantly expressed in intestinal epithelial cells and recognizes bacterial flagellin, contributing to gut homeostasis and systemic immunity, with associations in Crohn’s disease and bacterial infections [ 23 ]. Endosomal TLRs, such as TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9, are particularly important in recognizing viral and bacterial nucleic acids [ 24 ]. TLR7 and TLR8, which detect single-stranded RNA, play central roles in antiviral defense by inducing the production of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines [ 25 ]. These receptors are expressed in plasmacytoid dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells [ 26 ]. Dysregulation of TLR7 and TLR8 is associated with autoimmune diseases such as lupus and viral infections like HIV and influenza as discussed extensively below. TLR9, expressed in plasmacytoid dendritic cells and B cells, recognizes unmethylated CpG DNA and has been implicated in autoimmune diseases such as lupus and in cancer immunotherapy [ 27 ]. TLR10, the only known inhibitory TLR, is functional in humans but absent in mice, where it modulates immune responses and has been linked to allergic and inflammatory disorders [ 28 ]. Despite the conserved roles of TLRs across species, notable differences exist between murine and human TLRs. For instance, TLR13, which detects bacterial ribosomal RNA, is unique to mice, while TLR8 is largely inactive in mice but plays a prominent role in human antiviral immunity [ 29 ]. These species-specific differences in ligand recognition and downstream signaling pose challenges for translating findings from mouse models to human immunology. Additionally, expression patterns of TLRs vary between species, influencing their roles in immune defense and disease pathogenesis. Such differences emphasize the need for advanced in vitro systems and humanized animal models to bridge the translational gap in TLR research. Among the TLRs, TLR7 has gained particular attention due to its specific ability to detect single-stranded RNA from viruses, marking it as a critical component in the immune response against RNA viruses such as influenza, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2 [ 30 ]. TLR7 is predominantly expressed in plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDCs) and recognizes single stranded (ss) RNA from viruses [ 25 ]. As other TLR, TLR7 is synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum, transported to the Golgi, and is recruited endosomes. The localization of TLR7 is thought to be critical for ligand recognition as well as for preventing it from coming into contact with self-nucleic acids, which could cause autoimmunity [ 1 , 2 ]. In the endosome, TLR7 undergoes proteolytic cleavage by cathepsins B, S, L, H, and K and asparginyl endopeptidase to become a functional form that mediates ligand recognition and initiates signaling [ 3 ]. Type I IFN is one of the main products of TLR7 pathway, and it relies on a complex containing MyD88 and IRF7 (Fig. 1 ). This complex also contains TRAF3, TRAF6, IRAK4, IRAK1, IKKα, OPNi, and Dock2 [ 4 ]. After TLR engagement, MyD88 forms a complex with IRAK kinase family members, also known as the Myddosome [ 5 ] (Fig. 1 ). IRF7 is phosphorylated by IRAK1 and/or IKKα. Phosphorylated IRF7 (p-IRF7) is translocated into the nucleus to regulate the expression of type I IFN. At the same time, MyD88-IRAK4-TRAF6 complex also drives NF-κB-dependent inflammatory cytokine induction (Fig. 1 ). The signaling complex containing MyD88-IRAK1-TRAF6-IRF7 is formed within lipid bodies by the IFN-inducible Viperin, which activates IRAK1 by ubiquitination on lysine 63 [ 6 ]. NF-κB translocates into the nucleus to induce proinflammatory gene expression. The incorporation of TRAF3 activates IRF7, with further nucleus translocation and induction of type I IFN (Fig. 1 ). Several TLR7 agonists have been tested as potential treatments for HIV and HBV infection. One of the best known TLR7 agonists, imiquimod, approved by the FDA for treatment of anogenital warts, superficial basal cell carcinomas, and actinic keratosis [ 43 ], it’s been studied in phase III human HIV-positive patients with high grade anal squamous skin lesions [ 44 ]. Overall, the use of TLR agonists to support HIV and HBV therapies appears promising, warranting further investigation. Although the role of TLR7 in the respiratory viral diseases including COVID-19 and influenza has been drawn a great attention recently [ 13 , 14 ], the precise effects and mechanisms of the TLR7 in regulating immune responses in those diseases remain poorly understood, particularly regarding the various sensitivities to different RNA viruses. Understanding how TLR7 is activated and why TLR7 responds differently to RNA viruses is crucial for identifying novel therapeutic strategies and more effective vaccines to prevent those respiratory diseases. 2) Activation of TLR7 and downstream signaling protect against SARS-CoV-2 infection through enhancing innate and adaptive immunity The critical role of TLR7 in SARS-CoV-2 infection has been underscored by numerous clinical studies, distinguishing it from other TLRs that also play significant roles in this infection. Among them, TLR2 and TLR4 were documented TLRs contributing to the host defense against SARS-CoV-2 infection [ 48 – 50 ]. Moreover, as a double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) sensor, TLR3 can also sense dsRNA intermediate during replication of single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) in host cells. Reduced expression of peripheral blood TLR3 is linked to an adverse outcome in severe COVID-19 patients, suggesting an underlying role of TLR3 in regulating IFN response during SARS-CoV-2 infection [ 51 ]. Several studies have also demonstrated that other endosomal TLRs play important roles in the onset and progression of SARS-CoV-2, including TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 [ 52 , 53 ]. Strikingly, numerous clinical studies have consistently underscored the critical role of TLR7 in SARS-CoV-2 infection, distinguishing it from other TLRs that play significant roles in this infection [ 54 – 57 ]. Clinical insights into TLR7 mutations and severe COVID-19 in men Extensive clinical evidence demonstrates that the lower expression of TLR7 in men, facilitated by its escape from X-chromosome inactivation, results in weaker antiviral immunity and contributes to the higher severity of COVID-19 in men compared to women [ 57 , 58 ]. Multiple studies have highlighted the role of TLR7 mutations in severe male COVID-19 outcomes, with robust clinical evidence across diverse regions. For example, a study investigating rare TLR7 variants in young, severe COVID-19 cases from Spain and Italy revealed that 2.4% of males under 60 years old with no comorbidities carried deleterious TLR7 mutations, compared to only 0.24% in controls [ 54 ]. In an Italian cohort, rare loss-of-function variants in TLR7 were identified in 2.1% of severely affected young males, with functional analyses confirming impaired interferon pathways [ 55 ]. A case–control study in the Iranian population further supported these findings, demonstrating a significant association between polymorphisms in TLR7 and increased susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. The study included 244 hospitalized patients and 156 controls, with genotyping conducted through PCR–RFLP [ 56 ]. Another investigation analyzed 120 patients, demonstrating that specific TLR7 SNPs were associated with COVID-19 severity, particularly in male patients with comorbidities [ 59 ]. Epigenomic studies have also revealed sex-biased expression of TLR7, which may contribute to differences in disease severity between males and females. Transcriptomic analyses and CpG methylation studies found consistent downregulation of TLR7 in severe male cases compared to females [ 57 ]. Collectively, these findings emphasize the critical role of TLR7 in mediating antiviral immunity and highlight its influence on the observed sex bias in COVID-19 severity. Clinical insights into TLR7 mutations and severe COVID-19 in women Interestingly, TLR7 has been implicated in COVID-19 severity not only in males but also in females. In the study conducted in Europe, association signals for TLR7 rare deleterious were also detected in female-only young severe COVID-19 subgroups, suggesting mechanisms beyond X-linked recessive inheritance [ 54 ]. Similarly, an exome-wide association study conducted across 12 countries and including over 5,000 severe COVID-19 cases found that rare TLR7 variants increased the risk of severe disease by 5.3-fold in both sexes. This association was validated through whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing [ 60 ]. These findings broaden our understanding of TLR7’s role in COVID-19 pathogenesis, emphasizing its critical contribution to disease severity in women. Mechanistic insights from clinical and in vitro studies on TLR7-mediated signaling in severe COVID-19 The mechanistic studies on TLR7 in severe COVID-19, derived from clinical sample analyses and in vitro experiments, reveal significant insights into the pathogenesis of the disease. Several in vitro studies have tested the role of TLR7 in combating SARS-CoV-2 infection by TLR7 agonists [ 61 , 62 ]. In a vitro study investigating TLR7/8 signaling pathways, single-stranded RNA from SARS-CoV-2 was found to directly activate the TLR7/8-MyD88 pathway in patient-derived PBMCs, leading to dendritic cell (DC) activation, production of type I IFNs and cytokines, and polarization toward a Th1 immune response [ 63 ]. Another in vitro study observed that the TLR7/8 agonist R848 was unable to fully restore immune responses in severe COVID-19 cases, underscoring the complexity of immune dysregulation in such patients [ 64 ]. Further research has revealed variability in cell responses to TLR7 agonists, with some individuals exhibiting full restoration of immune signaling while others showed only partial or no response. These differences appear to depend significantly on the specific mutations in TLR7 and their functional consequences [ 55 ]. Several investigations have used imiquimod, a TLR7 specific agonist, to study its efficacy in stimulating immune responses. Using in vitro agonist treatment, these studies demonstrated that TLR7 signaling activation induces the production of type I and type II interferons, although its effectiveness varies depending on the genetic makeup of the patients [ 65 – 68 ]. For example, studies identified decreased expression of IFN-related genes such as IRF7 and ISG15 in some severe or critical COVID-19 individuals with TLR7 variants, reflecting an impaired response to stimulation [ 65 – 68 ]. Clinical data also highlights that in patients with severe COVID-19, key downstream components of TLR7 signaling, such as IRF7, were significantly downregulated, leading to reduced IFN production. For example, in one study, decreased levels of IRF7 and type I IFNs were observed in patient PBMCs stimulated with TLR7 agonists [ 55 ]. Another study reported a loss of functional TLR7 signaling in PBMCs, as evidenced by decreased IFN production upon stimulation with imiquimod, a TLR7 agonist [ 65 ]. Similarly, analyses of TLR7-deficient individuals revealed marked reductions in IRF7, IFNB1, and ISG15 mRNA levels, further indicating impaired downstream signaling [ 6 ]. These observations were corroborated by another investigation demonstrating reduced IRF7 levels in COVID-19 patients with genetic deficiencies affecting TLR7 [ 69 ]. The role of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) in TLR7-mediated responses has also been emphasized. In individuals with severe COVID-19, pDCs were found to produce significantly lower levels of IFNs in response to stimulation, highlighting their pivotal role in antiviral immunity [ 67 ]. Further, mutations affecting downstream signaling proteins, such as MYD88 and IRAK4, were shown to exacerbate disease severity by impairing TLR7-dependent pathways, underscoring the broader significance of this signaling axis in immune defense [ 70 ]. Additional mechanistic insights were provided by RNA sequencing experiments that analyzed transcriptomic changes following imiquimod stimulation in PBMCs. These studies identified reduced expression of genes such as IFNA1, IFIH1, TBK1, RSAD2, ACOD1, IFIT2, and CXCL10, all of which are critical to effective antiviral responses [ 68 ]. Another investigation explored the impact of TLR7 mutations on MYD88 and TIRAP interactions, demonstrating that these mutations disrupted complex formation, significantly impairing downstream signaling. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) was also identified as a potential therapeutic agent capable of enhancing TLR7-MyD88-TIRAP pathway function in these deficient systems [ 15 ]. In conclusion, these clinical observation studies highlight the critical role of TLR7, TLR7-MyD88-TIRAP pathway, IRF7 activation, and interferon production role in severe COVID-19. Dysregulated TLR7 signaling, particularly in pDCs, leads to impaired antiviral immunity. However, clinical observation studies have some limitations for further dissecting early events of pathogen and host interaction due to the difficulty in the collections of early infected cells and pulmonary tissues in COVID-19 patients. Mechanistic insights from In Vivo studies on TLR7 in severe COVID-19 To overcome these limitations, experimental studies are much needed for dissecting the underlying mechanisms. Ghimire et al. demonstrates that TLR7 deficiency in BALB/c mice increases susceptibility to a mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 (MA-CoV-2) strain, showing that it mediates both IFN responses and immune cell recruitment [ 71 ]. By employing single-cell RNA sequencing and bone marrow transplantation, we have examined TLR7 and IRF7 deficiencies in mice to dissect their effects on immune responses to SARS-CoV2 infection [ 72 ]. TLR7- and IRF7-deficient mice displayed substantially reduced production of IFNα and IFNγ, which impaired both innate and adaptive immunity [ 72 ]. Reduced nuclear translocation of IRF7 and diminished activation of downstream signaling were observed [ 72 ]. Additionally, antibody production against SARS-CoV-2 was delayed in these deficient mice, leading to increased viral loads and severe lung pathology [ 72 ]. This study highlighted TLR7's critical role in bridging innate and adaptive immune responses by facilitating effective IFN production and subsequent antibody generation [ 72 ]. Despite these contributions, key questions remain unresolved. It is unclear how and where TLR7 and TLR7-mediated signaling are activated to contribute to innate immunity against SARS-CoV2 infection in the severe COVID-19 and what endogenous or viral factors drive these activations. Furthermore, the role of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), which are pivotal producers of type I IFNs, in TLR7-mediated immune responses requires further investigation. The precise mechanisms by which TLR7 signaling supports adaptive immunity, particularly through interactions with other immune cells, are also yet to be elucidated. These gaps underscore the need for more comprehensive in vivo studies to clarify the cellular and molecular pathways modulated by TLR7 during acute severe SARS-CoV-2 infection. TLR7 dysregulation and immune persistence in long COVID The role of TLR7 in long COVID has garnered attention due to its potential involvement in sustained immune dysregulation post-acute infection. Evidence from clinical and genetic studies highlights impaired TLR7 signaling as a key factor. For example, a study involving 50 patients with post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) demonstrated reduced cytokine responses, including IFNα, IFNω, IL-1β, and TNFα, upon TLR7/8 stimulation, suggesting receptor exhaustion potentially caused by persistent viral RNA activation [ 73 ]. Another case study of a pediatric patient with long neuro-COVID identified a novel TLR7 variant leading to diminished interferon production and antiviral responses, implicating TLR7 deficiency in prolonged viral persistence and immune dysfunction [ 74 ]. These findings underscore TLR7’s critical role in regulating antiviral immunity and cytokine balance during recovery from SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, the available data are limited, and further research is needed to clarify how TLR7 activation, downstream signaling, and interactions with adaptive immunity contribute to long COVID pathogenesis. It is important to note that the chronic inflammation observed in long COVID shares similarities with autoimmune diseases, where TLR7-driven IFN production is also elevated and detrimental. In both conditions, prolonged activation of TLR7 leads to persistent immune activation, contributing to tissue damage and exacerbating clinical symptoms. For example, elevated IFN levels in long COVID patients are comparable to those observed in systemic autoimmune disorders such as lupus, where chronic inflammation underpins disease progression. These parallels between long COVID and autoimmune diseases suggest shared mechanisms of TLR7 activation and IFN overproduction. For this reason, we will also review the role of TLR7 in systemic lupus erythematosus in the later section. Understanding these commonalities could provide valuable insights into therapeutic approaches that modulate TLR7 signaling to reduce chronic inflammation while preserving its protective antiviral effects in the early stages of infection. This biphasic role of IFN, being protective in the acute phase but detrimental in the chronic phase, highlights the complexity of TLR7-mediated immune responses. The intricate interplay between TLR7 signaling and IFN dynamics underlines the need for a deeper understanding of how TLR7 regulates both the innate and adaptive arms of immunity. Therapeutic potential of targeting TLR7-signaling for COVID-19. Researchers have proposed novel applications of TLR7 agonists in vaccine development. For instance, one study introduced a dual TLR7/8 agonist adsorbed onto aluminum salts as a vaccine adjuvant candidate. This approach elicited a robust and balanced immune response, demonstrating potential for use in COVID-19 vaccine platforms [ 75 ]. In addition, TLR7 agonists also could serve as early-stage treatments to potentiate the innate responses for mitigating COVID-19 severity. In summary, TLR7 and its downstream signaling pathways play a crucial role in antiviral defense, contributing to immune protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection. In addition to its role in viral clearance, TLR7 has been implicated in antibody production, which may influence long-term immunity and potentially contribute to the pathogenesis of long COVID. However, the precise cellular mechanisms linking TLR7 to both innate and adaptive immune responses in SARS-CoV-2 infection remain incompletely understood. Further research is needed to dissect how TLR7-mediated pathways regulate antiviral immunity, particularly in balancing protective versus pathogenic responses. Animal studies and conditional knockout models will be essential to delineate the specific contributions of TLR7 in distinct immune cell populations. Understanding these mechanisms may provide insights into targeted therapeutic strategies for COVID-19 and post-viral complications such as long COVID. 3) The role of activation of TLR7 and downstream signaling in influenza virus infection The influenza virus continues to pose a significant public health challenge, due to its rapid genetic evolution, particularly through antigenic drift in surface proteins hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) [ 76 ]. Antigenic drift arises primarily due to the error-prone nature of the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, which introduces mutations during replication [ 77 ]. These changes enable the virus to evade prior immunity, necessitating the annual reformulation of vaccines [ 78 ]. While adaptive immunity targets variable regions of the virus, innate immune receptors recognize conserved viral features. Among these, TLR7, which detects single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), has emerged as a receptor of interest in influenza immunity. TLR7 is not essential for controlling acute influenza virus infection. Despite its ability to detect viral RNA, TLR7 activation may not be an essential component of the innate immunity against acute influenza virus infection. Unlike SARS-CoV-2, where mutations in the TLR7 gene have been associated with increased disease severity and poor acute clinical outcomes, no clinical studies have demonstrated a similar association between TLR7 mutations and the clinical course of influenza A virus infection. Several genetic factors have been identified during the 2009 H1N1 pandemic or in immunologically naïve infants and children. To date, variations in about 25 host genes have been associated with IAV outcomes [ 79 ], with some findings independently validated; however, none of them have been linked directly with TLR7. Experimentally, mice lacking TLR7 can still effectively clear H1N1 virus, pointing to redundancy in the innate immune system to influenza virus infection [ 16 ]. In support of this, a clinical study comparing TLR7 levels in the blood of healthy volunteers, H1N1-infected patients, and pneumonia patients found no significant differences in expression among these groups, indicating that systemic TLR7 level do not correlates with disease severity [ 80 ]. Furthermore, an in vitro and in vivo study using TLR7 agonist also documented that that pDCs were able to mount an immune response against influenza virus infection without relying on TLR7 [ 81 ]. This suggests that alternative mechanisms exist for pDC-mediated antiviral immunity against influenza virus infection [ 81 ]. Using wild-type C57BL/6 and TLR7 knockout mice, Miles et al. recently found an underappreciated duality of TLR7 in IAV disease, namely promoting inflammation-driven lung dysfunction during the acute infection but suppressing eosinophilic and CD8 + T cell-dependent hyperresponsiveness during disease resolution [ 82 ]. Further, influenza A virus and TLR7 activation potentiate NOX2 oxidase-dependent ROS production in macrophages [ 83 ], indicating that activation of TLR7 contribute to excessive inflammation leading to tissue injury [ 83 ]. Moreover, a large clinical study for HIV therapy, assessed the effects of TLR7 agonist Vesatolimod in over 600 participants in which subjects received varying doses of the agonist to assess its immunogenic effects [ 84 ]. The study found that higher doses of the agonist correlated with an increased likelihood of adverse events, including influenza-like symptoms, suggesting that excessive TLR7 activation can lead to heightened inflammatory responses rather than direct antiviral protection [ 84 ]. Together, these findings highlight potential for TLR7 to contribute to immunopathology when not properly regulated, arguing against TLR7 being an indispensable component of early innate immune responses to influenza virus infection. Critical role of TLR7 and its-signaling in adaptive immunity against influenza virus infection Although TLR7 is not essential for acute influenza viral clearance, TLR7 plays a pivotal role in coordinating adaptive immune responses to influenza virus infection. Studies in TLR7-deficient mice reveal impaired antibody production following influenza vaccination, emphasizing the role of TLR7 in promoting humoral immunity, where the absence of TLR7 leads to reduced virus-specific antibodies in the serum and antibody-secreting cells in their secondary lymphoid organs, particularly in bone marrow 16[. TLR7 signaling has been proven to be crucial for neutrophil activation and inflammatory cytokine production [ 85 ]. In addition, using TLR7 knockout murine models, TLR7 was underscored the critical role in IFN-γ production, attenuated NK cell activation, long-term immunity, and T cell responses [ 86 – 88 ]. These priming events in the immune response lead to the production of type I interferons (IFNs) and pro-inflammatory cytokines that serve as essential signals for the differentiation and maturation of immune effector cells. TLR7 signaling promotes isotype switching and affinity maturation in B cells by engaging the MyD88 pathway, leading to downstream activation of interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7) and NF-κB [ 89 ]. This cascade facilitates the generation of germinal centers and long-lived plasma cells, ultimately enhancing the production of high-affinity antibodies. In TLR7-deficient mice, this pathway is disrupted, resulting in diminished germinal center formation and a blunted humoral response to influenza virus. These findings align with studies demonstrating that TLR7 is indispensable for thew efficacy of inactivated or subunit vaccines, which rely heavily on the host’s ability to mount a strong antibody-mediated defense. Further supporting the role of TLR7 in adaptive immunity are studies employing TLR7 agonists as vaccine adjuvants. Agonists such as imiquimod and resiquimod have been shown to enhance the immunogenicity of influenza vaccines by boosting both humoral and cellular responses. In murine models, administration of TLR7 agonists alongside influenza virus antigens resulted in increased levels of antigen-specific IgG2a and IgG2b, indicative of a T helper 1 (Th1)-biased response [ 90 ]. Moreover, TLR7 signaling enhances the cross-presentation of viral antigens to CD8 + T cells, fostering the development of cytotoxic lymphocytes crucial for clearing infected cells. Mechanistically, this involves upregulation of MHC class I molecules and co-stimulatory signals such as CD80 and CD86 on dendritic cells, further supported by type I IFNs as third signals for T cell activation [ 91 ]. These findings establish TLR7 not only as a sensor of viral RNA but as a critical amplifier of adaptive immunity. Consequently, understanding and leveraging TLR7-mediated pathways offers a promising avenue for improving vaccine formulations and achieving long-term protective immunity against influenza A virus infection. Future studies should focus on the interplay between endosomal and cytoplasmic PRRs, especially in the context of viral immune evasion and vaccine response. Such integrative insights will be essential to design effective therapeutics that harness the innate immune system without provoking harmful inflammation. In summary, While TLR7 is not the primary PRR responsible for initiating the innate immune response to influenza virus infection in epithelial cells, its role in immune cell activation, cytokine production, and the support of adaptive immunity is both biologically significant and context dependent. 4) TLR7 signaling activation in RSV RSV poses a significant global health burden, particularly among neonates and elderly individuals. Epidemiological studies indicate that RSV infects over two-thirds of newborns within the first year of life, leading to severe respiratory complications in susceptible populations [ 92 ]. RSV is a negative-sense ssRNA virus of the Pneumoviridae family that infects airway epithelial cells and activates innate immune signaling through various TLRs [ 93 , 94 ]. The immune response to RSV involves a delicate balance between viral clearance and the risk of immunopathology. Excessive inflammation, marked by elevated levels of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and IL-17, contributes to epithelial damage, mucus hypersecretion, and airway obstruction [ 95 ]. In severe cases, particularly among infants and elderly individuals with compromised immunity, RSV infection progresses to lower respiratory tract disease, resulting in bronchiolitis or pneumonia [ 95 ]. Current therapies remain largely supportive, underscoring the need for new antiviral and immunomodulatory approaches. There is no clinical or genetic evidence from human studies supporting a role for TLR7 in RSV disease. Among TLRs, TLR4 remains the most clinically relevant receptor in RSV infection, with multiple genetic studies linking TLR4 variants to disease severity [ 96 – 100 ]. In contrast, the role of TLR7 has been investigated only in experimental settings. Future studies examining TLR7 signaling and its downstream mediators may provide critical insights into its potential as a therapeutic target. Experimental evidence supporting the role of TLR7 in RSV disease Unlike the limited clinical data, several in vivo and in vitro studies provide evidence supporting the relevance of TLR7 in RSV infection. A study highlights the importance of innate immunity over adaptive immunity in the host response to RSV infection [ 101 ]. Another study discussed the molecular mechanisms through which TLR7 detects RSV viral components, contributing to immune system activation [ 102 ]. In vitro experiments collectively establish that RSV infection directly activates TLR7, driving key immune responses. In one study, A549 cells, a model of human respiratory epithelial cells, demonstrated elevated TLR7 levels following RSV infection [ 103 ]. Similarly, another study using in vivo mouse models and infected cells confirmed that TLR7 is activated following RSV infection. The study showed that respiratory macrophages and dendritic cells depend on TLR7 activation to mediate the production of IL-33, a key cytokine in the immune response to RSV [ 104 ]. However, the effects of TLR7 signaling appear context dependent. Some studies show the detrimental effects of TLR7 activation in RSV infection. In one study, the authors infected wild-type and TLR7 knockout mice with RSV, and demonstrated that TLR7 activation contributed to prolonged inflammation, suggesting a detrimental role in RSV infection [ 105 ]. The authors suggested that TLR7-driven inflammation may influence the chronic phase by altering the immunological milieu of the lung, potentially shaping the adaptive response [ 105 ]. Another study supported this observation, using similar in vivo models to show that TLR7-mediated responses exacerbated inflammation, worsening the disease course [ 106 ]. Conversely, several studies highlight the beneficial role of TLR7 in RSV infection. One investigation used TLR7-deficient mice to assess the immune response to RSV. While TLR7 activation did not impact type I interferon production, it was associated with reduced airway inflammation and a protective Th17-mediated response [ 107 ]. Another study demonstrated that TLR7 activation enhances emergency myelopoiesis and antiviral immunity in mice, reducing RSV viral loads [ 108 ]. Additional research employing mouse models and in vitro experiments confirmed TLR7’s positive effects, such as improved viral clearance and immune regulation [ 96 , 109 – 111 ]. In contrast, some studies found no significant link between TLR7 activation and RSV infection outcomes. One experiment using TLR7-deficient and wild-type mice showed no measurable differences in disease severity or immune responses, suggesting TLR7’s limited role in certain contexts [ 112 ]. The role of TLR7 in RSV infection has been investigated in aged mice. Given that RSV disproportionately affects elderly individuals with more severe disease and worse clinical outcomes, these experiments in aged mice hold significant clinical relevance. A few studies highlighted the broader role of TLR7 and other TLRs in activating innate immunity during RSV infection, with aging dampening these responses and exacerbating disease severity [ 113 , 114 ]. These findings underscore the importance of studying TLR7 signaling in elderly models, as they mirror the clinical challenges faced by older patients with RSV and pave the way for targeted interventions aimed at this vulnerable population. In conclusion, the exact role of TLR7 in RSV infection remains unclear due to the lack of clinical evidence and the conflicting findings from in vitro and in vivo studies. While some studies highlight the detrimental or beneficial effects of TLR7 activation, others show no significant association. Despite these uncertainties, the critical role of pDCs and the MyD88-dependent interferon pathway in modulating the immune response to RSV is evident. These components likely play an integral role in determining the host’s response to the virus. Further exploration is essential to uncover the precise mechanisms by which TLR7 contributes to RSV pathogenesis and to resolve the current controversies surrounding its role. 5) The role of TLR7 in systemic lupus erythematosus Autoimmune diseases arise from losing immune tolerance, leading the immune system to attack healthy tissues. These conditions affect individuals of all ages, with a higher prevalence in women, and are modulated by genetic and environmental factors [ 115 ]. Autoimmune diseases affect approximately 3% to 5% of the world’s population, encompassing a wide range of disorders, from organ-specific diseases to complex systemic conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [ 116 ]. The vast diversity of these diseases makes understanding the underlying mechanisms that drive immune dysregulation crucial to developing effective interventions. Among these, SLE stands out due to its complexity and the involvement of innate immune receptors like TLR7, making it essential to understand specific mechanisms driving immune dysregulation. Research challenges include determining whether dysregulated immune responses are the primary cause or a secondary event in autoimmune diseases. Recent studies suggest that this dysregulation is mediated by factors such as innate receptors like TLR7, which play a central role in aberrant immune responses [ 18 , 117 ]. This section overviews TLR7-mediated signaling in SLE, highlighting clinical and experimental findings that elucidate the receptor's contribution to pathogenesis. Clinical relevance The SLE is characterized by autoreactive B and T cell responses, leading to immune tolerance loss and immune complex buildup in tissues. Clinical presentations range from mild symptoms like fatigue and joint pain to severe organ damage, such as kidney failure and cardiovascular complications [ 118 ]. In North America, SLE affects 23.3 per 100,000 people, disproportionately impacting African Americans, Hispanics, and Asians compared to whites [ 119 , 120 ]. This racial disparity and genetic diversity complicate diagnosis and management. Patients have mortality rates 2–3 times higher than the general population, with lupus nephritis, cardiovascular issues, and infections being the primary causes of death [ 121 ]. Genetic variations in the TLR7 gene are linked to increased SLE risk and distinct clinical features. For instance, variants in the UNC93B1 gene hyperactivate TLR7 signaling, leading to excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines in early-onset SLE [ 17 ]. Polymorphisms such as rs179008 and rs3853839 affect TLR7 expression, resulting in increased receptor activity associated with greater disease severity in SLE patients [ 122 , 123 ]. Additionally, increased TLR7 gene copy number is associated with higher SLE risk due to TLR7 overexpression and enhanced autoantibody production, especially when combined with specific polymorphisms [ 124 ]. These results reinforce TLR7's central role in the genetic predisposition to SLE. In addition to genetic variations, Murayama et al. [ 125 ] showed that prolonged retention of TLR7 in late endosome/lysosome compartments in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) from SLE patients results in an exacerbated production of interferon-alpha (IFN-α), a key cytokine directly related to disease activity and systemic inflammation. These findings demonstrate the link between TLR7 hyperactivity and the worsening of SLE clinical manifestations [ 125 ]. Therefore, targeting the TLR7 signaling pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy, offering new perspectives for managing this multifaceted disease. Moreover, the female predisposition to TLR7-mediated autoimmunity, particularly in diseases like SLE, is strongly linked to the escape of TLR7 from X-chromosome inactivation. This escape leads to biallelic expression of TLR7 in immune cells, especially B cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), enhancing their response to TLR7 activation [ 126 ]. Increased expression of the X-linked TLR7 gene due to impaired X-chromosome inactivation contributes to the sex bias in SLE, with women exhibiting a more robust TLR7-driven immune response and higher susceptibility to the disease [ 126 , 127 ]. Experimental studies The TLR7 has been extensively studied in SLE, with experimental models revealing crucial aspects of its contribution to pathogenesis. Although the exact mechanisms are not yet fully understood, these studies have significantly expanded our understanding of the central role of TLR7 in the initiation and progression of lupus, opening up new therapeutic possibilities. Therefore, murine models are essential to investigate the pathogenic mechanisms of SLE and test new therapeutic approaches. Transgenic and knockout models allow studying the overexpression or deletion of specific genes, clarifying the role of TLR7 in immunity. Although no model fully captures the complexity of human lupus, these studies provide valuable information to understand the disease and explore new therapeutic targets [ 128 ]. Brown et al. [ 18 ] identified the gain-of-function variant TLR7 Y264H in a child with severe lupus. By introducing this variant into mice, researchers observed splenomegaly, increased antinuclear antibodies (ANAs), and reduced survival. In addition, the mutation led to the accumulation of age-associated B cells (ABCs) and increased germinal center (GC) B cells. The deficiency of MyD88, an adaptor protein of the TLR7 pathway, reversed these phenotypes, indicating that TLR7 signaling is essential for the autoimmune response in SLE [ 18 ]. Goel et al. [ 129 ] demonstrated that activation of TLR7 by imiquimod, an agonist, increased the production of interferon lambda (IFN-λ) and exacerbated autoimmune responses in mice. These animals developed splenomegaly and hematologic dysfunctions, such as leukocytosis and thrombocytopenia, characteristics of a dysregulated immune system. Interestingly, IFN-λ receptor knockout mice showed significant reductions in inflammation, suggesting that IFN-λ acts as a critical mediator in TLR7-induced SLE pathology [ 129 ]. Cosgrove et al. [ 130 ] investigated the role of TLR7 on conventional B cells and dendritic cells (DCs) in developing SLE. Using MRL/lpr mice with selective deletion of TLR7 on B cells, the researchers found that TLR7 deficiency in B cells reduced proteinuria and glomerular disease. The absence of TLR7 was especially beneficial in Tlr9-deficient mice, suggesting that the interaction between TLR9 and TLR7 on B cells plays a crucial role in autoimmunity. These results support the idea that inhibiting TLR7 while promoting TLR9 activity may be a promising strategy for treating SLE [ 130 ]. Moreover, TLR8 plays a complementary role; while TLR7 induces type I interferons, TLR8 promotes pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-12 [ 131 ]. These interactions imply that therapeutic strategies targeting TLR7 inhibition or TLR9 enhancement could mitigate inflammation and autoimmunity in lupus. Zhang et al. [ 132 ] provided additional evidence by demonstrating that the reduction of TLR7 mRNA expression induced by the herbal formula Jiedu-Quyu-Ziyin Fang decreased autoimmune activity in MRL/lpr mice. The decrease in TLR7 was correlated with reduced anti-dsDNA antibodies and increased serum C3 levels. These findings indicate that modulating TLR7 expression effectively controls inflammation and autoantibody production in SLE [ 132 ]. Potential mechanisms TLR7, a Toll-like receptor located in endosomes and encoded by an X chromosome-linked gene, serves as a dual receptor for guanosine and uridine-containing ssRNA [ 133 , 134 ]. Under normal conditions, TLR7 detects viral RNA and activates innate immune responses via the MyD88-mediated signaling pathway, recruiting adaptor proteins such as IRAK and TRAF6. This leads to the activation of transcription factors NF-κB and IRF7, responsible for producing pro-inflammatory cytokines and type I interferons, respectively [ 133 , 135 , 136 ]. In the context of SLE, defective clearance of apoptotic cells results in the accumulation of self-nucleic acids, responsible for the initiation of systemic autoimmunity [ 137 ]. Persistent activation of TLR7 in autoreactive B cells leads to amplified inflammatory responses and the generation of autoantibodies, exacerbating disease pathogenesis [ 133 , 138 ]. This hyperactivation directly contributes to the excessive production of type I interferons, linking self-RNA detection to the development of autoimmunity. Elevated levels of circulating IFN-α in patients with SLE correlate with the presence of autoantibodies against RNA-binding proteins. Moreover, the overexpression of interferon-stimulated genes in blood cells underscores the central role of type I interferon in SLE pathogenesis [ 139 – 141 ]. TLR7-mediated activation of B cells induces NF-κB signaling, leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12, which foster a chronic inflammatory environment. This promotes B cell maturation and autoantibody production, resulting in the formation of pathogenic immune complexes that deposit in tissues and exacerbate the clinical manifestations of SLE [ 16 ]. Additionally, TLR7 activation drives germinal center development and differentiation into plasmablasts, aggravating disease progression [ 142 ]. Certain B cell subsets, such as age-associated B cells (ABCs) and DN2 cells, are particularly susceptible to TLR7 activation, leading to their expansion and increased production of autoantibodies like antinuclear antibodies (ANAs), which are critical in autoimmune diseases [ 136 , 143 , 144 ]. In pDCs, TLR7 activation amplifies the production of IFN-α, which intensifies the activation of mononuclear cells, B cells, and T cells, thereby perpetuating autoimmunity [ 145 , 146 ]. The released IFN-α induces the expression of interferon-stimulated genes, further enhancing immune cell activation and sustaining the chronic inflammation characteristic of SLE [ 142 ]. A critical aspect of SLE pathogenesis involves a self-amplifying mechanism where autoantibody-containing immune complexes stimulate pDCs to release type I interferons. These complexes, composed of autoantibodies bound to self-nucleic acids, are internalized by pDCs through Fc gamma receptors, leading to TLR7 activation within endosomes [ 142 ]. This process sustains inflammatory responses and promotes the maturation of monocytes into dendritic cells. The cycle continues as autoreactive CD4 + T cells activate CD8 + T cells and B cells, contributing to the chronic inflammation characteristic of SLE. This reciprocal interaction between autoantibodies and type I interferons intensifies disease progression. Therapeutic implications of targeting TLR7 Novel therapeutic targets for SLE are imperative due to the substantial disease burden and the limited efficacy of existing treatments. Current therapies—including glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants like mycophenolate mofetil and azathioprine, and biologic agents such as belimumab and anifrolumab—aim to suppress the immune system but often come with significant side effects and may not provide durable remission or prevent long-term damage [ 147 , 148 ]. Given TLR7's central role in SLE pathogenesis, selective antagonists have emerged as promising strategies. These agents aim to block aberrant TLR7 signaling, thereby reducing inflammatory cytokine production and improving clinical outcomes. The selective TLR7 and TLR8 inhibitor M5049 (enpatoran) demonstrated efficacy in preclinical lupus models by inhibiting inflammatory cytokine production in vitro and significantly reducing proteinuria, autoantibody levels, and type I interferon-associated gene activation in vivo [ 149 ]. Subsequently, Port et al. [ 150 ] conducted a phase 1, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial with 96 healthy participants to evaluate enpatoran's safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics. The drug was well-tolerated at all doses, with no significant adverse events, and demonstrated a dose-proportional pharmacokinetic profile with rapid absorption. Notably, enpatoran effectively inhibited TLR7/8-mediated cytokine production, including IL-6 and IFN-α, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for diseases characterized by TLR7/8 overactivation. Further studies are needed to assess its long-term safety and efficacy in SLE patients [ 150 ]. Similarly, the dual TLR7/8 inhibitor MHV370 showed promise in preclinical and early clinical studies. In vitro, MHV370 inhibited interferon-α and other pro-inflammatory cytokines in human and murine cells. In lupus-prone mice, it reduced proteinuria, preserved renal function, and decreased immune complex deposition in kidneys, modulating autoantibodies targeting RNA-containing antigens while leaving anti-dsDNA antibodies unaffected [ 151 ]. A phase 1 study confirmed MHV370 was well-tolerated in healthy adults, achieving complete inhibition of CD69 expression on B cells and over 90% inhibition of TNF secretion at clinically relevant doses. Despite mild increases in serum creatinine, the overall safety profile was favorable. These results warrant further investigation in SLE patients, considering sex differences and the need for chronic treatment strategies [ 152 ]. E6742, another TLR7/8 antagonist, presents compelling evidence for its role in lupus treatment. It significantly reduced cytokine production, including IL-6 and IFN-α, and mitigated key lupus features such as proteinuria, kidney damage, and elevated RNA-related autoantibodies in murine models [ 153 ]. By selectively blocking TLR7-driven immune responses, particularly interferon-stimulated gene expression, E6742 highlights TLR7 as a pivotal therapeutic target. Collectively, these advancements underscore the potential of TLR7 antagonists as innovative therapies for SLE. Future research should prioritize clinical trials to evaluate these compounds' efficacy and safety in SLE patients. Additionally, exploring strategies to enhance TLR9 activity or modulate the TLR7/TLR9 balance may offer synergistic benefits, further improving therapeutic outcomes. TLR7 is central to SLE pathogenesis, mediating aberrant immune responses that lead to chronic inflammation and autoimmunity. Genetic factors—including variations, overexpression, and escape from X-chromosome inactivation—contribute to its hyperactivity, particularly in women, explaining the sex bias in SLE prevalence. Interactions with other Toll-like receptors like TLR9 and TLR8 further modulate disease progression. Activation of immune cells such as B cells, pDCs, ABCs, and DN2 cells by TLR7 results in excessive autoantibody production and tissue damage. Understanding TLR7's role positions it as a promising therapeutic target. Selective TLR7 antagonists like M5049, MHV370, and E6742 have shown efficacy in preclinical and early clinical models. Future research should focus on clinical trials of these agents and explore strategies to modulate the TLR7/TLR9 balance. Advancements in technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing and CRISPR gene editing could further elucidate TLR7's role in SLE and aid in developing personalized therapies, ultimately improving outcomes for SLE patients. 6) Conclusion and discussion TLR7 is a key innate immune sensor that recognizes ssRNA and initiates type I IFN production and proinflammatory signaling. While SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, and RSV are all ssRNA viruses, current evidence suggests they elicit distinct immune responses through TLR7 signaling. SARS-CoV-2 infection is characterized by rapid and robust TLR7 activation, leading to early IFN responses and subsequent antibody production, linking innate and adaptive immunity. In contrast, influenza virus and RSV demonstrate inconsistent roles for TLR7 in innate immune activation, and the connection between TLR7 and adaptive immune responses, particularly antibody production, remains poorly defined. These observations highlight a critical knowledge gap: why do ssRNA viruses, sharing a common ligand for TLR7, induce divergent immune outcomes? The mechanisms underlying TLR7 activation, the specific cell types involved, and the pathways that bridge innate and adaptive responses—especially antibody production—require further investigation. Conditional knockout mouse models targeting TLR7 in defined immune cell populations offer a promising strategy to delineate cell-specific roles of TLR7 in response to each virus. Such models could not only clarify how TLR7 contributes to immunity or pathology in a virus-specific manner but also inform the development of targeted therapies that modulate TLR7 signaling appropriately for each pathogen. In the context of autoimmune diseases such as SLE, where TLR7 is strongly implicated in driving autoantibody production and systemic inflammation, key mechanistic questions also remain unresolved. It is unclear what endogenous stimuli initiate TLR7 activation, how dysregulation of this pathway sustains autoreactive B cell responses, and which immune cell subsets are principally involved. A deeper understanding of these processes is essential not only for elucidating disease biology but also for designing precision immunotherapies aimed at selectively dampening pathogenic TLR7 signaling while preserving its protective antiviral functions. Taken together, TLR7 plays a multifaceted and context-dependent role in immunity. Elucidating the cell-type–specific mechanisms of TLR7 activation and function across viral infections and autoimmunity is essential for advancing both basic immunology and translational medicine. Future work leveraging conditional genetics, single-cell profiling, and viral infection models will be crucial in harnessing TLR7’s therapeutic potential.
