# Paradoxical glycemia in an adult with ABCC8 p.Arg1352His: a case of maturity-onset diabetes of the young, type 12

## Abstract

Abstract Adults presenting with atypical diabetes, especially those with unexplained hypoglycemia, often pose a significant diagnostic challenge. Here, we describe a 53-year-old woman initially treated for type 2 diabetes who later developed recurrent hypoglycemia, with increased severity after fundoplication surgery, despite medication cessation, exhibiting persistent, and paradoxical glycemic variability. Detailed metabolic investigation ultimately revealed a heterozygous ABCC8 p.Arg1352His pathogenic variant, confirming a diagnosis of maturity-onset diabetes of the young, type 12. This case underscores the crucial role of genetic testing in diagnosing complex, monogenic glycemic disorders and illustrates the unique management complexities of a dual hyperglycemic–hypoglycemic phenotype, emphasizing the need for heightened clinical suspicion of an underlying genetic disorder potentially exacerbated by upper gastrointestinal surgery.


## Introduction

Monogenic disorders caused by variants in genes linked to insulin secretion can manifest paradoxical glycemic patterns, sometimes causing both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia in the same individual over time [ 1 ]. One such example is related to variants in the ABCC8 gene, which encodes the sulfonylurea receptor 1 (SUR1) subunit of the pancreatic β-cell KATP channel, a key regulator of insulin secretion [ 2 ] ( Fig. 1 ). Inactivating (loss-of-function) variants in ABCC8 (often biallelic) are a leading cause of neonatal-onset hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia [ 3 ]. Conversely, activating (gain-of-function) ABCC8 variants impair insulin secretion and can cause diabetes, including neonatal or young-onset monogenic diabetes [ 2 ]. Notably, certain heterozygous ABCC8 variants give rise to a dual phenotype; patients may present initially with hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and later develop diabetes related to inadequate insulin secretion [ 4 , 5 ]. Thus, a single ABCC8 variant can cause both hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and monogenic diabetes, with highly variable age of onset and clinical course.

One such ABCC8 variant is p.Arg1352His, which has been documented as a pathogenic cause of both hyperinsulinism and maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) [ 3 , 6-11 ]. We report the case of a 53-year-old woman with a heterozygous ABCC8 c.4055G>A, p.Arg1352His variant who was initially managed as type 2 diabetes (T2D) and subsequently developed recurrent hypoglycemia after fundoplication. This case highlights the importance of considering genetic testing in atypical diabetes and illustrates the clinical challenges of managing a dual glycemic phenotype associated with an ABCC8 variant.

## Case presentation

A 53-year-old female with a history of T2D, gastroesophageal reflux, Nissen fundoplication (age 49), postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), and dulaglutide-induced pancreatitis presented for evaluation of recurrent hypoglycemia.

T2D was diagnosed at age 36 and initially treated with metformin and sulfonylureas. Due to inadequate glycemic control, insulin was introduced within 1 year after diagnosis. Treatment with insulin and sulfonylureas was complicated by frequent episodes of hypoglycemia, with increased frequency of postprandial hypoglycemia episodes after fundoplication surgery. At age 50, she was hospitalized following an episode of profound hypoglycemia (glucose 20-30 mg/dL, SI: 1.1-1.7 mmol/L; laboratory reference: 70-100 mg/dL [3.9-5.6 mmol/L]) in the fasting state, accompanied by confusion. By age 52, due to persistent intermittent hypoglycemia, all diabetes medications were discontinued. Despite no medications for diabetes, she continued to experience both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, with hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) 6.1%.

Family history was notable for early onset diabetes in her mother (diagnosed age 26), father (age of onset unclear), and both a maternal aunt and uncle. The patient's medications included glucagon nasal spray as needed, midodrine, losartan, doxepin, omeprazole, and omalizumab. Physical examination was notable for mild acanthosis nigricans on the neck and body mass index 34.2 kg/m 2 .

## Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory evaluation at 8 Am after a fast of 12-hours revealed glucose 70 mg/dL (SI: 3.9 mmol/L), insulin 4.2 µU/mL (SI: 29.2 pmol/L; lab reference: 2.6-24.9 µU/mL [18.1-173.1 pmol/L]), C-peptide 0.5 ng/mL (SI: 0.17 nmol/L; reference: 1.1-4.4 ng/mL [0.36-1.46 nmol/L]), proinsulin 1.7 pmol/L (reference: 3.6-22 pmol/L), beta-hydroxybutyrate 0.2 mmol/L (reference: <0.3 mmol/L), cortisol 9.2 µg/dL (SI: 253.8 nmol/L; reference: 6-18.3 µg/dL [165.5-504.8 nmol/L]), and normal insulin-like growth factor 2/insulin-like growth factor 1 molar ratio of 8 (reference: <10). HbA1c was 7.9%. Insulin autoantibody titer was low-positive at 0.08 nmol/L (reference: <0.02 nmol/L), consistent with prior insulin use.

A prolonged inpatient supervised fasting study was terminated at 32 hours when plasma glucose dropped to 38 mg/dL with appropriately suppressed insulin of 0.7 µU/mL (SI: 4.9 pmol/L; reference for hypoglycemia during prolonged fasting <3 µU/mL [SI: <20.8 pmol/L]) and C-peptide 0.2 ng/mL (SI: 0.066 nmol/L; reference <0.6 ng/mL [SI: 0.2 nmol/L]) and appropriately increased beta-hydroxybutyrate 4.3 mmol/L (>2.7 mmol/L), which indicate appropriate suppression of endogenous insulin secretion in the fasting state.

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) to assess glycemic patterns demonstrated marked glycemic variability with sensor glucose values ranging from 39 to 401 mg/dL (SI: 2.2-22.2 mmol/L), mean 214 mg/dL [SI: 11.9 mmol/L]; and coefficient of variation 44.4% ( Fig. 2 ). Time-in-range (70-180 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-10 mmol/L]) was 38%, with 61% above 180 mg/dL [SI: 10 mmol/L] and 1% below 70 mg/dL [SI: 3.9 mmol/L]. Low sensor glucose values predominantly occurred during or after afternoon meals and exercise and during nocturnal hours.

Genetic testing (targeted monogenic diabetes panel, Blueprint Genetics Inc.) identified a heterozygous ABCC8 c.4055G>A (p.Arg1352His) likely pathogenic variant, consistent with MODY, type 12 (MODY12).

Our overall assessment pointed to the heterozygous ABCC8 p.Arg1352His variant as the primary driver of her paradoxical glycemic patterns, leading to both hyperglycemia (likely due to impaired insulin secretion) and hypoglycemia (likely due to inappropriate regulation of insulin release in the nonfasting state). This underlying genetic predisposition was likely exacerbated by both obesity and fundoplication-induced altered regulation of gastric emptying and nutrient delivery to the absorptive intestine, contributing to increased postprandial insulin secretion, and further increases in glycemic variability.

## Treatment

Initial management focused on mitigating hypoglycemia through dietary and pharmacologic interventions. The patient was advised to consume frequent small meals emphasizing complex carbohydrates and protein, to avoid simple carbohydrates, and to carry glucose for rapid correction of hypoglycemia. The α-glucosidase inhibitors, such as acarbose or miglitol, have been successfully used for postprandial hypoglycemia due to their ability to slow carbohydrate digestion, reducing both postprandial glycemia and insulin levels. Acarbose was trialed but discontinued due to gastrointestinal intolerance; miglitol 25 mg 3 times daily modestly reduced postprandial glucose excursions despite mild gastrointestinal side effects.

Given persistent hypoglycemia, low-dose diazoxide (25 mg at bedtime) was initiated to suppress insulin secretion and increase hepatic glucose production. Cornstarch, a highly complex carbohydrate with slow absorption, was recommended before exercise and at bedtime. With these measures, HbA1c rose to 7.9%, leading to discontinuation of diazoxide and introduction of basal insulin (insulin glargine, initially 10 units nightly) and sitagliptin 50 mg daily to attenuate postprandial hyperglycemia.

Over the next 5 months, insulin glargine was titrated to 14 units nightly, and a conservative sliding-scale insulin regimen was implemented with meals. The patient continued dietary modifications, use of CGM, and cornstarch use before exercise.

## Outcome and follow-up

At her 6-month follow-up visit, the patient reported tolerance of miglitol and glargine and a reduction in episodes of symptomatic hypoglycemia. HbA1c decreased modestly from 7.9 to 7.6%. CGM demonstrated continued hyperglycemia (mean sensor glucose 201 mg/dL [SI: 11.2 mmol/L]) with slightly improved glycemic variability (coefficient of variation 40.2%), and time-in-range 45%.

## Discussion

This case report underscores the intricate and sometimes paradoxical nature of β-cell dysfunction resulting from a single heterozygous ABCC8 p.Arg1352His variant. This specific genetic variant is recognized for its association with both hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and monogenic diabetes, exemplifying the dual glycemic phenotype characteristic of certain KATP channelopathies [ 6 ]. The clinical trajectory of our patient, progressing from a diagnosis of T2D requiring insulin to a subsequent overlay of severe hypoglycemia, aligns with the “biphasic” pattern increasingly reported for dominantly inherited KATP channel variants [ 11 ].

The likely mechanism behind this dual phenotype involves the complex impact of the p.Arg1352His variant on function of the KATP channel. While the initial hyperglycemic presentation might suggest an early impairment of glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, the later development of hypoglycemia indicates a potential shift toward inappropriate insulin release, possibly due to altered channel sensitivity to ATP and MgADP [ 12 ]. Although p.Arg1352His has not been individually characterized, heterozygous ABCC8 variants in the nucleotide-binding domain region show reduced KATP channel responses to MgADP and diazoxide in vitro [ 13 , 14 ], and isolated human KATP-deficient islets demonstrate insulin secretion that is uncoupled from glucose yet robustly stimulated by amino acids [ 15 ]. This may explain our patient's phenotype, in which insulin was appropriately suppressed during fasting while hypoglycemia predominated postprandially, suggesting nutrient-stimulated rather than fasting-driven dysregulation. Dedicated functional studies of this specific variant are needed to fully characterize its pathophysiology.

Genetic testing played a pivotal role in elucidating the etiology of our patient's complex glycemic profile. The identification of the ABCC8 variant provided a definitive diagnosis that guided subsequent management. While sulfonylureas are often used in the management of diabetes related to KATP channelopathies, in our patient they were associated with exacerbation of hypoglycemia. Consistent with the predominantly postprandial rather than fasting pattern of hypoglycemia in our patient, treatment was redirected toward postprandial-focused strategies, including dietary modification and miglitol to blunt postprandial insulin secretion; low-dose diazoxide, a KATP channel opener, proved effective in mitigating spontaneous hypoglycemia. The diazoxide response suggests partial preservation of channel function, which is more commonly observed in dominant rather than recessive ABCC8 variants [ 14 ].

Factors beyond the ABCC8 variant may have contributed to the patient's hypoglycemia, particularly that occurring in the postprandial state. Postprandial hypoglycemia can develop after Nissen fundoplication or other upper gastrointestinal surgical procedures, potentially linked to dysregulated gastric emptying, increased incretin secretion, and exaggerated insulin response [ 16 ]. Additionally, the presence of POTS, which can be associated with dysregulation of postprandial glucose metabolism and increases in prandial glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide levels, as well as additional symptoms mimicking hypoglycemia, might have played a role in her clinical presentation [ 17 ]. The interplay between these factors, obesity, and the underlying ABCC8 variant may have contributed to the complex clinical picture. While autoimmune hypoglycemia can also present with postprandial hypoglycemia, the low-titer antibody was more likely reflective of prior insulin therapy. Further metabolic studies would be required to test these possibilities in a research environment.

The successful management of this patient required a tailored therapeutic approach, combining careful insulin titration for hyperglycemia, dietary interventions to stabilize blood glucose, and considering the use of diazoxide to counteract hypoglycemia when needed. This case underscores the critical need for clinicians to maintain vigilance for monogenic diabetes in patients presenting with atypical clinical features, particularly when there is a progression to unexplained hypoglycemia. Monogenic diabetes, including MODY subtypes caused by ABCC8 -pathogenic variants, may account for 1-5% of all diabetes cases but is often underdiagnosed, particularly in adults [ 18 ]. Therefore, genetic testing should be considered for all individuals with atypical diabetes presentations, even in older adults, in order to guide management. Moreover, individuals with underlying disorders of insulin secretion may be at greater risk for hypoglycemia resulting from upper gastrointestinal surgery, including both bariatric surgery and fundoplication, requiring further vigilance for this complication.

In summary, this case highlights the clinical significance of genetic testing in unraveling complex glycemic disorders. It further illustrates the dual glycemic phenotype associated with the heterozygous ABCC8 p.Arg1352His variant, with exacerbation of hypoglycemia after upper gastrointestinal surgery, and emphasizes the importance of individualized dietary management and diverse therapeutic strategies to achieve glycemic stability.
